# The Birds Will Be Here in the Morning

### 1

On the morning the trouble found her, Vanessa Bell stopped to watch a man retrieve his hat from a tree.

The hat was yellow. The tree belonged to the city. The man, who had probably expected neither of these facts to become important before breakfast, stood beneath it holding a paper cup.

“Jump,” said the woman beside him.

“I have coffee.”

“I’ll hold it.”

“You’ll drink it.”

“I might.”

Vanessa could have reached the hat. She was tall, and the branch was low. But a small brown bird had landed on the brim and was inspecting a loose thread.

She waited.

So did the man.

The bird pulled experimentally.

“Absolutely not,” he told it.

When it flew away with the thread, Vanessa stepped forward and lifted down the hat.

“Thank you,” he said.

“There’s a repair shop on Alder Street.”

“For one thread?”

“For when it comes back.”

He looked up.

She left him considering the possibility.

The tram arrived smelling of warm brakes and somebody’s orange. Vanessa found a place by the window. Below the elevated tracks, the city opened its shutters: bakeries, bicycle stalls, a shop that sold objects for which nobody had yet identified a use. A woman on a balcony was shaking a rug with the ferocity of a personal feud.

Vanessa loved this hour. People were present without yet demanding much of one another.

At her stop she bought coffee from Ibrahim, who had been announcing his retirement for seven years.

“You look pleased,” he said.

“A bird stole part of a hat.”

“That’ll happen.”

“Does it?”

“Eventually everything happens. Sugar?”

“No.”

“I ask because people change.”

“Not that quickly.”

She carried the cup through the courtyard of Bell Measurement, past the plum tree her employees had planted without consulting her. Its roots would eventually interfere with the water main. She had obtained a survey, hired someone to divert the pipe, and refrained from making a speech about it.

Inside, she found her operations manager standing over a dismantled instrument.

“Before you say anything,” Dev said, “it was already broken.”

“You’ve removed the part that tells us how.”

“Yes. That was an exciting discovery.”

Vanessa put down her coffee.

She had founded the company twelve years earlier with one useful invention, an unhelpful patent lawyer, and enough savings to survive eight months. Now thirty-seven people worked there. Their instruments measured tiny discrepancies in the machinery that connected inhabited worlds.

Vanessa had never visited most of those worlds. Nevertheless, she had strong opinions about their maintenance practices.

At thirty-six, she had a home she liked, knees she looked after, work she could defend, and friends who knew when to leave her alone. She had worked hard for these things. Some mornings she still stood in her kitchen and felt astonished that nobody could order her to become someone else.

Dev moved aside.

On the bench lay a transit regulator from a decommissioned vessel.

Someone had engraved a line inside its housing.

Vanessa leaned closer.

*We were here when the doors were made.*

She blinked.

The engraving looked different.

Now it was a series of shallow, irregular scratches.

“What language is this?”

“What language is what?”

She turned the housing toward him.

“That,” she said.

Dev put on his glasses.

“Tool marks.”

“No.”

He looked at her.

Vanessa looked back at the housing.

The sentence had returned.

Underneath it, in smaller letters:

*Please stop opening them.*

***

At eleven, two visitors asked to see her.

One was a broad woman with silver hair and a walking stick. The other wore a suit whose quiet elegance suggested either considerable money or a profession in which appearing to have considerable money was useful.

The woman introduced herself as Doctor Sabine Okafor.

The man said, “Director Pell.”

“Director of what?”

“The Continuity Office.”

“That doesn’t tell me.”

“No,” said Doctor Okafor. “It doesn’t.”

Vanessa liked her slightly.

They sat in her office. Through the window, she could see Dev trying to discourage a delivery vehicle from entering the courtyard.

Pell laid a photograph on her desk.

It showed a white transit gate suspended over a planet. Beneath it, a storm spiraled across the ocean.

“This is Merrow,” he said. “Eleven days ago.”

A second photograph showed the same planet.

The ocean was smooth.

“Where’s the storm?”

“Gone.”

“Storms do that.”

“The atmosphere is gone too.”

Vanessa didn’t touch the photograph.

Doctor Okafor watched her face.

“Three hundred and twelve million people,” she said. “We believe it happened in less than a minute.”

Vanessa looked out at the courtyard.

Dev had put his hand on the delivery vehicle’s hood. He was speaking to its camera with great patience.

“Why are you showing me this?”

Pell glanced at the regulator she had brought upstairs.

“Because you can read the inscription.”

She felt a sharp, unpleasant certainty.

“You knew it was there.”

“Yes.”

“And sent it here.”

“Yes.”

“You tested me without asking.”

“We had limited—”

“No. You can explain the urgency without pretending it changes what you did.”

Doctor Okafor lowered her eyes.

Pell sat back.

After a moment he said, “You’re right.”

Vanessa waited. An apology was not an explanation.

“There have been seventeen incidents,” he said. “Over two hundred and forty years. Different kinds. Most looked natural. Some were attributed to conflict or engineering failure.”

“Merrow wasn’t.”

“No.”

“How does an engraving connect them?”

“We think somebody has been trying to warn us.”

“Somebody?”

Doctor Okafor opened a small case.

Inside lay a ring made of dark wood.

Vanessa recognized it before she understood why.

Her grandmother had worn one just like it.

***

Her grandmother had called the family gift *hearing through the walls*.

Not literal walls. Vanessa had understood that even at eight.

“Everything has more rooms than you think,” Grandma Bell used to say.

She could find things. Keys, lost animals, once a boy who had climbed into an abandoned refrigeration unit. She had a habit of pausing in doorways as though waiting for somebody to finish speaking.

Vanessa’s mother called it intuition.

Her uncle called it nonsense.

Vanessa had stopped thinking about it after her grandmother died. There had been studies to finish and bills to pay. The world offered plenty of measurable difficulties.

Doctor Okafor said, “Your grandmother worked with us.”

“No, she didn’t.”

“Her name was Irene Bell.”

“She made furniture.”

“She did both.”

Vanessa looked at the ring.

She remembered her grandmother sanding a chair in the garden, a ribbon of wood curling from the plane. She remembered the ring tapping against a glass.

“Why didn’t she tell me?”

“I don’t know.”

It was the first answer that hadn’t seemed prepared.

Pell leaned forward.

“We need you to come with us.”

Vanessa felt the sentence settle into the room.

She looked at the photograph of Merrow again.

“Where?”

“An installation beyond the inhabited routes.”

“For how long?”

“We don’t know.”

“What happened to the last person you took?”

Neither answered quickly enough.

### 2

She didn’t leave that day.

Pell found this difficult.

Vanessa found his difficulty uninteresting.

She asked for documents. She asked to speak privately with the surviving member of the previous expedition. She called her lawyer, whose response was to ask whether the government had finally noticed the tax treatment of interstellar freight.

Then she called her friend Celia.

“Can you come over?”

“Dinner?”

“Yes.”

“Good dinner or something-has-happened dinner?”

“Both, if I manage it.”

Celia arrived carrying bread and a bottle she had selected entirely because its label showed a disapproving goose.

They had known each other twenty years.

Celia taught music to children whose enthusiasm frequently exceeded their concern for timing. She had a small apartment, a large laugh, and a talent for making Vanessa admit what she was actually asking.

They cooked.

Vanessa explained.

Celia stopped chopping.

“Your grandmother?”

“Apparently.”

“She once told me my earrings were arguing.”

“Were they?”

“They were different metals. I thought it was a joke.”

For a while the pan supplied the conversation.

Finally Celia said, “Do they need you, or do they need somebody like you?”

“There are others. Most can’t read what I can read. One is eighty-nine. One vanished with the previous expedition.”

“And you?”

“I’m the one they have available.”

Celia resumed chopping, slowly.

“Do you want to go?”

Vanessa considered saying no. It would have been true, but incomplete.

“I want someone to be able to do something.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“I know.”

After dinner they sat on the floor because Vanessa’s sofa had been claimed by Celia’s elderly dog.

Outside, a tram moved through the dusk. Its windows were bright, each containing a small portion of somebody’s evening.

Vanessa thought about the photograph with no atmosphere.

“You don’t have to earn the right to stay alive,” Celia said.

“I’m aware.”

“I’m saying it anyway.”

Vanessa rested her head against the sofa.

“I’m frightened.”

Celia reached for her hand.

“Good. Bring that with you.”

***

The expedition survivor was called Agnes.

She spoke from a rehabilitation ward, where an orange tree stood outside her window.

She had lost her left hand and much of her hearing.

“The place isn’t empty,” she told Vanessa.

“What place?”

“The space between destinations.”

Agnes closed her eyes briefly.

“When we travel, we think we skip the distance. We don’t. We pass through somewhere.”

“What lives there?”

“I don’t know what to call them.”

“Are they attacking us?”

“Something is.”

“What happened to your expedition?”

Agnes looked at her remaining hand.

“I heard my husband. He’s been dead eleven years.”

Vanessa waited.

“I knew it wasn’t him. I followed anyway.”

“Why?”

“He sounded cold.”

***

She left four days later.

Dev took over the company. Her lawyer held emergency authority. Celia received the spare key, instructions for the plants, and a message Vanessa had recorded and hoped nobody would ever play.

On the final morning, Vanessa exercised, showered, and ate breakfast sitting down.

She packed practical clothes, two novels, and the little objects that made an unfamiliar room feel less anonymous. Her private life had always included choices she kept to herself; those required no explanation now.

The departure terminal contained a gift shop.

She bought a packet of salted nuts.

Pell found her studying postcards.

“We’re ready.”

“Then you can wait while I pay.”

He looked at the postcard in her hand.

It showed her city at sunset.

“For somebody?”

“For me.”

### 3

Their vessel was called *Useful Weather*.

Its pilot, Roshan Dey, met Vanessa at the ramp wearing slippers and carrying a bowl.

“Are you the person who sees things?”

“Apparently.”

“Good. The navigation display’s been lying about the bathroom.”

He was sixty, round-faced, and entirely unwilling to explain whether he was joking.

Doctor Okafor had brought six cases of equipment.

Pell had brought weapons.

Their fourth companion was Lydia Chen, a medic who asked Vanessa about allergies before introducing herself.

By the time they left orbit, Vanessa knew where the lifeboats were, where the spare coffee lived, and that Roshan sang while navigating.

He sang badly.

“That’s the point,” he said when she asked.

“What point?”

“If I sing well, people request songs.”

***

They first stopped at a world called Apricity, where almost every building had been constructed on water.

Their contact lived above a public bath.

His name was Tomás Arce. He wore a pink dressing gown and kept bees on his balcony.

“Irene’s granddaughter,” he said.

Vanessa was becoming tired of being introduced to strangers through a dead woman.

“My name is Vanessa.”

“Yes. Forgive me.”

He offered her tea.

She accepted.

The others went downstairs to inspect an archive. Vanessa remained with Tomás, who had known her grandmother for forty years.

“She was afraid you’d be recruited,” he said.

“So she said nothing?”

“She thought she had time.”

Vanessa held the warm cup.

That was a sentence people used after nearly every kind of loss.

Tomás took a sheet of paper from a drawer.

It contained a drawing: several circles joined by fine lines. Along the edges were symbols Vanessa could read.

*Door.*

*Guest.*

*Return.*

At the center:

*Permission.*

“Who wrote this?”

“Irene.”

“What does it mean?”

“We built the transit network using structures we found. We understood enough to make them operate.”

“But not enough to know what they were.”

Tomás nodded.

From below came laughter and the slap of someone jumping into water.

“What are they?”

“I think they’re part of a living thing.”

Vanessa looked at the drawing again.

“Are we hurting it?”

“We have been passing ships through it for six hundred years.”

“That doesn’t answer me.”

“No,” he said. “It doesn’t.”

***

That evening Lydia persuaded them to eat outside.

Apricity’s lanterns hung low over the water. Fish gathered beneath each light. On a distant terrace, musicians were arguing over whether to begin again.

Doctor Okafor laughed for the first time Vanessa had heard.

Roshan ordered something that arrived on fire.

Pell didn’t relax, exactly, but he removed his jacket.

Lydia told a story about stitching a diplomat’s ear back on after a ceremonial animal had objected to him.

For an hour Vanessa could almost believe she was traveling.

She found herself looking at Lydia’s hands: capable, lightly scarred, careful with the small bones of her dinner.

Lydia noticed.

“Something on my sleeve?”

“No.”

“Oh.”

She smiled, and returned to her plate.

Vanessa liked that she let the moment remain small.

Later, Tomás came running down the terrace.

His dressing gown was gone. He carried a weatherproof bag.

“We need to leave.”

Pell stood.

“Why?”

“The bath downstairs has another floor.”

“It has always had—”

“No,” Tomás said. “Underneath.”

The water beyond the terrace rose.

It rose without waves.

Lanterns tilted. Fish fell out of their circles of light.

Under the lifted sheet of water, Vanessa saw a pale surface covered in openings.

Something pressed against them from the other side.

### 4

They ran through streets draining upward.

Roshan called the ship.

Lydia stopped for a child whose shoe had become trapped beneath a fallen bench.

Pell shouted at her to keep moving.

She ignored him.

Vanessa and Tomás lifted the bench together. Lydia freed the child and handed her to a man running the opposite way.

“The high platforms,” Tomás told him. “Get off the water.”

A transit arch stood at the end of the street.

It was a local connector, meant to take passengers to the inland ridge.

A crowd had formed beneath it.

The arch flickered.

Vanessa saw words traveling around its rim.

*Open.*

*Open.*

*Open.*

They were being written over older words.

She could still make those out.

*Occupied.*

*Wait.*

She reached the control panel.

Pell caught her arm.

“The ship’s coming.”

“This gate needs to close.”

“There are people waiting.”

“There’s something waiting on the other side.”

He released her.

The panel displayed a familiar maintenance interface. For the first time since leaving home, Vanessa stood before a problem that had buttons.

She isolated the gate’s local power.

The arch remained lit.

She disconnected its reserve.

Still lit.

Beneath the glass, a line appeared.

*You have no authority.*

Vanessa felt, absurdly, the old anger of being eighteen and told she had made a competent point in an unacceptable tone.

She put her palm against the panel.

“Then who does?”

The glass grew warm.

An impression entered her mind—not a voice, not an image. The sense of standing outside somebody’s house at night, ringing and ringing while the occupants hid.

She withdrew her hand.

The crowd surged.

“Move back,” she said.

Nobody did.

She climbed onto the control housing.

“This gate is unsafe. Go to the ridge on foot.”

A woman shouted that her father couldn’t walk.

Vanessa looked toward him: an elderly man sitting on a wheeled suitcase.

“Four of you,” she said, pointing. “Carry him. Take turns.”

Someone protested.

Lydia was already helping.

Vanessa touched the panel again.

This time she pictured the gate closing: hinges, weight, the ordinary finality of a door shutting.

Something inside her answered.

The arch went dark.

Pain traveled up her arm.

Her fingers folded involuntarily.

Behind her, the crowd began to move.

***

They took thirty-eight people aboard a ship designed for twelve.

Tomás stayed.

He had reached the ramp when someone called his name.

A young woman stood on a balcony across the canal, holding a baby.

Tomás looked at Vanessa.

“My neighbor,” he said.

“Bring them.”

He ran back.

The lifted water struck the terrace.

Roshan waited until the ship’s stabilizers began to fail.

Vanessa watched the balcony disappear behind a white wall.

Then Apricity dropped away beneath them.

In the crowded cabin, a little boy asked when they would return.

Nobody answered.

***

Her arm recovered over three days.

She could not remember the smell of coffee.

She knew what coffee was. She knew whether she liked it. But when Roshan handed her a cup, it smelled only hot.

Doctor Okafor examined her.

“We saw something similar with Agnes.”

“Similar?”

“She lost her memory of her husband’s voice.”

Vanessa put down the cup.

“You knew there was a cost.”

“We knew there might be.”

“And you didn’t tell me.”

“I should have.”

Vanessa waited for more.

Doctor Okafor had none.

“Leave,” Vanessa said.

The doctor left.

That night Lydia sat outside her door.

She didn’t ask to come in.

Eventually Vanessa opened it.

“I can’t smell coffee.”

Lydia nodded.

“I heard.”

“It’s such a stupid thing.”

“No.”

Vanessa leaned against the frame.

“I don’t know what else I’ll lose.”

Lydia stayed with her until she could sleep.

### 5

They reached the installation beyond the inhabited routes.

It had once been a monastery.

The people who built it had believed a god lived in the space between stars. Its later occupants had replaced the chapel with laboratories while preserving the stained glass, largely because nobody had found a safe way to remove it.

An old transit structure filled the courtyard.

Unlike modern gates, it had no machinery attached.

It looked like two enormous hands holding a circle.

Doctor Okafor brought Irene Bell’s ring.

Vanessa refused it.

“Tell me everything first.”

Pell began to speak.

She looked at him.

“Everything.”

***

The Continuity Office had discovered the pattern seventy years earlier.

A mathematician had noticed that certain disasters improved the transit network’s efficiency.

Not immediately. Over time.

A destroyed world left a route unused. A depopulated region permitted a new connection. A civil war forced ships through a particular sequence of gates.

The network was being rearranged.

Doctor Okafor showed Vanessa a model.

In its oldest form, the routes resembled branches.

In its present form, they curved inward.

“A cage,” Vanessa said.

“A funnel,” said Pell.

Toward what, they didn’t know.

Irene and Tomás had believed the gates were communicating distress.

Pell’s predecessors had believed the gates were the threat.

“They developed a way to collapse the original junction,” Doctor Okafor said. “The point from which the entire network grew.”

“And?”

“It would end interstellar transit.”

“For how long?”

Pell said, “Permanently.”

Vanessa looked at the model.

Hundreds of worlds depended on imported food, medical supplies, replacement components. Some would survive. Others would not.

“How many?”

“Billions,” said Doctor Okafor.

Pell’s face tightened.

“We don’t know that.”

“We know enough.”

Vanessa looked from one to the other.

“And you brought me to operate it.”

“Yes,” Pell said.

“The weapon can reach the junction,” Doctor Okafor added. “It needs somebody who can establish contact.”

Vanessa sat down.

Beyond the window, a windless tree stirred.

“What did my grandmother want to do?”

Doctor Okafor looked at the ring.

“Ask what was wrong.”

***

Vanessa went into the courtyard alone.

For a long time she stood under the stone hands.

She thought about Tomás going back.

She thought about Merrow.

Then she thought about her kitchen, with its chipped green bowl and the spoon that never fit in the drawer.

She took the ring from her pocket.

The wood was warm.

When she put it on, the courtyard remained exactly as it was.

Then she noticed the tree.

Its shadow pointed toward her.

So did every other shadow.

She placed her hand on the gate.

Someone, somewhere, had been waiting so long that waiting had become almost all they were.

Vanessa wept before she understood that the grief wasn’t hers.

A shape moved beyond the circle.

Vastness was the first thing she felt.

Then injury.

Then the exhausting effort of holding something shut.

She said, “I don’t know how to help you.”

The answer arrived as a memory she had never lived.

A bright chamber.

Many voices.

Doors opening between places that had never been separate.

Visitors arriving.

Visitors welcomed.

Then one visitor who remained after everyone else had gone.

Hungry.

Patient.

***

She woke on the courtyard stones.

Doctor Okafor knelt beside her.

“What happened?”

Vanessa tried to answer.

Instead she asked, “How did I meet Dev?”

The doctor stared.

“I know who he is,” Vanessa said. “I know he works for me.”

She closed her eyes.

Their first interview had been one of her favorite stories. Dev had arrived at the wrong building, argued that its signage was inadequate, then demonstrated that her prototype contained a fault she had missed.

She could remember telling that story.

She could no longer remember the day.

### 6

They called the living structure the Host.

The thing it had trapped, they called the Guest.

Vanessa disliked both names. They made captivity sound like a social inconvenience.

But they needed words.

The Host had built passages through itself, allowing people to cross distances. It had welcomed them for reasons Vanessa could feel but not translate. Perhaps company. Perhaps curiosity. Perhaps the pleasure of seeing what small, brief creatures would do.

The Guest had learned to feed on the departures.

Not bodies.

The lives connected to bodies: recognition, attachment, the dense accumulation of having been somewhere and mattered to someone.

Modern transit scans had made those connections easier to find.

“It can follow a person home,” Vanessa said.

Doctor Okafor stopped writing.

“Yes.”

The missing atmospheres, the accidents, the wars: methods of breaking entire populations loose at once.

“And the Host has been containing it?”

“While we kept making more doors.”

Pell stared at the model.

“For centuries.”

***

They worked for six days.

Vanessa ate when Lydia reminded her. She walked the monastery grounds each morning. She insisted they all sleep, then nearly violated her own rule.

Roshan took her notebook.

“You can have it back after breakfast.”

“That’s patronizing.”

“Yes. Bed.”

She went, furious.

In the morning she apologized.

He returned the notebook without making anything of it.

Their plan grew from something Vanessa knew.

A transit regulator did not force a route to exist. It corrected a discrepancy between what the gate expected and what the machinery supplied.

The modern network had been forcing the Host to maintain thousands of connections simultaneously.

They could shut those connections down in an orderly sequence.

The Host might then recover enough strength to expel the Guest.

“Might,” Pell said.

“Yes.”

“The collapse device will work.”

“And kill billions.”

“It will save the rest.”

Vanessa looked at the map.

She could not prove her plan.

She would have distrusted anyone who claimed they could.

***

On the seventh day, Roshan found the second ship.

It waited on the far side of the monastery’s moon.

Pell had called it before they arrived.

Its crew carried the collapse device.

Doctor Okafor confronted him in the old chapel.

“You agreed to give us time.”

“I have.”

“You’re going ahead.”

“If this fails.”

“Who decides it has failed?”

“I do.”

Vanessa stood in the doorway.

Pell looked tired enough to be ill.

“Your plan requires every major transit authority to cooperate,” he told her. “Some won’t answer. Some think we’re committing sabotage. One has requested my arrest.”

“How long have you known about the device?”

“Thirty years.”

“And spent thirty years hoping someone else would find a better answer.”

His eyes lifted.

“Yes,” he said.

The admission made it harder to hate him.

It did not make her trust him.

***

That evening Celia called.

The connection was poor. Her face arrived in fragments.

“Your plants are conspiring,” she said.

Vanessa smiled.

“Separate them.”

“I tried. They prefer each other.”

Behind Celia stood Vanessa’s kitchen.

The green bowl was on the counter.

For a moment she wanted to reach into the screen.

“Are you all right?” Celia asked.

“No.”

Celia’s expression changed.

Vanessa told her what she could.

The losses. The plan. Pell.

When she finished, Celia was quiet.

“Come home,” she said.

“I can’t yet.”

“You can. You might decide not to.”

Vanessa looked down.

Celia had never allowed her to hide a choice inside a statement of impossibility.

“You’re right.”

“Of course I am. It’s exhausting.”

Vanessa laughed, then cried so abruptly that she couldn’t disguise it.

Celia waited.

Before the connection ended, Vanessa said, “Tell me how we met.”

Celia’s face went still.

Then she began.

### 7

The transit authorities answered.

Not all of them. Enough.

Some agreed because Doctor Okafor’s evidence convinced them. Some because Merrow had frightened them. One agreed only after Roshan threatened to broadcast a recording of its director confusing a maintenance alarm with a musical interlude.

They scheduled the shutdown for a period of minimum traffic.

Hospital worlds received supplies. Ships were recalled. Emergency corridors remained open as long as possible.

It was imperfect.

Every hour brought another exception.

Vanessa signed decisions she wanted more time to consider.

On the last night, Lydia came to her room.

“Company?”

“Yes.”

They sat beside the window.

Stars were scattered over the black courtyard.

Vanessa said, “I think I would abandon this whole thing if someone I loved were in danger.”

Lydia didn’t answer immediately.

“I think about that too.”

“What do you decide?”

“I usually don’t have to decide between a person and everyone. Usually I have to decide who gets my hands first.”

“And if you did?”

“I don’t know.”

Vanessa rested her forehead against the glass.

“I hate that answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

After a while Lydia took her hand.

Later they kissed.

Neither made a promise.

***

At the appointed hour, the first gates closed.

On the table in the former chapel, white routes vanished one by one.

Vanessa stood in the courtyard wearing her grandmother’s ring.

Through it she could feel the Host gathering itself.

The effort resembled drawing breath after crying.

The second wave closed.

Then the third.

An alarm sounded inside.

Pell came to the courtyard.

“The home cluster isn’t responding.”

Vanessa turned.

“My world?”

“All seven.”

She ran inside.

On the communications display, Celia stood in the departure terminal.

People crowded behind her.

“I don’t know why they called us here,” she said.

“Get outside.”

“The doors won’t—”

The picture shifted.

For a moment Celia’s face remained.

Then someone else looked through it.

The expression was attentive.

Curious.

A line appeared at the bottom of the screen.

*Keep this door.*

Vanessa felt the Host recoil.

The Guest had found an opening that mattered to her.

***

Pell ordered the collapse ship to prepare.

Doctor Okafor shouted at him.

Roshan tried to reach the home cluster.

Vanessa watched Celia press her palm to the camera.

The terminal lights went out behind her.

Only her face remained lit.

“Vanessa?”

“I’m here.”

“Something’s happening.”

“I know.”

Lydia stood beside Vanessa.

No advice. No demand.

Just presence.

The network needed the final seven gates shut.

The home cluster’s authorities could no longer operate them.

Vanessa could.

She knew it with the same certainty that had let her close the arch on Apricity.

She could close them with the terminal occupied.

She could leave Celia inside.

Her hand hovered over the table.

Pell said, “We need you.”

Celia was trying not to cry.

Vanessa thought about a million strangers.

Then she thought about Celia arriving with bread.

“I can’t,” she said.

Pell looked at her.

She made herself meet his eyes.

“I won’t.”

For a moment nobody moved.

Then he called the collapse ship.

“Proceed.”

### 8

Doctor Okafor struck him.

It was clumsy and shocking.

Pell stumbled against the table.

She took his transmitter.

“Do not proceed.”

The ship’s commander asked for authentication.

Pell reached for her.

Lydia stepped between them.

Vanessa was already moving.

She ran through the chapel into the courtyard.

The Host was waiting.

She put both hands on the gate.

“Can you bring them through?”

The answer was immediate.

No.

Not through the closed routes.

Not without releasing what it held.

Vanessa pictured the terminal.

Celia. The people behind her. A child asleep on somebody’s shoulder.

“Then take me there.”

The circle filled with darkness.

She stepped into it.

***

The terminal smelled of electrical insulation.

Vanessa fell to her knees.

She heard someone say her name.

Celia reached her first.

For several seconds they held one another so tightly it hurt.

Then Vanessa stood.

The windows looked out on nothing.

No city. No tracks.

Beyond the glass stretched a pale surface with openings all across it.

The terminal had been pulled halfway through.

People were calling relatives on devices that showed their own faces.

Vanessa climbed onto a luggage bench.

“Turn those off.”

Some did.

Most didn’t.

She raised her voice.

“Whatever speaks to you through them is keeping you here.”

A man near the doors shouted that she didn’t know that.

She didn’t argue.

She showed him the gate opening behind her.

The crowd went quiet.

***

She could hold the opening.

She could not make it reach the monastery.

That route had closed.

The Host offered another destination.

A place it remembered.

Vanessa saw water.

“No,” she whispered.

Apricity.

There were no other available doors.

The city had been injured, not destroyed.

People remained there.

So did pieces of the Guest.

Vanessa understood what she would be carrying toward them.

She looked at Celia.

Then at the crowd.

She had come because she would not abandon her friend.

Now she had brought everyone to another terrible decision.

She wanted a better answer.

There wasn’t one yet.

***

A voice spoke beside her.

“Vanessa.”

Tomás stood under the departures board.

He was wet.

His pink dressing gown clung to his arms.

She stared.

“You’re dead.”

“I expect so.”

Behind him, a young woman held a baby.

Other figures gathered along the wall.

Vanessa remembered Agnes’s husband.

She stepped back.

Tomás nodded.

“Yes. Be careful.”

“How do I know it’s you?”

“You don’t.”

The answer hurt more than reassurance would have.

He looked toward the gate.

“But I know where you’re trying to go.”

“Apricity.”

“The water’s down. People are on the ridge.”

“Will this bring it with us?”

“Yes.”

Celia took Vanessa’s arm.

Tomás said, “Unless someone keeps its attention.”

***

The Guest spoke through the departures board.

*I can give them back.*

Every screen filled with people.

Vanessa’s grandmother.

Tomás.

Strangers with faces that made the crowd cry out.

The Host shuddered.

Vanessa understood something then.

The Guest had learned the shapes of people by consuming what connected them.

It could offer those shapes.

Maybe more than shapes.

She did not know.

It had killed worlds to obtain them.

Her grandmother smiled from a screen.

Vanessa wanted her so badly that she felt physically sick.

“I missed you,” she said.

The smile deepened.

“You know what I’d pay,” Vanessa continued.

The screen flickered.

“So you should know why I won’t pay it with somebody else.”

She turned away.

“Celia. Get them through.”

“What are you doing?”

“Closing the door behind you.”

“You’re coming.”

“I’ll try.”

Celia shook her head.

Vanessa took her face in both hands.

“I mean it. I’ll try. Help me have something to come back to.”

***

The first passengers crossed.

Vanessa held the opening while the Guest pressed against her attention.

It showed her mornings.

Her mother brushing her hair.

Dev arriving for his interview.

A summer afternoon she had forgotten even before the magic began taking things.

She recognized each scene just long enough to feel it go.

Celia moved people through the gate.

Some resisted.

One woman would not leave a screen showing her son.

Celia knelt beside her.

Vanessa could not hear what she said.

Eventually the woman came.

At the far end of the terminal, Tomás and the others stood facing the windows.

The pale surface outside darkened.

Vanessa held on.

She forgot the name of the street where she lived.

She forgot Ibrahim.

She forgot what had happened to the yellow hat.

She knew she was losing something good.

That knowledge remained after the thing itself was gone.

### 9

At the monastery, Pell lowered his hand.

The collapse ship was asking for confirmation.

Doctor Okafor had run to the courtyard.

Lydia followed.

Roshan remained at the table.

He enlarged the model.

The home cluster’s routes had changed.

Six were dark.

One led somewhere unexpected.

Apricity.

He stared.

Then he called its surviving authority.

***

Vanessa could no longer feel her hands.

The last passengers crossed.

Celia came back for her.

“Now.”

“I can’t let go.”

“Then tell me how to help.”

Vanessa tried to remember how they had met.

Nothing.

It terrified her more than the thing outside.

“I don’t know you.”

Celia flinched.

For a moment she looked very young.

Then she said, “You do at the moment.”

The truth of that steadied Vanessa.

The door began to narrow.

Outside, Tomás looked toward her.

The figures beside him raised their hands.

A sensation moved through the terminal.

Not hunger.

Something quieter.

Recognition without possession.

The Host had heard them.

Vanessa felt a new door become possible.

She understood its cost.

A living person would have to remain connected to it.

Not hold it shut.

Keep it open for the Host, while the Guest was drawn somewhere it could no longer reach the network.

The far side contained no inhabited worlds.

No routes.

No mornings.

She looked at Celia.

“I need you to leave.”

“No.”

“Please.”

Celia began to cry.

Vanessa could not remember the years that entitled her to ask.

She asked anyway.

***

Then other hands found the door.

At the monastery, Doctor Okafor wore a wooden ring.

On a hospital world, Agnes pressed her remaining palm against a transit panel.

On Apricity, a woman stood beside an arch with a child holding her skirt.

Across the network, people who had spent their lives noticing things they couldn’t explain answered something they had never been asked before.

The pressure divided.

Vanessa staggered.

She heard Doctor Okafor, very faintly.

“You’re not the only one.”

The Guest struck.

Agnes screamed.

One connection vanished.

Another.

The Host gathered the closed routes into itself.

For an instant Vanessa felt all its doors: the places they had gone, the creatures they had welcomed, the long pleasure of contact.

She offered it the terminal.

The injured city.

The seven worlds.

Everything still living within reach.

Then she let go.

***

Celia dragged her through.

They fell onto wet stone.

People stood all around them under a gray sky.

Someone was sobbing.

Someone was asking for a doctor.

Above Apricity’s abandoned harbor, the transit arch folded inward.

Its circle became a line.

Then nothing.

Vanessa rolled onto her back.

A bird crossed the sky.

She watched it until she could no longer see it.

### 10

Interstellar transit stopped for eleven months.

Some worlds lost people.

The numbers were argued over, revised, weaponized, and mourned.

Supply ships traveled by older, slower means. Communities that had expected to receive help in hours learned what months could demand of them.

Vanessa read every report she could bear.

She did not find a way to make her choice innocent.

Celia was alive.

So were thousands from the terminal.

Others had died while the final route remained open.

Both things stayed true.

***

Pell visited her rehabilitation room once.

He had resigned.

His replacement had initiated three investigations, none of which pleased anyone involved.

He brought no flowers.

“I would have done it,” he said.

“I know.”

“The collapse.”

“I know.”

He stood beside the chair without sitting.

“You risked the entire operation for one person.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“I can understand it,” he said. “I can’t trust it.”

Vanessa nodded.

He sat down then.

They spoke for twenty minutes about Apricity’s food deliveries.

When he left, she did not expect to see him again.

***

Doctor Okafor had lost the memory of her first child’s birth.

Agnes had died.

Three of the other people who answered the Host had died too.

Vanessa asked for their names.

She wrote them in a notebook.

She had become particular about writing things down.

The doctors could explain some of her injuries. They could explain almost nothing about the memories.

Certain facts remained. Certain experiences returned in pieces.

Others were gone.

She knew she had founded a company.

She didn’t remember signing its first lease.

She knew she had loved her grandmother.

The sound of her laugh was missing.

She knew Celia had stayed.

Some days that was enough.

Some days it wasn’t.

***

Lydia visited when her work allowed.

Their first evening together after the hospital, Vanessa burned dinner.

Lydia opened a window.

“That’s reassuring.”

“What is?”

“You’re still bad at something.”

“I used to be good at this.”

“Were you?”

Vanessa considered the evidence.

“I have only my own testimony.”

They ate bread, cheese, and a tomato Lydia found in the refrigerator.

Later Vanessa said, “I don’t want you to stay because of what happened.”

“I’m not.”

“I don’t want to be your patient.”

“You’re not.”

“I’m going to be difficult about this.”

Lydia put down her glass.

“Vanessa, you were difficult before.”

She laughed.

It was the first time the sound had surprised her in weeks.

***

When the Host opened a passage again, it did so beside Apricity’s inland ridge.

No machinery summoned it.

A circle appeared between two trees.

Three words traveled around its edge.

*Are you ready?*

Nobody entered for nine days.

Then a small scientific vessel passed through.

It returned.

Its crew described a journey that took six minutes instead of six seconds.

For two of those minutes, something vast accompanied them.

The passengers had been asked where they wished to go.

The new transit agreements were slow to negotiate.

Vanessa worked on the instruments.

Doctor Okafor worked on the language.

For the first time, shutting a gate was treated as an answer rather than a fault.

### 11

Vanessa came home in autumn.

Dev met her at the courtyard entrance.

The plum tree had grown.

“You’ve moved my office,” she said.

“Only the contents.”

“Where?”

“Somewhere with better light.”

She looked at him.

“I thought you might want it,” he said.

He had kept the company alive.

Better than alive.

There were new employees, new contracts, several decisions she wouldn’t have made.

She listened to him explain.

Then she thanked him.

She worked three days a week at first.

She relearned names.

Sometimes a colleague would begin a familiar story and stop upon seeing her face.

“Finish it,” she would say.

It was difficult to be the person everybody remembered.

It was also difficult to be alive.

She intended to keep doing both.

***

One morning she took the tram.

The air smelled of rain.

She could smell coffee again, though she couldn’t know whether it smelled the same.

At the stop, a man stood beneath a tree holding a yellow hat.

He looked at her.

Then away.

Then back.

“Excuse me.”

She waited.

“You helped me once.”

“Did I?”

“My hat.”

He lifted it.

Along the brim ran a careful line of blue stitching.

“I was told,” he began, then stopped.

Vanessa knew that hesitation now.

People wanted to thank her. People wanted to question her. Sometimes they wanted to tell her she had saved someone. Sometimes that she hadn’t.

The man looked up at the branch.

“There was a bird,” he said.

“What did it do?”

“Stole a thread.”

Vanessa smiled.

“That sounds useful.”

“Apparently.”

He put the hat on.

For a moment they stood together, listening to the leaves.

***

Celia came for dinner that evening.

She brought bread.

Vanessa had cooked something that survived.

They ate on the balcony while the city settled into its night.

Below them, a couple argued gently about directions.

Somewhere a child was practicing a musical instrument with more conviction than accuracy.

Celia told Vanessa a story.

It involved a terrible apartment, a leaking ceiling, and two young women who had each believed the other knew how to repair a pipe.

Vanessa listened.

At the end she said, “Were we happy?”

“Not all the time.”

“But that day?”

Celia thought.

“Eventually.”

Vanessa held out her hand.

Celia took it.

For a while neither spoke.

Then a bird landed on the balcony rail.

Small, brown, watchful.

It turned its head toward the bread.

Celia covered the basket.

“Oh, no.”

The bird waited.

Vanessa broke off a piece.

“Don’t encourage it,” Celia said.

“I’m establishing diplomatic relations.”

“That’s how you got into trouble last time.”

She laughed, but tears had begun to gather in her eyes.

Vanessa saw them.

She moved her chair closer.

The bird took the bread and flew into the tree beyond the balcony.

Inside, dishes waited in the sink. Vanessa had work in the morning. There would be messages she didn’t want to answer and measurements that refused to agree and, eventually, lunch.

She watched the branch where the bird had landed.

It shifted once beneath the leaves.

Celia leaned against her shoulder.

Vanessa stayed until the light was gone.

**The End**
