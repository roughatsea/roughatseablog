# Unintroduced Referents: When Prose Pretends the Reader Already Knows

## Activation

When the user tells you to read or follow this file, read it completely before drafting or revising. Apply it as a companion to the instructions for the particular piece. Do not turn it into a ban on pronouns, definite articles, implication, suspense, or ordinary inference.

This guide addresses a specific failure of orientation: prose refers to something as though the reader can already identify it, even though the writing has not made that thing sufficiently available.

The governing question is:

> Can a careful reader identify exactly what this expression refers to, at this moment, without reconstructing a sentence or context the prose never supplied?

If the answer is no, the writing has incurred referential debt. Repair that debt unless the uncertainty is deliberate, useful, and resolved at the right time.

---

## I. The Problem

A **referent** is whatever an expression asks the reader to identify. It may be:

- A person, place, object, group, or institution
- An idea, category, method, argument, or metaphorical object
- A fact, event, action, condition, or entire preceding proposition
- A set whose members can be counted, contrasted, or ranked
- A stage in a process, location in a document, or point in time
- A source, speaker, study, tradition, or body of evidence

An **unintroduced referent** appears when the prose treats one of these as already identifiable before the reader has a reliable way to identify it.

Consider:

> The catalog is selective.

The grammar is complete, but the discourse is not. Which catalog? When did a catalog become part of the article? The reader may infer that an earlier list of terms constitutes a catalog, but the sentence makes the reader invent a missing step in the article's reasoning.

Several repairs are possible, depending on the intended meaning:

> This article gathers the most useful terms into a selective catalog.

> What follows is a selective catalog rather than an exhaustive reference.

> The terms below form a selective catalog. Its purpose is recognition, not mastery.

The important change is not merely replacing *the* with *a*. The revision performs the missing conceptual action: it creates or identifies the catalog before making claims about it.

### Introduction does not require prior repetition

A referent can be available even when its exact noun has not appeared before:

> We entered an abandoned house. The kitchen smelled of wet plaster.

No earlier sentence mentions a kitchen. The house, however, supplies an immediate and familiar frame in which *the kitchen* is easy to identify. This is often called **bridging** or **associative anaphora**.

Likewise, an identifying phrase can introduce a referent on first mention:

> The decision to cancel the launch arrived after midnight.

The phrase following *decision* tells the reader which decision the sentence means.

The test is therefore not “Has this exact noun appeared already?” The test is “Has the reader been given enough information to identify what the expression means here?”

## II. Legitimate Inference and Useful Withholding

Do not force the prose to introduce every object with bureaucratic ceremony. Some referents are available through shared knowledge, the immediate situation, a strong semantic relationship, or purposeful narrative design.

### Shared and situationally visible referents

Expressions such as *the reader*, *the sun*, *the title above*, or *the door on your left* may be identifiable from the communicative situation. Confirm that the shared context actually exists. A reference to “the chart below” fails when the chart was removed, renamed, or placed elsewhere by a responsive layout.

### Strong bridges

Readers ordinarily accept relationships such as:

- A house → the roof
- A book → the final chapter
- A trial → the jury
- A car → the steering wheel

The relationship must be obvious enough for the intended audience and specific enough to identify one plausible referent. “A company → the architect” is a weaker bridge because a company may have many kinds of architects—or none—and the intended meaning may be literal or metaphorical.

### Immediate forward reference

Occasionally the referent follows the expression that points toward it:

> This was the problem: the detector could identify smoke, but not distinguish a fire from burnt toast.

This **cataphoric** structure works because the answer arrives immediately and the suspense is small. Do not make a reader carry an empty *this*, *it*, or *he* across a long passage unless the delay itself serves the work.

### Intentional withholding in fiction

> He was late again.

As an opening, this may create useful pressure. The reader does not yet know who *he* is or what happened before, but the uncertainty may be deliberate and temporary.

Fiction may withhold identity, motive, history, or meaning. It should still orient the reader to the immediate action. The reader may wonder **who the man is** while understanding **what he is doing in the room**. Confusion is defensible when it creates an intended experience and the work repays it. Accidental confusion merely resembles a missing paragraph.

## III. The Forms Unintroduced Reference Takes

### 1. Definite descriptions that imply prior acquaintance

The definite article often tells the reader to locate a particular, identifiable thing:

> The framework changes how we understand inheritance.

> The tension never disappears.

> The movement rejected those assumptions.

Each sentence may be sound if the framework, tension, or movement is already available. On first mention, these nouns often create false familiarity. Abstract nouns are especially risky because the reader cannot use physical context to identify them.

Inspect first uses of article-specific nouns such as:

- The argument, approach, bargain, catalog, conflict, conversation, distinction, framework, idea, mechanism, method, model, movement, pattern, pipeline, problem, project, question, shift, system, tension, theory, tradition, or work

This is a watchlist, not a blacklist. The words become a problem only when their specificity exceeds what the preceding prose has established.

### 2. Pronouns with no usable antecedent

A pronoun can fail in three ways.

**The antecedent is absent:**

> It changed the entire design.

Nothing available tells the reader what *it* denotes.

**The antecedent is ambiguous:**

> Lena sent Priya the report after she revised it.

Who revised the report?

**The antecedent is stale:**

The noun appeared many paragraphs ago, several other candidates have intervened, and the writer still assumes *it*, *they*, or *this* will retrieve the original subject.

Repair by naming the subject, repeating the accurate noun, restructuring the sentence, or restoring the referent to attention. Repetition is often cheaper than ambiguity. Do not substitute a synonym merely to avoid repeating the one name that keeps the passage coherent.

Not every *it* needs an antecedent. In “It is raining” and “It is difficult to know,” *it* can perform a grammatical function without referring to a particular object. Diagnose meaning before revising.

### 3. Demonstratives that point into fog

The words *this*, *that*, *these*, and *those* can refer to a noun, an event, a claim, a relationship, or an entire passage:

> The samples were contaminated, the temperature log was incomplete, and two controls failed. This changed the conclusion.

What changed the conclusion: the contamination, the missing log, the failed controls, or their combination?

Add a noun when it identifies the intended object:

> Together, these quality-control failures changed the conclusion.

When the intended referent is a proposition, state enough of that proposition to remove doubt:

> Because the study could no longer distinguish a biological effect from laboratory error, its conclusion had to change.

Be especially suspicious of sentence openings such as *This shows*, *This matters*, *This suggests*, *That changed everything*, and *This is why*. They often gesture toward reasoning that the writer should state.

### 4. Relational expressions without their other term

Some words presuppose a relationship or a set:

- *The other approach* presupposes at least two approaches.
- *The former* and *the latter* presuppose a clearly ordered pair.
- *Both explanations* presupposes exactly two accessible explanations.
- *The response* presupposes an action, statement, or event being answered.
- *Its consequence* presupposes a uniquely identifiable cause or owner.
- *The alternative* presupposes a default or first option.

Name the set or relationship before using its shorthand. If the reader must count backward, choose between several possible pairs, or remember which item came first, repeat the names instead of using *former* and *latter*.

### 5. Enumeration without a framed list

> The second reason is maintainability.

This sentence requires a first reason and a recognizable enumeration. A preceding paragraph may contain another reason without having presented it as the first member of a list.

The same problem appears in *next*, *finally*, *another*, *the remaining issue*, and *the third possibility*. Establish the series before indexing it, or remove the index:

> Maintainability creates a separate problem.

Enumeration should reveal structure, not make the reader reverse-engineer one.

### 6. Comparisons and superlatives without a reference class

> This method is faster.

> It was the most important reform.

> The newer design is more reliable.

Faster than what? Most important among which reforms, by what consequence, and over what period? More reliable under which conditions?

The missing referent may be an alternative, a baseline, a population, a time period, or a criterion. State the comparison when it affects the claim. A reference class that exists only in the writer's notes does not exist for the reader.

### 7. Presupposed events and states

Words can smuggle in an earlier event or continuing condition:

- *Again* presupposes a previous occurrence.
- *Still* presupposes an earlier state that continues.
- *Return* presupposes a prior presence or condition.
- *Stop* presupposes that the action had been happening.
- *Continue* presupposes an established action or state.
- *Also* and *too* presuppose a parallel item.
- *Even* presupposes a scale of more and less expected cases.

> The committee rejected the proposal again.

If no earlier rejection has been established, the sentence imports history without supplying it. Introduce the history, remove the trigger, or retain the gap only when discovering the missing history is part of the intended experience.

### 8. Time, place, and document pointers without anchors

> At this stage, the model becomes unstable.

> From here, the argument changes.

> As discussed above, the exception matters.

> Meanwhile, the company changed its policy.

These expressions depend on an accessible process, location, earlier discussion, or simultaneous timeline. Name the stage, section, event, or date when the anchor is not unmistakable.

Document pointers are fragile. Headings move. Paragraphs are cut. Mobile layouts place figures differently. Prefer a descriptive reference—“In the section on bridging”—when *above* or *below* may not survive revision or presentation.

### 9. Sources and collective speakers who materialize mid-argument

> The researchers found no significant difference.

> Critics objected to the definition.

> They later revised the estimate.

Which researchers? Which critics? Who are *they*? A vague collective may conceal several sources with different evidence or positions.

Introduce the source near its first consequential claim. Use a name, study, institution, document, or bounded description that lets the reader identify the speaker and judge the claim. In nonfiction, source identity is part of the evidence, not merely a grammatical antecedent.

### 10. Labels and metaphors introduced as settled objects

Writers often discover a pattern while drafting and then name it as though the name has been present all along:

> The machine rewards safe imitation.

> The bargain could not last.

> The pipeline had become a trap.

These may become powerful governing metaphors, but first the prose must show what *machine*, *bargain*, or *pipeline* encompasses. Define the label through concrete constituents or introduce it as an act of naming:

> Editors, recommendation systems, and risk-averse publishers formed what amounted to a machine for rewarding safe imitation.

After that sentence, *the machine* has somewhere to point.

Do not let a vivid label replace an explanation the reader still needs. Naming a pattern is not the same as demonstrating it.

### 11. References inherited from headings, prompts, notes, or other works

A writer may believe a referent was introduced because it appeared in:

- The assignment or conversation that preceded the draft
- Research notes, an outline, or a private working brief
- A source being summarized
- A heading that names a broad topic but not the specific object
- An earlier version of the article
- Another article in a series
- A caption, chart, sidebar, or interactive element the reader may not have seen

The published piece must provide its own necessary context. A link to another article may offer depth; it should not serve as a hidden antecedent for a sentence that otherwise cannot be understood.

### 12. Technically introduced but no longer accessible

Introduction is not permanent. A referent can fall out of working attention after:

- A long digression
- A section break
- Several new people or concepts
- A switch in time, location, or scale
- A change in terminology
- Multiple objects of the same grammatical gender or number

Reintroduce the noun with enough identifying information to restore it. Do not congratulate the prose for having mentioned the term twenty paragraphs earlier if the reader must search backward to recover it.

## IV. How the Failure Enters a Draft

Understanding the cause helps prevent repairs that fix one sentence while leaving the drafting habit intact.

### Private-context leakage

The writer has lived with the subject long enough that its people, categories, and relationships feel mutually known. The reader encounters them for the first time. Expertise can produce this failure as easily as carelessness.

### Prompt and source leakage

The drafting context already named the framework, study, dispute, or story. The prose carries that shared context forward even though the final reader never saw it.

### Conceptual compression

The writer moves directly from evidence to a conclusion about *the pattern*, *the system*, or *the conflict* without writing the sentence that groups the evidence and establishes the abstraction.

### Revision orphaning

An antecedent is deleted, moved, combined, or renamed. Its pronouns, definite descriptions, numbering, and transitions remain behind. This is one reason referential errors often appear in otherwise polished prose.

### Section-by-section assembly

Each section was drafted against its own outline or conversation. When the sections are joined, their openings retain assumptions that belonged to separate contexts.

### Synonym drift

A writer calls the same thing *the method*, *the approach*, *the system*, and *the model* to avoid repetition. The reader reasonably wonders whether these are four related things. Stable naming is a form of clarity, not a failure of style.

### False salience

The referent is prominent in the writer's mind but not on the page. This produces pronouns and demonstratives that feel obvious during composition and baffling during a cold read.

### Phantom continuity

The prose uses *the framework*, *this shift*, *the deeper problem*, or a similar expression to create the sound of continuity. No earlier sentence actually did the conceptual work. The passage seems to remember a paragraph that was never written.

This is common in generated prose, which can reproduce the surface signals of a developed argument without maintaining a reliable ledger of what the reader has been told. Human writers produce it too, especially when drafting quickly from a mature private outline.

## V. Repair Strategies

Choose the smallest repair that makes the intended referent available. Do not explain more than the reader needs.

### Introduce the entity

> **Unstable:** The framework distinguishes ownership from access.

> **Repaired:** This article uses a two-part framework that distinguishes ownership from access.

### Identify it within the noun phrase

> **Unstable:** The decision divided the board.

> **Repaired:** The decision to close the Tucson office divided the board.

### Name the proposition

> **Unstable:** This undermined the result.

> **Repaired:** Because the control samples were contaminated, the experiment could no longer support its original result.

### Make the bridge explicit

> **Unstable:** The architecture became the central obstacle.

> **Repaired:** As the prototype grew, its ad hoc collection of services hardened into an architecture that made every new feature harder to add.

### Establish the set before indexing it

> **Unstable:** The other explanation is simpler.

> **Repaired:** Two explanations remain: measurement error and seasonal variation. Seasonal variation is simpler.

### Supply the baseline

> **Unstable:** The new collector is more reliable.

> **Repaired:** The new collector survives off-screen rendering more reliably than the visible browser-tab collector did.

### Repeat the accurate name

> **Unstable:** Maya gave Leila the draft after she revised it.

> **Repaired:** After Maya revised the draft, she gave it to Leila.

Or, if Leila revised it:

> After Leila revised the draft, Maya sent it to her.

### Restore a distant referent

> **Unstable:** It failed for the same reason.

> **Repaired:** The cache invalidation strategy failed for the same reason: it assumed timestamps would always increase.

### Reorder the reasoning

Sometimes the necessary introduction already exists later. Move it before the first dependent reference instead of repeating it.

### Delete the empty pointer

> **Unstable:** This is why the policy failed: it excluded contractors.

> **Repaired:** The policy failed because it excluded contractors.

A transition that points vaguely backward may be less useful than a direct statement of cause.

## VI. The Referential Audit

Perform this audit after structural revision, when major introductions and deletions have settled, and again during the cold-reader pass. Do not announce the audit unless the user asks for editorial notes.

### Pass 1: Mark high-risk expressions

Inspect:

- Pronouns whose antecedents are not in the same or immediately preceding passage
- Sentence-opening *this*, *that*, *these*, and *those*
- First uses of *the* plus an article-specific abstract noun
- *Other*, *another*, *both*, *former*, *latter*, ordinals, comparisons, and superlatives
- *Again*, *still*, *already*, *return*, *stop*, *continue*, *also*, *too*, and *even*
- *Here*, *there*, *now*, *then*, *meanwhile*, *at this stage*, *above*, and *below*
- Collective sources such as *researchers*, *critics*, *experts*, and *they*
- Metaphorical labels such as *the machine*, *the bargain*, *the pipeline*, and *the conversation*

Do not revise mechanically. Marking only locates places where the prose may be asking the reader to retrieve context.

### Pass 2: Apply the tests

For each marked expression, ask:

1. **Pointing test:** What exact words, situation, or shared knowledge make the referent available?
2. **Which-one test:** Could a careful reader reasonably ask “Which one?”, “Whose?”, “Compared with what?”, “When before?”, or “What does *this* include?”
3. **Competitor test:** Is more than one plausible antecedent available?
4. **Cold-entry test:** Does the reference remain intelligible after a section break or a pause in reading?
5. **Distance test:** Has too much material intervened for a pronoun or shorthand label to remain active?
6. **Source-boundary test:** Was the referent established only in a prompt, note, source, linked article, chart, or previous draft?
7. **Intention test:** If the reader is temporarily uncertain, what does that uncertainty accomplish, and when is it resolved?

If the pointing test has no concrete answer, the prose is relying on the writer's private context.

### Pass 3: Repair and reread

Introduce, identify, repeat, reorder, or delete. Then reread the surrounding paragraph, not merely the repaired sentence. An explicit noun may expose a second problem: the paragraph never established why that noun matters.

### Stop before overcorrection

Do not produce sequences like:

> A house has a kitchen. The kitchen is a room in the house. We entered the kitchen.

The original bridge was effortless. Excess introduction delays the writing and insults the reader's ordinary inferential ability.

The goal is not maximum explicitness. It is reliable orientation at the level of effort the work intends to demand.

## VII. Mode-Specific Standards

### Fiction

Fiction may create suspense by withholding identity, history, motive, or the meaning of an object. Preserve that freedom while keeping the immediate scene legible.

Ask:

- Does the reader know who is acting, even if they do not yet know why?
- Can the reader distinguish purposeful mystery from a continuity error?
- Does delayed identification create pressure, intimacy, estrangement, or surprise?
- Does the work resolve the uncertainty before it becomes mere bookkeeping?

An unidentified *he* can be an artistic choice. Three unidentified men represented by *he* in the same scene are usually an editing problem.

### Nonfiction

Nonfiction carries a higher burden where reference affects truth, evidence, attribution, or comparison.

- Name consequential sources and bound vague groups.
- Define technical and article-specific terms near first use.
- State the basis and reference class of comparisons.
- Distinguish a source's claim from the writer's inference.
- Do not rely on the research process, linked material, or the user's prompt to supply missing context.
- Reintroduce terms when a long or modular article expects readers to enter through individual sections.

Clarity does not require resolving every implication or argument. It requires giving the reader enough information to know what the argument is about and where each consequential claim came from.

---

## VIII. Final Gate

Before delivering the work, confirm that all applicable statements are true:

- Every pronoun with a referential function has one accessible intended antecedent.
- Every sentence-opening demonstrative points to a clear noun, event, or proposition.
- Every first-use definite description is identifiable through prior discourse, an immediate strong bridge, shared context, or identifying content within the phrase.
- Every article-specific abstraction is created or defined before later sentences treat it as a settled object.
- Every relational term, enumeration, comparison, and superlative has an established set, counterpart, baseline, or reference class.
- Every *again*, *still*, *return*, *continue*, and similar trigger has the history it presupposes—or withholds that history deliberately.
- Every source or collective speaker is identified as precisely as the claim requires.
- No necessary referent exists only in the prompt, notes, sources, outline, previous draft, or another article.
- Distant referents are restored after section breaks, digressions, terminology changes, or competing candidates.
- Any remaining uncertainty is intentional, useful, and resolved at the right time.
- The revisions have not replaced graceful inference with needless exposition.

Then read once more for the sensation that prompted this guide: a sentence that sounds as though the reader has walked into a conversation already in progress. When that sensation appears, find the missing introduction—or decide, consciously, that the open door is where the piece should begin.

## Further Reading

The practical category **unintroduced referent** crosses several areas of linguistics rather than naming one formal error. Useful neighboring concepts include anaphora, discourse-new definite descriptions, cognitive accessibility, bridging, deixis, cataphora, presupposition, and accommodation.

- Herbert H. Clark, “[Bridging](https://aclanthology.org/T75-2034.pdf)” (1975), on the inferences readers use to connect new expressions with available knowledge.
- Jeanette K. Gundel, Nancy Hedberg, and Ron Zacharski, “[Cognitive Status and the Form of Referring Expressions in Discourse](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/cognitive-status-and-the-form-of-referring-expressions-in-discourse/7DC1C7EF73CFA86E91F4C6300C77EC37)” (1993), on how referring forms signal assumptions about what is accessible to a listener or reader.
- Renata Vieira and Massimo Poesio, “[An Empirically Based System for Processing Definite Descriptions](https://aclanthology.org/J00-4003.pdf)” (2000), which distinguishes direct anaphora, bridging descriptions, and discourse-new definite descriptions.
- Francis Cornish, “[‘Antecedentless’ Anaphors: Deixis, Anaphora, or What?](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/antecedentless-anaphors-deixis-anaphora-or-what-some-evidence-from-english-and-french1/B848B26BD950ABE530C9F1D36A779F62)” (1996), on expressions whose interpretation depends on more than a textual antecedent.
- Florian Schwarz, “[Presuppositions, Projection, and Accommodation](https://florianschwarz.net/wp-content/uploads/papers/HandbookExpSemPrag_Ps.pdf)” (2016), on information that language presents as already taken for granted and how readers accommodate it.
