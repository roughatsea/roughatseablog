At the 2026 Hay Festival, Sarah Wynn-Williams spent an hour onstage beside two people who had come to discuss her memoir. She did not speak. She did not nod. She did not shake her head.

The silence was not theater. In March 2025, Meta had obtained an emergency arbitration order directing its former public-policy executive to stop promoting *Careless People* and, so far as she had the power to do so, to stop its further publication. Macmillan, her publisher, was not a party to the severance agreement behind the arbitration. It kept selling the book. A year later, Wynn-Williams's counsel said Meta had warned that even her appearance at Hay could violate the order. She sat through the scheduled hour while other people talked about the book she had written. [Reuters reported the terms of the ruling](https://www.reuters.com/legal/meta-wins-halt-promotion-careless-people-tell-all-book-by-former-employee-2025-03-13/); [the Guardian documented the silent appearance](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/jun/01/meta-whistleblower-lawyer-prevented-promoting-book-sarah-wynn-williams-hay-festival).

That spectacle contains a useful distinction. Meta's intervention was real, aggressive, and partly successful: it constrained the author. It did not make the book disappear. The company could bind Wynn-Williams through a private agreement; it could not bind Macmillan through one. There is also no public evidence that Mark Zuckerberg personally ordered the legal campaign, so the precise statement is that **Meta tried to suppress a book highly critical of Meta and its senior leaders**.

Most lists of banned books begin after this point. They collect novels removed from schools, prosecuted for obscenity, forbidden at borders, or denounced by governments after copies already existed in abundance. Those histories matter. But the famous books on them are, in one sense, the survivors. *Ulysses* survived. *Lolita* survived. *The Satanic Verses* survived. Their prominence can hide a harder question:

> What happens when the effort works?

Successful suppression does not produce a celebrated banned book. It produces an empty place where a book should have been: a manuscript burned before it could be copied; a print run converted back into pulp; an edition rewritten under injunction; a work available everywhere except the country whose people it addressed; a bound typescript that exists in an archive but cannot be published.

This is an attempt to catalogue those absences.
