A book can help shape the culture around you for decades without ever reaching your bookshelf.

William Cooper's *Behold a Pale Horse* is such a book. Published in 1991, it gathered claims about government secrecy, UFOs, assassinations, secret societies, and world government. Its audience crossed boundaries that a conventional account of American politics might keep separate: UFO enthusiasts, antigovernment circles, prison readers, and hip-hop artists encountered it for different reasons. Mark Jacobson's biography, [*Pale Horse Rider*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/316378/pale-horse-rider-by-mark-jacobson/), follows that unusual cultural circulation.

Discovering Cooper late can produce two reactions at once. There is the obvious question about whether his claims are true. Then there is another, more productive question: how much consequential human thought has been happening outside the rooms we happen to frequent?

Follow that second question far enough and the route becomes unexpected. It leads from conspiracy culture to a Russian librarian who wanted humanity to resurrect everyone who had ever died; from a philosophy of hope to the design of courtyards; from games to the organization of an economy; from a novel about cosmic intelligence to a forest growing for readers a century away.

These ideas do not form a single school. Some contradict one another. Some are supported by evidence, some are arguments about values, and some are extravagant conjectures. What connects them is a question worth pursuing without requiring any one thinker to supply the final answer:

**What might existence become if intelligent beings learned to do more than survive it—if they learned to make it a place worth inhabiting?**

## First, keep the instruments of doubt

Cooper is an instructive starting point because cultural influence and reliability are different things.

The original *Behold a Pale Horse* reproduced *The Protocols of the Elders of Zion*. Cooper tried to redirect its supposed conspiracy toward the Illuminati. Substituting names does not authenticate a fabricated document. The [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion) explains the forgery's history and its use in spreading antisemitism. That is a serious evidential and ethical failure, not an incidental blemish on an otherwise reliable guide.

Yet dismissing every concern about hidden power would be another failure. The [Church Committee](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/church-committee.htm) investigated American intelligence abuses in 1975–76. COINTELPRO involved the disruption and discrediting of political targets. MKULTRA included nonconsensual drug experiments. Operation Northwoods was a 1962 military proposal for manufactured pretexts for intervention against Cuba; it was not an operation that was carried out. The [original Northwoods memorandum](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/CMC-60/joint-chiefs-pretexts-to-invade-Cuba-1962) is available through the National Security Archive.

Distinguishing a proposal from an executed program is exactly the sort of apparently small distinction on which credible skepticism depends. So is distinguishing documented abuse from a later claim about capabilities. Evidence of unethical experiments is not evidence that the experimenters achieved every power attributed to them.

The existence of real conspiracies makes careful investigation necessary. It does not make an all-encompassing conspiracy more likely simply because it can absorb those examples into its story.

Robert Shea and Robert Anton Wilson's [*Illuminatus!* trilogy](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/165363/the-illuminatus-trilogy-by-robert-shea-and-robert-anton-wilson/) approaches this territory through satire, contradictory perspectives, and the mischievous influence of Discordianism. Its usefulness here is literary: it makes certainty itself unstable. A story can teach the reader to enjoy connections while mistrusting the temptation to turn every connection into proof.

There are more concrete ways to study power. Edward Bernays's [*Propaganda*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/78634) openly advocates organized persuasion. Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky's [*Manufacturing Consent*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/78912/manufacturing-consent-by-edward-s-herman-and-noam-chomsky/) proposes institutional mechanisms through which news can become systematically biased: ownership, advertising, sourcing, organized pressure, and ideology. These are arguments to examine, not universal keys. Their advantage over a master plot is that they direct attention toward arrangements a reader can investigate.

Who pays? Who supplies the information? Which mistakes threaten a career? Who can impose a cost on an institution that fails its stated purpose?

Those questions also keep us from mistaking every bad outcome for the coordinated intention of a hidden author. Sometimes an organization produces harm because many people respond to the incentives immediately in front of them. Understanding that mechanism can make reform more specific—and more difficult—than identifying a villain.

## The discovery is larger than the secret

An unfamiliar idea can feel like something withheld. Usually the explanation is less dramatic: translation, disciplinary boundaries, publishing history, educational selection, and the repetition built into recommendation systems.

An author can be foundational inside one tradition and almost invisible in another. Knowing a name does not mean knowing its most surprising project. H. G. Wells wrote *The War of the Worlds*, but he also proposed a [*World Brain*](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262542562/world-brain/). Konstantin Tsiolkovsky helped establish the theory of spaceflight, but his technical work existed beside a speculative cosmic philosophy. A person who has encountered one has not necessarily encountered the other.

This is the territory explored in Rough at Sea's [Anti-Algorithm expeditions](/notes/the-anti-algorithm-three-expeditions/): moving beyond the next recommendation to discover a question you did not know people had already spent their lives asking.

There is relief in that discovery. Independently arriving at an idea and then finding a predecessor need not diminish the thought. It supplies interlocutors. Someone has already developed objections, invented terminology, taken a wrong turn, or followed the implications beyond the point where your own thinking stopped.

Aby Warburg's [*Mnemosyne Atlas*](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/library-collections/warburg-institute-archive/bilderatlas-mnemosyne) offers a useful image of the process. He arranged reproductions of artworks and other images on panels, using their relationships to investigate the survival and transformation of expressive forms. Juxtaposition became a way of thinking.

But an atlas of connections needs a legend. The relationship between two ideas might be documented influence, a shared historical setting, or an analogy made by a later reader. The connections in this essay are often that third kind. Putting Fyodorov beside a contemporary artist, or Suits beside an ecological economist, does not establish a hidden genealogy. It opens a comparison whose value depends on what it helps us see.

## A future that remembers the dead

Nikolai Fyodorov—also transliterated Fedorov—was a nineteenth-century Russian philosopher and librarian. His Common Task proposed that humanity unite around the physical resurrection of everyone who had died, accomplished through future science. It belonged to a Christian philosophical vision of universal kinship, not to a demonstrated research program. His [life and philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/fedorov/) are an accessible entrance to Russian Cosmism.

The immediate objection is technological. We have no demonstrated means of doing this. The thought becomes more interesting when we pause over its ethical demand before debating its feasibility.

A splendid future normally arrives by leaving people behind. Those who endured the diseases, wars, deprivation, and failed institutions that preceded it do not get to enter. Fyodorov made that exclusion central. Progress could not be complete while its beneficiaries were permanently separated from their ancestors.

One need not accept his theology or his proposed solution to feel the force of the challenge. It changes the unit of concern. Humanity becomes more than the current population plus its descendants; obligation reaches backward as well as forward.

There are difficulties beyond engineering. Would everyone want restoration? Who would decide the conditions of renewed life? What would count as recovering a particular person? Could one civilization's supposedly universal task become an excuse to subordinate every living person's purposes to a single compulsory project? A compassionate ambition still needs consent and limits on authority.

### What “quantum archaeology” adds—and does not add

Fyodorov also imagined reconstruction from dispersed physical traces. That makes him a significant predecessor to later ideas about technological resurrection. Calling him the inventor of quantum archaeology, however, would collapse different historical and scientific claims.

In the transhumanist usage at issue here, “quantum archaeology” means hypothetical future recovery of information about the past in enough detail to reconstruct a deceased person. Giulio Prisco's [2011 explanation](https://giulioprisco.blogspot.com/2011/12/quantum-archaeology.html) explicitly invokes undiscovered quantum effects. It is a primary source for an advocate's proposal, not evidence that the proposal works.

The obstacles are substantial. A physical trace is not automatically a readable record. Even if a theory preserves information at a fundamental level, that does not show that an observer could access, identify, and assemble the relevant information. Recovering a person's memories and dispositions would still leave philosophical questions about identity: whether a reconstruction continues that person's life, produces a new individual, or does something our present categories describe poorly.

Nothing about the word *quantum* resolves those problems. The historical kinship between projects is much better supported than the proposed technology.

Alfred North Whitehead offers a different response to loss. In his process philosophy, past experiences endure through their contribution to subsequent reality; in his theology, the world is received into divine experience. [Objective immortality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/process-theism/) names a kind of enduring significance and preservation. It does not, by itself, promise that an individual continues to have conscious experiences after death. Whitehead's metaphysics and Fyodorov's resurrection project should not be blended into a single claim.

Ernest Becker's [*The Denial of Death*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Denial-of-Death/Ernest-Becker/9780684832401) turns the question toward human motivation: how might mortality shape our investments in achievement, identity, and things we hope will outlast us? Adolfo Bioy Casares's [*The Invention of Morel*](https://www.nyrb.com/products/morel-s-invention), newly translated as *Morel's Invention*, approaches preservation and identity through a fugitive's encounter with mysterious visitors on an island. It is best read without further plot explanation.

Together these works refuse to let “the future” become an abstraction that forgets particular lives. They offer different answers, with different truth claims, to the difficulty of caring about someone who can disappear.

## A home that has never existed

Ernst Bloch's *The Principle of Hope* treats longing as material for philosophy. Daydreams, music, stories, architecture, and popular entertainment can carry anticipations of a life that existing arrangements have not made possible.

His [Not-Yet](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bloch/) gives this orientation a name. Bloch's account is a philosophical interpretation of an unfinished world, rooted in his distinctive Marxism. It is not a scientific finding that the universe is moving toward happiness. Hope can fail. An imagined future has to encounter material conditions and human action.

His idea of *Heimat*, or home, gives longing an unusual direction. The place of deepest belonging need not be a lost origin to which we return. It can be something still to be made.

That distinction helps explain why an invented world can feel familiar without resembling any place where we have lived. A small town in a game, a room in a novel, or a passage of music may seem to recognize a need that ordinary life leaves unnamed. The feeling is real; its interpretation remains open. It might arise from nostalgia, aesthetic convention, predictable routines, imagined social trust, or some combination of these.

Cozy games are a useful example. *Stardew Valley*, *Animal Crossing*, and the *Harvest Moon* tradition make everyday activities legible and often rewarding: tend something, make something, learn the place, become known to its inhabitants. They also differ in their demands, and “cozy” does not guarantee that a game feels restful to every player. Their appeal cannot be reduced to one cause, nor can a cultural observation establish why an entire genre became popular.

Still, Bloch invites a worthwhile reading: these worlds sometimes let people rehearse belonging. Their attraction can expose demands that have become so normal we barely notice them. Why must rest be earned? Why does useful work so often feel disconnected from its result? Why is becoming part of a place difficult?

Christopher Alexander brings the question into built space. In *The Timeless Way of Building* and the collaborative *A Pattern Language*, he pursued the conditions that make places feel alive. His [Quality Without a Name](https://christopher-alexander-ces-archive.org/research/the-quality-without-a-name/) concerns an interlocking experience of wholeness, comfort, freedom, and fit. Pattern language turns attention toward recurring relationships at many scales, from settlements to rooms and windows.

Alexander's stronger claims about objective quality remain debatable. His practical challenge survives disagreement: when a place feels hospitable, can we identify what helps produce that experience, preserve it, and build more of it? Rough at Sea's [exploration of coziness](/notes/what-is-cozy-the-science-of-feeling-safe-warm-and-at-home/) examines adjacent psychological evidence without claiming one complete science of feeling at home.

Mark Fisher supplies a more troubled counterpart to Bloch. [*Capitalist Realism*](https://www.collectiveinkbooks.com/zer0-books/our-books/capitalist-realism-new-edition) examines the sense that the existing economic order has become the boundary of possibility. His writing on lost futures asks why culture can feel haunted by developments that once seemed imminent and then failed to arrive. That is cultural criticism, not proof that an earlier era was better for everyone. Nostalgia can remember the promise and edit out who was excluded from it.

The useful task is to recover the capacity to expect something different without rehabilitating the injustices of the past.

## Once survival stops being the assignment

Imagine food, shelter, and health no longer consuming most of a person's time. What would make effort meaningful then?

Bernard Suits's [*The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia*](https://broadviewpress.com/product/the-grasshopper-third-edition/) develops its answer through philosophical dialogue. His compact definition of playing a game is a “voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles.” The rules prevent the easiest route to an outcome, and the player accepts that restriction because it makes the activity possible.

A golfer could carry the ball to the hole. Doing so would abandon what the player came to do. The inefficient means are part of the point.

Suits gives utopia something to contain after compulsory labor recedes. People could invent challenges worth undertaking for their own sake. His argument is deliberately stronger than the modest claim that leisure is pleasant, and readers need not accept that games exhaust the goods of an ideal life. Friendship, contemplation, artistic creation, and care may resist reduction to game structures.

The distinction between chosen and imposed difficulty is valuable even without accepting his whole account. Mastering a difficult game and navigating an unnecessarily obstructive benefits system may both require persistence. Their demands have different moral standing. A person can stop playing; abandoning the administrative struggle may mean losing necessities.

This is where resilience and progress come apart. Resilience helps people withstand harm. Progress changes the conditions that keep producing preventable harm. A society can praise endurance while continuing to impose the same avoidable burdens.

Removing those burdens would leave plenty of room for difficulty. Music takes practice. Discovery involves failure. Friendships require attention. A mountain remains a mountain even when nobody has to climb it to obtain food. The aim is to expand the range of worthwhile effort people can choose.

James P. Carse's [*Finite and Infinite Games*](https://jamescarse.com/books/finite-and-infinite-games/) makes a different philosophical distinction: play oriented toward winning within an ending, and play oriented toward continuing participation. It is not a result from mathematical game theory. As a metaphor for civilization, it asks whether achievement should culminate in a final victorious arrangement or keep possibilities open for people who follow.

William Morris's [*News from Nowhere*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3261) presses on a problem the postscarcity thought experiment can postpone: necessary work still has to be done. Morris imagines craft and useful activity becoming sources of pleasure. Iain M. Banks's [Culture](https://groups.google.com/g/rec.arts.sf.written/c/RMeezCFdROs/m/wuT-QSbjBSsJ) relocates much material necessity to immensely capable machines, leaving people free to pursue projects and pleasures. *The Player of Games* is a good fictional entrance, while the Culture's interventions in other societies complicate any uncomplicated portrait of abundance.

Neither a pleasing workshop nor a powerful machine settles who receives freedom. Utopia has to account for the people maintaining its conditions.

## The people keeping the world alive

Mierle Laderman Ukeles makes maintenance impossible to ignore. Her *Manifesto for Maintenance Art* and [*Touch Sanitation*](https://queensmuseum.org/exhibition/mierle-laderman-ukeles/) bring cleaning, care, and the sustained work of sanitation into artistic attention. Between 1979 and 1980, she visited New York's sanitation workforce, thanking thousands of workers for keeping the city alive.

Placed beside the Common Task, maintenance art corrects an imbalance of scale. Civilization already depends on people performing the less spectacular work of preventing disappearance: preserving buildings, feeding dependents, repairing water systems, keeping records, caring for bodies. A future vision that regards this work as beneath its dignity has misunderstood its own foundations.

Sophie de Grouchy's [*Letters on Sympathy*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sophie-de-grouchy/) offers another route from ordinary care to social philosophy. Published in 1798, her account connects embodied pleasure, pain, dependence, and sympathy to questions of justice. It is an eighteenth-century argument, not a validated modern developmental model. Its relevance is the insistence that moral life has a history in relations between vulnerable beings.

Hannah Arendt's [natality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arendt/#FreeNataPlur) directs attention toward beginnings. Each newcomer can initiate something that was not predictable from what came before. This gives future generations a role beyond inheriting a finished plan: they may change its purposes.

That is a demanding qualification to utopian design. People are not components whose needs can be specified once and optimized forever. The inhabitants must retain the power to revise the world they inherit.

## Give imagination something to build with

Fred Polak's [*The Image of the Future*](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Image_of_the_Future.html?id=LBJmAAAAMAAJ), available in Elise Boulding's translated and abridged edition, investigates how images of the future relate to cultural development. His sweeping historical thesis should be read as an interpretation, not a proven law that societies prosper whenever they become optimistic.

It nevertheless supports a useful question: what do people think their capabilities are for?

Science fiction can help supply answers. Calling it **civilizational infrastructure** is an analogy: stories can make possible arrangements discussable, supply shared references, and help coordinate aspirations. A novel cannot distribute electricity or guarantee political agreement. It can make a different daily life imaginable in enough detail that people start arguing about its institutions.

The metaphor also needs a warning. A compelling future can recruit people into authoritarian projects. Pleasant imagery can conceal extraction. A society with one authorized image of happiness may become hostile to those who want another life. The infrastructure worth building would support competing futures and public criticism of them.

Ruth Levitas's [*Utopia as Method*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9781137314253) treats imagined social reconstruction as provisional, reflexive inquiry. What assumptions about work, care, ownership, or the human person hold a proposed society together? What does it leave out? Erik Olin Wright's [Real Utopias Project](https://www.realutopiasproject.com/history/) moves toward concrete institutional alternatives, subjecting proposals to sustained criticism rather than declaring the desired destination sufficient.

Buckminster Fuller's [World Game](https://www.bfi.org/about-fuller/big-ideas/world-game/) brings that ambition into cooperative simulation. Participants use information about resources and human needs to explore how the world might work better. Its educational and design ambitions do not establish that a simulation can solve poverty or reconcile political conflict.

Models must choose objectives, simplify reality, and decide whose losses count. Better information does not dissolve disagreements about ownership or redistribute power by itself. A technically efficient plan can fail because the people who would lose influence can block it, or because it never represented the affected people adequately in the first place.

Elinor Ostrom supplies firmer empirical ground. Her research on shared resources documented arrangements through which communities could govern commons without fitting a simple market-versus-state choice. Her [Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/lecture/) examines polycentric governance: multiple centers of decision-making operating across scales. Cooperation has conditions, including workable rules, monitoring, conflict resolution, and participation. It is neither automatic nor ruled out by a slogan about human selfishness.

Stafford Beer's organizational cybernetics asks a related coordination question. Project Cybersyn, developed by Chilean participants working with Beer during Salvador Allende's government, combined telex networks, software, economic modeling, and an operations-room design. The undertaking remained incomplete and ended with the 1973 coup. Eden Medina's [history](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262016490/cybernetic-revolutionaries/) examines its technical work and political limits. Beer's [*Designing Freedom*](https://houseofanansi.com/products/designing-freedom) extends the question of how organizations might coordinate while preserving autonomy.

Alexander Bogdanov's earlier *Tektology* sought principles of organization across fields; his [*Red Star*](https://iupress.org/9780253013507/red-star/) imagined socialist life on Mars. Ivan Illich's [*Tools for Conviviality*](https://www.panarchy.org/illich/conviviality.html) supplies a test that ambitious systems can fail: does a tool expand people's capacity to direct their lives, or make them dependent on institutions they cannot influence?

Read together, these thinkers complicate the hope that better administration alone will produce a better world. The incentives governing an institution, the information reaching its decision-makers, the distribution of authority, and the ability to challenge decisions all matter. Greed is one source of dysfunction. It is not a complete account of institutional failure.

David Graeber's [essay on pointless work](https://davidgraeber.org/articles/on-the-phenomenon-of-bullshit-jobs-a-work-rant/) asks why organizations can sustain activities that workers themselves struggle to justify. With David Wengrow, [*The Dawn of Everything*](https://academic.macmillan.com/academictrade/9781250858801/thedawnofeverything/) challenges narratives that make present hierarchies seem the inevitable result of social complexity. Both works are provocative arguments; their scope and interpretations warrant scrutiny. They help make the present arrangement available for questioning.

## A planet is not an unlimited supply closet

Kenneth Boulding's [*The Economics of the Coming Spaceship Earth*](https://www.panarchy.org/boulding/spaceship.1966.html) places a physical constraint beneath these institutional questions. An economy cannot indefinitely treat its surroundings as an unlimited source of material and an unlimited destination for waste. Earth receives solar energy and is approximately closed with respect to matter; these are different kinds of boundary.

A humane future has to sustain the conditions of its abundance. More production is not an adequate measure of success when it erodes the systems on which life depends.

Joanna Macy's [Great Turning](https://www.joannamacy.net/main) frames a possible movement toward a life-sustaining civilization through resistance to damage, alternative arrangements, and changes in understanding. It is an ethical and activist orientation rather than a prediction guaranteed by history.

Architecture makes the test tangible. Paolo Soleri's [Arcosanti](https://arcosanti.org/vision/the-arcosanti-story/), begun in Arizona in 1970, attempts to combine architecture and ecology in a compact settlement. It remains far smaller than the projected city. Its value as an experiment does not establish the success of the original vision. Questions about who builds, governs, finances, and maintains a place are part of its architecture, not external inconveniences to be considered after the beautiful model.

Hildegard of Bingen offers a much older vocabulary of flourishing. Her *viriditas*, or greenness, has spiritual and theological meanings extending beyond the color of plants. A [scholarly account](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108573832.007) situates it in her writing and its earlier traditions. It would be anachronistic to enlist her as a modern ecological scientist. Her language can still sharpen attention to the difference between remaining intact and being alive with possibility.

## Who gets to inhabit the future?

The word *humanity* can hide an enormous assumption: that our way of experiencing the world is the only kind worth considering.

Jakob von Uexküll's [*A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781452903798/a-foray-into-the-worlds-of-animals-and-humans/) introduces the *Umwelt*: an organism's meaningful world, structured by its capacities to perceive and act. A dog and a human can share a street without encountering the same field of significance. Designing a world worth inhabiting requires asking whose experience the design recognizes.

Lynn Margulis's work on [endosymbiosis](https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/resources/heroes/margulis/) complicates another apparently obvious boundary: the individual. The bacterial origins of mitochondria and plastids are firmly supported. Some of the partnerships that made complex life possible became components of new organisms. This does not prove that social cooperation is always good, or that all of Margulis's broader theories were correct. It does show why familiar boundaries between individual and collective deserve investigation.

Michael Levin and collaborators study how bioelectric signaling contributes to anatomical development and regeneration. [Published work on planarians](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24882814/) describes altered regenerative patterns following interventions in cellular communication. Those findings can be studied separately from broader proposals to describe tissues as cognitive systems. Neither the experiments nor the vocabulary of memory licenses a claim that consciousness has been explained or that human resurrection is near.

The philosophical opening is more modest and more useful: perhaps some of our inherited categories of agency, individuality, and intelligence are too narrow. That possibility deserves experiments with clear claims and opportunities for failure.

Olaf Stapledon's [*Star Maker*](https://www.weslpress.org/9780819566935/star-maker/), published in 1937, supplies an imaginative counterpart. Its narrator's journey expands through alien worlds and increasingly vast forms of mentality. The novel uses cosmic scale to make human assumptions look local.

It is not a cheerful travel brochure for the future. Its account of creation places suffering and the relation between creator and creatures under pressure. That makes it a valuable companion to utopian thought: larger intelligence and larger scale do not automatically mean a kinder existence. The ability to build something and the ability to care for its inhabitants remain separate achievements.

Ivan Yefremov's *Andromeda*, discussed in the [*Encyclopedia of Science Fiction*](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/yefremov_ivan), imagines a far-future socialist civilization and wider cosmic fellowship. Its assumptions differ from Banks's and Stapledon's. Reading across those differences is more fruitful than assembling a shelf of supposedly interchangeable optimistic futures.

## The far horizon needs its own evidence labels

Tsiolkovsky's [cosmic philosophy](https://www.tsiolkovsky.org/en/the-cosmic-philosophy/the-cosmic-philosophy/) places spaceflight within speculations about matter, feeling, and the long development of intelligence. His contributions to astronautics do not verify his metaphysics. Some of his prescriptions about the direction of life also deserve ethical criticism; cosmic ambition does not make a vision humane.

J. D. Bernal's [*The World, the Flesh and the Devil*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bernal/works/1920s/soul/) imagined space habitats, modified bodies, and transformed intelligence in 1929. It is useful evidence that contemporary-sounding posthuman questions have a longer history. It is not evidence that those transformations will occur, or that everyone would welcome them.

John von Neumann's theory of self-reproducing automata and later [NASA systems-engineering studies](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19800025701) give autonomous expansion a more formal foundation. A construction principle, however, is not a working asteroid factory. Materials, energy, manufacturing closure, control, and error correction remain real engineering problems.

Freeman Dyson's 1979 [*Time Without End*](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.51.447) asked whether life could continue indefinitely under specified cosmological and biological assumptions. His analysis made the assumptions explicit enough to be challenged. [Lawrence Krauss and Glenn Starkman](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9902189) examined how a universe dominated by a cosmological constant changes the prospects. Indefinite subjective life is not an established forecast. The lesson is methodological: ambition becomes more intellectually useful when someone states what would have to be true.

Other discoveries belong in a different category. Jean Gebser's [structures of consciousness](https://gebser.org/jean-gebser-bio/) are a speculative philosophical account, not a confirmed scientific sequence of mental evolution. Alexander Chizhevsky's attempts to relate solar cycles to human history are [historically interesting](https://chizhevsky.gmik.ru/en/meet-a-l-chizhevsky/), but sweeping claims about revolutions and wars following those cycles are not established science. A commemorative museum is a source for a thinker's career, not independent validation of his hypotheses.

John C. Lilly's [Earth Coincidence Control Office](https://www.williamjames.com/transcripts/lilly.htm) is an especially clear boundary case: a personal cosmology of meaningful coincidences, not a scientifically demonstrated cosmic organization. Philip K. Dick's [*VALIS* and *Exegesis*](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/philip-k-dick/the-exegesis-of-philip-k-dick/9780575132443/) preserve another extraordinary encounter between experience, interpretation, and proliferating explanations. Their literary and autobiographical interest does not authenticate their cosmological possibilities.

Ancient [Gnostic traditions](https://iep.utm.edu/gnostic/), including writings associated with the Nag Hammadi collection, offer varied accounts of hidden origins, inferior creators, and liberating knowledge. These traditions should not be flattened into one doctrine, or treated as confirmed descriptions of reality. Their resemblance to modern fiction is an invitation to comparison; a claim that a particular game or film borrowed from a particular text needs evidence of its own.

Wonder survives these distinctions. It can even become more interesting when fascination no longer has to carry the burden of belief.

## Stories that keep other possibilities available

Jorge Luis Borges can turn a philosophical problem into a place. “The Library of Babel” and “The Garden of Forking Paths,” collected in [*Labyrinths*](https://www.ndbooks.com/book/labyrinths-selected-stories-and-other-writings/), make infinity, classification, and branching possibility available to the imagination. Their worlds are not necessarily hospitable. They enlarge what fiction can hold.

Andrei Tarkovsky's [*Stalker* and *Solaris*](https://www.filmlinc.org/series/two-by-tarkovsky/) complicate desire and recognition. What would it mean to have a deepest wish fulfilled if you did not fully know what that wish was? What do we do when an apparent encounter with another being is entangled with our own memory and need? These are slow films that make uncertainty part of the experience rather than a puzzle to be cleared away.

Ursula K. Le Guin's [“The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction”](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/the-carrier-bag-theory-of-fiction) offers a different principle of organization. A story can gather, sustain, and connect. It need not derive all its movement from conquest. This matters for imagining humane worlds: a culture that recognizes only spectacular victory will struggle to tell good stories about maintenance, mutual dependence, and the continued life of a place.

There can be conflict in such stories. The question is what the story teaches us to notice, and which activities it allows to count as consequential.

Paul Otlet and Henri La Fontaine's Mundaneum project, and Wells's *World Brain*, pursued a related ambition through knowledge systems. Otlet's [place in information history](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/networking-the-web/) predates the web, with classification, cards, and proposals for remotely accessible knowledge. Neither project was simply the internet before computers. Both ask whether shared access to organized knowledge might help people coordinate a world.

The internet demonstrates how incomplete that hope was. Access does not guarantee understanding. Information systems also require provenance, correction, governance, and ways to resist manipulation. The route from Cooper to Otlet returns us to the same practical problem: how to make discovery easier without making credulity easier alongside it.

## Plant something for a reader you will never meet

Katie Paterson's [Future Library](https://www.futurelibrary.no/) began in 2014. A forest in Norway is growing to supply paper for texts intended to be published in 2114. Writers contribute manuscripts to be held unread until then.

Its realization is uncertain. Trees must survive. Institutions must persist. People must continue handing responsibility to successors. The work binds a distant promise to activities someone can perform now: protect the forest, preserve the manuscripts, sustain the trust.

Placed beside Fyodorov, it faces in the opposite temporal direction. One asks that future humanity remember those who came before; the other prepares an encounter for people who will come after. Neither can be reduced to an optimistic mood. Both make time a question of responsibility.

We do not need confidence that every cosmic ambition can be achieved to distinguish preventable suffering from chosen difficulty. We do not need a final model of happiness to notice that an institution rewards conduct contrary to its purpose. We can ask people what makes a place inhabitable, test changes, make the results visible, and preserve their ability to disagree.

A civilization that makes people increasingly good at enduring avoidable harm has left work unfinished. So has one that removes hardship while allowing little room for curiosity, affection, or play.

In Norway, part of that unfinished work currently takes the form of growing trees. Someone must keep tending them long enough for a stranger to turn the page.

## Reading routes through the territory

The links above connect claims to sources. These routes offer a less linear way into the works. Publisher pages identify editions; they do not necessarily provide free full texts. Links to advocates and commemorative institutions document what someone proposed, not whether it is true.

### For discovery without surrendering skepticism

- **William Cooper in context:** Mark Jacobson, [*Pale Horse Rider*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/316378/pale-horse-rider-by-mark-jacobson/). A cultural biography is a better first orientation than treating *Behold a Pale Horse* as a reference work. Pair it with the Holocaust Museum's [account of the *Protocols*](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion).
- **Play with conspiracy narratives:** Robert Shea and Robert Anton Wilson, [*The Illuminatus! Trilogy*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/165363/the-illuminatus-trilogy-by-robert-shea-and-robert-anton-wilson/). Satirical fiction drawing on Discordianism; expect deliberate instability.
- **Investigate documented power:** the Senate's [Church Committee history](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/church-committee.htm), followed by primary records. Keep COINTELPRO, MKULTRA, and the Northwoods proposal distinct.
- **Examine persuasion and representation:** Bernays, [*Propaganda*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/78634); Herman and Chomsky, [*Manufacturing Consent*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/78912/manufacturing-consent-by-edward-s-herman-and-noam-chomsky/); Guy Debord, [*The Society of the Spectacle*](https://www.zonebooks.org/books/3-the-society-of-the-spectacle). Adam Curtis's *The Century of the Self* is a film companion on psychology and public relations; watch its montage as a historical argument, not an unmediated record.
- **Discover through juxtaposition:** Warburg's [*Mnemosyne Atlas*](https://warburg.sas.ac.uk/library-collections/warburg-institute-archive/bilderatlas-mnemosyne). The panels invite looking before committing to a total explanation.

### For hope, loss, and a world that feels like home

- **The largest moral ambition:** begin with the [introduction to Fyodorov](https://iep.utm.edu/fedorov/), then pursue *What Was Man Created For?* and George M. Young's [*The Russian Cosmists*](https://academic.oup.com/book/6271). Read [Prisco's quantum-archaeology proposal](https://giulioprisco.blogspot.com/2011/12/quantum-archaeology.html) separately, as conjecture.
- **Two different accounts of mortality:** Whitehead through the [process-theism overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/process-theism/), and Becker's [*The Denial of Death*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Denial-of-Death/Ernest-Becker/9780684832401). Preservation in metaphysics and explanations of human motivation are different projects.
- **A future home:** the Stanford [Bloch overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bloch/) before the three volumes of *The Principle of Hope*. Pair it with Fisher's [*Capitalist Realism*](https://www.collectiveinkbooks.com/zer0-books/our-books/capitalist-realism-new-edition) and his [*Ghosts of My Life*](https://www.collectiveinkbooks.com/zero-books/our-books/ghosts-my-life) for the cultural experience of futures that seem to have disappeared.
- **Make belonging concrete:** Alexander's [Quality Without a Name](https://christopher-alexander-ces-archive.org/research/the-quality-without-a-name/), then *The Timeless Way of Building* and *A Pattern Language*. For a contrasting historical vocabulary, see Hildegard's [*viriditas*](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108573832.007).
- **Care and beginnings:** Grouchy's [*Letters on Sympathy*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sophie-de-grouchy/), Arendt on [natality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arendt/#FreeNataPlur), Ukeles's [maintenance art](https://queensmuseum.org/exhibition/mierle-laderman-ukeles/), and Paterson's [Future Library](https://www.futurelibrary.no/).

### For play and the institutions supporting it

- **Begin with the grasshopper:** Suits, [*The Grasshopper*](https://broadviewpress.com/product/the-grasshopper-third-edition/), then Carse, [*Finite and Infinite Games*](https://jamescarse.com/books/finite-and-infinite-games/). They use play to ask different questions.
- **Imagine daily life after necessity:** Morris, [*News from Nowhere*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3261); Banks, *The Player of Games*, with his [notes on the Culture](https://groups.google.com/g/rec.arts.sf.written/c/RMeezCFdROs/m/wuT-QSbjBSsJ); Yefremov's *Andromeda*, introduced [here](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/yefremov_ivan).
- **Treat alternatives as research:** Levitas, [*Utopia as Method*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9781137314253), and Wright's [Real Utopias Project](https://www.realutopiasproject.com/history/). Read Ostrom's [Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/lecture/) for empirical investigations of collective governance.
- **Study coordination and autonomy:** Fuller's [World Game](https://www.bfi.org/about-fuller/big-ideas/world-game/); Medina's [*Cybernetic Revolutionaries*](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262016490/cybernetic-revolutionaries/); Beer's [*Designing Freedom*](https://houseofanansi.com/products/designing-freedom); Illich's [*Tools for Conviviality*](https://www.panarchy.org/illich/conviviality.html). Bogdanov's [*Red Star*](https://iupress.org/9780253013507/red-star/) supplies a fictional companion to his organizational ambitions in *Tektology*.
- **Question what counts as necessary:** Graeber's [short essay on work](https://davidgraeber.org/articles/on-the-phenomenon-of-bullshit-jobs-a-work-rant/) and Graeber and Wengrow's [*The Dawn of Everything*](https://academic.macmillan.com/academictrade/9781250858801/thedawnofeverything/).
- **Keep the physical world in view:** Boulding's [Spaceship Earth essay](https://www.panarchy.org/boulding/spaceship.1966.html), Macy's [work on the Great Turning](https://www.joannamacy.net/main), and the history of [Arcosanti](https://arcosanti.org/vision/the-arcosanti-story/).

### For minds and futures beyond the familiar

- **The cosmic starting point:** Stapledon, [*Star Maker*](https://www.weslpress.org/9780819566935/star-maker/). Read it for philosophical scale and alien perspectives rather than a conventional character-driven adventure.
- **Earlier posthuman possibilities:** Tsiolkovsky's [cosmic writings](https://www.tsiolkovsky.org/en/the-cosmic-philosophy/the-cosmic-philosophy/) and Bernal's [1929 essay](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bernal/works/1920s/soul/). Separate engineering accomplishments from philosophy and political commitments.
- **Possibilities with explicit assumptions:** Dyson's [1979 paper](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.51.447), the [Krauss–Starkman challenge](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9902189), and NASA's [self-replicating systems study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19800025701), following von Neumann's theoretical work.
- **Different kinds of living world:** Uexküll, [*A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781452903798/a-foray-into-the-worlds-of-animals-and-humans/); NASA's [Margulis profile](https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/resources/heroes/margulis/); Levin and collaborators on [bioelectric pattern information](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24882814/).
- **Speculative intellectual history:** Gebser's [*The Ever-Present Origin*](https://gebser.org/jean-gebser-bio/), Chizhevsky's [career](https://chizhevsky.gmik.ru/en/meet-a-l-chizhevsky/), and Lilly's [E.C.C.O. account](https://www.williamjames.com/transcripts/lilly.htm). These carry very different evidential standing from experimental findings in biology.

### For fiction that changes the shape of the question

- **Short conceptual worlds:** Borges, [*Labyrinths*](https://www.ndbooks.com/book/labyrinths-selected-stories-and-other-writings/), beginning with “The Library of Babel” or “The Garden of Forking Paths.”
- **Preserved people and uncertain reality:** Bioy Casares, [*Morel's Invention*](https://www.nyrb.com/products/morel-s-invention); Dick, *VALIS*, with the [*Exegesis*](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/philip-k-dick/the-exegesis-of-philip-k-dick/9780575132443/) as a much larger later undertaking. For historical background rather than a key to all these works, read an [introduction to Gnostic traditions](https://iep.utm.edu/gnostic/).
- **Desire under examination:** Tarkovsky's [*Stalker* and *Solaris*](https://www.filmlinc.org/series/two-by-tarkovsky/). Allow time and attention; neither film is in a hurry to resolve its uncertainty.
- **Another way to hold a story:** Le Guin's [“The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction”](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/the-carrier-bag-theory-of-fiction). A short essay with implications for which kinds of life fiction makes visible.
- **Imagination and shared knowledge:** Polak's [*The Image of the Future*](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Image_of_the_Future.html?id=LBJmAAAAMAAJ), Otlet in the [Computer History Museum timeline](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/networking-the-web/), and Wells's [*World Brain*](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262542562/world-brain/).
