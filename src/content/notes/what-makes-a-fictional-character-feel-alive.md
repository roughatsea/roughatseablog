Two characters enter a burning building to rescue a child.

The first is brave. He runs through the smoke, finds the child, carries her outside, and receives the grateful applause of the crowd. His behavior is consistent with everything the story has told us: he is a good man who does good things.

The second is brave, too. But when he hears the child screaming, he freezes. The sound recalls another rescue, years earlier, when a child died in his arms. For a moment he feels something so ugly that he can barely acknowledge it: he wishes this child would stop screaming, even if that meant he did not have to go inside.

He goes inside anyway. He saves her. When people call him a hero, he feels ashamed—not because he hesitated, but because he knows what he briefly wished.

Both characters perform the same heroic act. Only one has begun to feel like someone who might exist when the book is closed.

What creates that difference? What makes a character three-dimensional rather than flat, relatable rather than remote, worth cheering for rather than merely watching? And can psychology tell us anything that literary criticism has not already understood?

A useful answer begins by separating four qualities we too often lump together: **depth, identification, sympathy, and fascination**. They can overlap, but none guarantees the others.

## The persuasive illusion of another mind

A great fictional character creates the impression of a consciousness that exists independently of the plot. The character appears to have private priorities, mistaken assumptions, embarrassing impulses, and ways of seeing the world that the author did not invent merely to advance the next scene.

E. M. Forster's *Aspects of the Novel* (1927) supplied the enduring distinction between *flat* and *round* characters. His most useful criterion for roundness is not how many traits a character possesses, but whether the character can surprise the reader convincingly.

Those last two words belong together. A protagonist who behaves randomly is surprising but not deep. A protagonist who does exactly what the reader predicts, for exactly the reasons the narrator has already explained, may be coherent but uninteresting. Roundness lives in the space between: behavior that initially surprises us and becomes intelligible as we learn who this person is.

The soldier's hesitation complicates his bravery without canceling it. In fact, it makes his courage more consequential. His visible action and private experience disagree, but they do not contradict the underlying human being.

There is a common shortcut that does not work particularly well: give a virtuous character a vice, or give an abrasive character a sad childhood, and call the result complexity. A list of inconsistent attributes is not a personality. **Depth emerges when the attributes causally interact.**

A generous woman might refuse help because she fears indebtedness. A ruthless leader might show tenderness toward an animal because the animal asks nothing of him. A brilliant doctor might be incapable of apologizing because an admission of error threatens the identity on which her confidence depends. The interesting question is not whether people can contain opposites. Of course they can. It is why these opposites coexist in *this* person.

## Relatable to whom?

Writers are often told to make their characters relatable. The instruction sounds practical until one asks what it means.

A reader may have little in common with an immortal goddess, a desperate criminal, or an artificial intelligence. Yet the goddess's fear of losing a loved one, the criminal's craving for dignity, or the machine's uncertainty about its own worth may be acutely recognizable. Conversely, a character with precisely the reader's age, profession, and social background can remain emotionally inaccessible.

A [2023 meta-analysis of character–audience similarity and identification](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-023-04842-4) found that similarity had a relatively small overall relationship with identification, and psychological similarity was more consequential than many observable similarities. The specific measurements and contexts matter, but the broad implication is useful: identification cannot be reduced to demographic resemblance.

At least three reader experiences deserve separate names:

- **Recognition:** I know what that feeling is like.
- **Understanding:** I can follow the emotional logic, even if I would never make that choice.
- **Identification:** For a little while, I inhabit this character's perspective or goals.

We can understand a villain without identifying with him. We can identify with an alien without sharing his circumstances. We can feel compassion for a character whose decisions we consider indefensible.

The reader does not need a fictional twin. Often what matters is finding a familiar emotional structure inside an unfamiliar life.

## Why we cheer—and why we sometimes keep watching when we don't

Wanting a character to win is not the same as wanting to know what happens next.

Samwise Gamgee, in *The Lord of the Rings*, earns our investment through devotion, vulnerability, perseverance, and the tangible cost of his loyalty. Walter White, in *Breaking Bad*, can command attention even as his choices become increasingly intolerable. His intelligence and self-justifications invite scrutiny, not necessarily approval.

Media psychology has long examined these differences through concepts such as identification and *affective disposition*: the attachments and aversions audiences form toward fictional people. Morality matters to these judgments, but liking is only one form of engagement.

An admirable character invites hope. A pitiable one invites compassion. A dangerous or self-deceiving one may invite fascination. A sufficiently complex character can move the reader through all three responses.

What makes a character's goal emotionally gripping is often not its size but its **cost**.

A woman wants to become the first in her family to attend college. So far, she has a recognizable objective. Her father is dying, and she is the only available caregiver. Now the objective has a price. He has never supported her education, but at last he tells her to go. Her victory might feel like abandonment; her sacrifice might feel like surrender.

The central question is no longer simply whether she gets into college. It is whether she can choose a future without becoming someone she cannot respect.

Stories make values visible by forcing them into competition. A person may sincerely believe both that loyalty is sacred and that a child must be protected at any cost. A plot becomes character-revealing when the two commitments cannot both be honored.

An accomplished author does not necessarily settle the moral argument for the reader. The choice can reveal what the character values most, or disclose that the character never knew their own priorities.

## The character's way of seeing is part of the character

The most demanding characterization occurs not only in what a fictional person does, but in what they notice.

Consider a simple event: Margaret arrives home to find a messy living room.

> Margaret entered the house. It was untidy, and she became angry at her irresponsible husband.

Now consider the same scene viewed through a more particular consciousness:

> Three coffee mugs on the table. A sock beneath the sofa. Apparently David had spent eight uninterrupted hours at home without encountering a single household object.

The second version gives us Margaret's attention, judgment, and exasperated sense of humor. She does not need to announce her emotion. The scene is already filtered through it.

Literary critics call one technique for accomplishing this *free indirect discourse*: third-person narration slips toward a character's language and perspective without explicitly announcing the transition. In *How Fiction Works*, James Wood examines the extraordinary subtlety with which narration can place us both inside and outside a fictional consciousness.

The same room would look different to someone anxious about a missing child, someone relieved to be home, or someone who has never felt welcome there.

A distinct personality is not merely a different set of answers to life's questions. It is a different set of questions, a different allocation of attention, a different model of what matters.

This is also why eloquent dialogue is not enough. If everyone in a novel notices the same things, frames problems the same way, and expresses pain with the same beautifully balanced sentences, the writer's voice has swallowed the characters.

## The private life that exceeds the plot

A detective must solve the mystery. That is a narrative function, not yet a person.

Suppose the detective resents how colleagues celebrate cases in which a killer was caught but a victim remains dead. He notices handwriting before faces. He repeatedly delays calling his adult daughter, though he reads her last message every evening. None of these details needs to turn into a revelation about the murderer.

But they should not be random decoration. The details may suggest a habit of substituting professional exactitude for intimacy, an obsession with recoverable evidence in a life full of irrecoverable losses.

A convincing fictional mind usually implies more than the story explicitly discloses. The reader senses that the person has a continuous existence beyond the handful of scenes selected for publication.

Too much explanation can damage that effect. A woman refuses to attend her father's funeral. The novelist can supply a tidy explanation—she resents him for leaving when she was young—or show her meticulously polishing his old shoes on the morning of the service and then refusing to leave the house.

The second scene supports several interpretations at once. Grief, anger, devotion, paralysis, and guilt might all be present. The uncertainty is not a missing piece of characterization. It is part of the character's plausibility.

Human beings seldom understand their own motives as neatly as a conventional narrator might explain them.

## Three master classes in being wrong about oneself

Three familiar literary characters show how misunderstanding one's own mind can generate a whole novel.

In Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice*, **Elizabeth Bennet** is genuinely witty and perceptive. Those strengths also help produce her overconfidence. Her intelligence does not shield her from error; it helps her elaborate the error persuasively. A virtue and a weakness share the same root.

In Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment*, **Raskolnikov** constructs intellectual theories that fail to contain his actual emotional experience. Reading him involves investigating the discrepancy between his explanation of himself and his behavior.

In Tolstoy's *Anna Karenina*, **Anna** cannot be adequately compressed into either a cautionary tale about moral failure or a romantic tale about desire. Her emotional needs collide with social conditions, self-judgment, fear, and the limits of the world available to her.

These works are not great merely because their characters suffer. They are great in part because their characters' *interpretations of their suffering* alter what happens next.

The most revealing mistake a fictional person makes may not be a mistaken decision. It may be a mistaken theory about who they are.

## What experimental psychology can—and cannot—tell us

Literary critics have described these effects for generations. Psychologists are now attempting to isolate and measure pieces of the reader's response.

In [*Conceptualizing and Measuring Character Depth*](https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2025.2460171), published online in 2025, Rebecca Frazer, Matthew Grizzard, and Emily Moyer-Gusé treated perceived depth as the extent to which characterization evokes a detailed, multifaceted mental model of a fictional person's psyche, behavior, and experience. Their work introduced and tested a six-item measure. This does not constitute a laboratory definition of literary greatness; it makes one important part of our reaction measurable.

In a subsequent [2026 experimental investigation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.106506), the researchers manipulated character depth alongside moral behavior. Greater perceived depth affected how audiences evaluated characters, including subsequent emotional responses. The result matters because depth is not simply another name for goodness. Our response to a person is influenced by how fully we conceive of them, not just whether we endorse what they do.

Research on identification also complicates the idea that an audience has one general level of connection. A [2024 study developing a multidimensional identification scale](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-024-06191-2) distinguished components including perspective-taking, understanding, emotional involvement, and psychological merging. Those dimensions can move together, but they are not interchangeable. The popular request to make someone “relatable” hides several different artistic goals.

A further question is whether complicated fictional minds train readers to understand real minds. A [2026 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1865031), synthesizing 21 studies and pooling results from 12 of them, reported a small overall association or advantage on empathy-related and social-cognitive outcomes (Hedges' *g* = 0.23). The studies were heterogeneous, with uneven methods and outcomes. The findings do **not** establish that reading a particular literary novel will make someone kinder, or that intricate characterization reliably produces durable empathy.

The more modest conclusion is still interesting: mentally simulating complex social situations may exercise some capacities involved in perspective-taking, particularly when readers engage actively rather than passively.

Most importantly, the new research measures something that exists partly **inside the reader**. A character's depth is not simply the number of facts on the page. It is the richness of the person the audience constructs from those facts.

A novelist can write twenty pages of childhood biography and produce a cardboard person. Another can write one evasive answer to a difficult question and cause readers to infer a lifetime.

I suspect the most fruitful way to think about characterization is as **guided inference**. The writer chooses actions, omissions, perceptions, and consequences. The reader works backward to assemble a human being. This is an interpretive synthesis of literary and cognitive theory, not a scientifically validated formula for creating great characters.

## A character whose expertise becomes her undoing

Consider an engineer aboard a generation ship, responsible for keeping a thousand passengers alive on a century-long journey.

A conventional protagonist might be brilliant, determined, protective, and skilled at solving technical crises. There is nothing wrong with her. There is also nothing that yet makes her psychologically necessary to the story.

Now give her a contradiction that reaches into her profession. She believes survival of the ship must take precedence over any individual life. Yet she cannot bear being personally responsible for someone's death.

Twenty years earlier, she made the mathematically correct decision to sacrifice six people to save several hundred. Everyone called her a hero. She has spent two decades organizing her identity around the obligation to make terrible decisions, while privately refusing herself forgiveness.

The agricultural system now begins to fail. The reliable solution requires a permanent reduction in population. She informs the governing council. What she does *not* disclose is a speculative alternative that might save everyone, but might destroy the ship.

Her choice follows her convictions. It also protects her from reopening a wound she has mistaken for wisdom.

When a young technician discovers the suppressed alternative, she becomes furious. He is challenging her engineering judgment, certainly. But he is also threatening the belief that has allowed her to live with herself for twenty years: that certainty is more ethical than hope.

Later, she opens the population registry and studies the photographs of the people her recommended solution would condemn. She knows all their names.

Now her intelligence, fear, professional ethics, guilt, and self-conception have become one mechanism. Her defining strength is implicated in her possible downfall. She does not merely experience the plot. **Her particular psychology generates it.**

An entirely different engineer, placed aboard the same ship with the same machinery and the same crisis, would create a different novel.

That is an unusually rigorous test of character: if the protagonist were replaced with another broadly competent, likable person, would the story still unfold in essentially the same way?

## A working test for writers

The following questions are a craft diagnostic, not a validated psychometric scale. Not every character needs to pass every test. In comedy, allegory, and supporting roles, deliberate flatness may be precisely what the work requires.

For a major psychologically developed character, however, ask:

1. **Independent existence:** Does this person seem to care about things that do not serve the immediate plot?
2. **Selective attention:** Would they notice and interpret the same room differently from someone else?
3. **Psychological causality:** Can their surprising choices be understood through their values, habits, and history?
4. **Productive contradiction:** Do their strengths and weaknesses grow from related causes?
5. **Agency:** Do their decisions change what happens, including consequences they would prefer to avoid?
6. **Moral independence:** Can the reader understand them without having to approve of them?
7. **Interpretive space:** Is there something meaningful the reader must infer rather than simply be told?
8. **Relational variety:** Do different relationships reveal different credible aspects of their personality?
9. **Emotional investment:** Does the reader care whether they succeed, fail, escape, confess, or persist—even if only out of fascination?
10. **Irreplaceability:** Would the story become fundamentally different if a different person occupied their role?

An affirmative answer to ten questions will not manufacture artistic greatness. But unanswered questions can expose exactly where a protagonist has become a delivery device for the author's ideas.

## The character who changes the reader

There is a final standard more demanding than depth, plausibility, or likability.

A competent writer can make a reader understand a fictional person. An exceptional writer can let the reader understand that person better than the person understands themselves. On rare occasions, a character then exposes something the reader has not understood about their own life.

Perhaps an apparently noble character's generosity reveals the covert desire for admiration beneath our own kindness. Perhaps an apparently cowardly character's decision helps us see the difference between being unafraid and being brave. Or a strange, distant person demonstrates a possibility for tenderness, endurance, or self-knowledge that had not previously occurred to us.

These effects cannot be reduced to a checklist, and no current experiment reliably predicts which invented people will live in readers' memories for decades. Different readers bring different histories, values, and emotional expectations to the same page.

But the possibility is extraordinary. A novelist can arrange a series of sentences so that a stranger begins to seem alive. The reader supplies thoughts the sentences never literally contain. Eventually, in trying to understand that stranger, the reader notices something about themselves.

A flat character performs a function in a story. A three-dimensional character seems to possess a life. The rarest characters make the reader's own life look different.
