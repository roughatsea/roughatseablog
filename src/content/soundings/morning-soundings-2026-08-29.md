# Morning Soundings: The Hour Electricity Wanted to Be Used

We built an ethic around turning things off. A grid fed by weather sometimes needs the opposite.

Today’s deeper question is what abundance does to a price signal. First, three acts of redirection: domestic objects become story grammar, lithium failures are pushed sideways, and old films acquire music that their makers never heard.

## Three signals

### A room becomes a sentence when the player places the objects

*Whisper of the House* arrived on PlayStation 5 on August 27. The premise is small enough to overlook: play the housekeeper in a town, help residents move, clear clutter, prepare shops, arrange keepsakes, and occasionally find a cat. GD Studio says the game contains more than 1,800 pieces of furniture, over ten layouts, and no single correct arrangement for many commissions. Its [account of the design](https://blog.playstation.com/2026/08/27/whisper-of-the-house-opens-its-doors-on-ps5-today/) calls the mystery layer “cozy snooping.”

That phrase identifies a smart exchange of verbs. Most detective games make objects legible by letting the player inspect them. Here, interpretation happens through handling: a cup, letter, plant, or unfinished corner has to be moved before the resident’s life comes into focus. Placement works like syntax. The same belongings can produce comfort, concealment, neglect, or ceremony depending on their relations.

This is the quiet strength of maintenance games. Tidying is usually what happens between adventures or after the credits. Making it central treats care as consequential action and interior design as environmental storytelling performed by the player. Whether the mysteries justify that lovely machinery is a question for the finished game. The underlying idea already travels: authorship can mean deciding what belongs beside what.

### A battery failure has been given a harmless direction

Lithium-metal solid-state batteries promise much greater energy density, but thin lithium-filled cracks can cross the solid electrolyte and short the cell. A team from Stanford, SLAC, Arizona State, Kyungpook National University, and Waterloo reports a mechanical way to redirect the danger. In a [*Nature* paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10734-x), biaxial compression made dendrites propagate perpendicular to the electric field instead of across the electrolyte. The cells accumulated many internal dendrites without immediately short-circuiting, even under extreme test conditions.

The work also offers direct evidence in a long-running argument about where dendrites begin. X-ray measurements and post-test imaging found isolated lithium deposits at pores and grain-boundary junctions inside the ceramic. The researchers used a heated shape-memory-alloy ring to apply the squeeze; [Stanford’s explanation](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/08/solid-state-batteries-compression-short-circuiting) says cells continued for thousands of cycles while horizontal dendrites formed.

This is still a laboratory strategy for one garnet electrolyte, not a production battery architecture. Its larger engineering lesson is sturdier. Reliability does not always require abolishing failure. When a crack, flood, or packet loss cannot yet be prevented, geometry can give it somewhere less destructive to go. The battery survives because the defect is permitted to exist in the wrong direction.

### A silent film is a score-shaped opening

Washington State University musicians will perform newly commissioned scores for classic films at the Kenworthy Performing Arts Centre’s Silent Film Festival on September 2 and 9. Among the new work is composition student Tristan Donaldson’s score for Charlie Chaplin’s *The Immigrant*, according to [WSU’s August 28 announcement](https://news.wsu.edu/news/2026/08/28/wsu-musicians-help-silent-films-find-a-new-voice/). The Kenworthy is presenting three Chaplin shorts on the second evening, all written and directed by Chaplin between 1917 and 1921, with the music performed live.

Calling the films silent hides their original condition. Early screenings commonly had accompaniment by a pianist, ensemble, or orchestra, and the music could change from venue to venue. The Library of Congress preserves more than 3,000 [scores and arrangements made for silent-film accompaniment between 1904 and 1927](https://www.loc.gov/collections/silent-film-scores-and-arrangements/about-this-collection/). A surviving print is therefore only part of the historical event.

A new score is not restoration in the narrow sense. It cannot recover the music of one vanished night. It restores variability itself: the film becomes a live collaboration again, open to timing, emphasis, and the acoustics of a particular room. Preservation sometimes means keeping a work unfinished enough for another artist to enter.

## Deep sounding: the hour electricity wanted to be used

At noon on a windy Sunday, a washing machine can become grid infrastructure.

This sounds like a joke produced by overextended climate policy. It follows from the most basic fact about an electrical system: supply and demand must remain balanced. A kilowatt-hour is measured as if it were a uniform quantity, but its usefulness depends on where and when it appears. Electricity beside a hungry motor is valuable. The same electricity trapped behind a congested transmission line, at an hour when nearby demand is already satisfied, may be worth nothing. It can even carry a negative wholesale price because somebody must incur a cost to stop producing it or find somewhere for it to go.

Households rarely see that variation. A retail tariff usually compresses time, location, network charges, policy levies, and hedging into one comprehensible rate. That average is convenient. It also tells a person in a constrained, wind-rich region to conserve at the very moment the physical system would benefit from more demand.

A new working paper asks what happens when the signal is repaired.

Diego Lopez Garcia, Robert Metcalfe, Andrew Schein, and Yixin Sun worked with Octopus Energy on randomized “demand turn-up” events involving roughly 120,000 eligible residential customers across Great Britain and Spain. Customers received notice about a day ahead of periods when renewable electricity was abundant. Different groups were offered a half-price window, free electricity, a negative price that paid them to consume, or no incentive. The events lasted one hour in Great Britain and two in Spain.

People moved.

In Great Britain, a 50 percent discount increased hourly consumption by about 14 percent relative to the control group. Free electricity increased it by 31 percent. Paying customers five or fifteen pence per kilowatt-hour on top of making electricity free produced little additional response. In Spain, the corresponding increases were about 6 percent for half price and 13 percent for free. A related opt-in trial with Northern Powergrid in northern England recorded an increase of roughly 105 percent among participants during free periods. The estimates and experimental design appear in the authors’ [working paper](https://rmetcalfe.net/LopezMetcalfeScheinSun_DemandSideAlternativeRenewableCurtailment.pdf) and [interactive summary](https://rmetcalfe.net/demand-turnup.html).

Zero did most of the work.

That result is more revealing than a smooth demand curve would have been. A household has a finite inventory of flexible tasks: charge the car, run the dishwasher, heat the water, perhaps cool or warm the rooms. Once those loads have been pulled into the window, a more negative price cannot conjure another electric vehicle. Attention is finite too. “Free this hour” is a clean instruction; a small payment layered onto a bill is arithmetic.

The response varied with equipment and circumstance. Customers with electric vehicles and time-of-use tariffs reacted most strongly, which makes physical sense: a car battery can accept a large amount of energy and charging can often move without reducing anyone’s comfort. British households mostly shifted consumption from adjacent hours during the free treatment, while the Spanish trial found more genuinely new demand. Those differences caution against treating an elasticity measured in one market as a law of nature. Appliances, climate, tariffs, event length, and the people who participate all change what can move.

The experiment is interesting because it reverses a familiar moral vocabulary. For most of the fossil-fuel era, using less energy was a reliable proxy for causing less harm. Fuel was scarce, combustion produced pollution, and peak demand called expensive plants into service. “Turn it off” compressed a complicated system into good household advice.

Wind and sunlight do not repeal scarcity. They make it lumpy.

When output exceeds local demand or transmission capacity, system operators curtail generation. A wind turbine is turned down or a solar plant is disconnected even though another unit of output would require almost no extra fuel. Storage and new transmission can move that surplus through time and space, but neither is unlimited or free. Flexible demand moves useful work toward the energy instead.

That distinction matters. Running an empty dryer for sport would absorb electricity, but it would destroy value elsewhere through machine wear. Charging a vehicle earlier performs work that had to be done later. Heating a well-insulated water tank stores energy in temperature. Freezing a warehouse a little more deeply stores cold. A data center may shift a deferrable computation. These loads are not batteries in the literal sense; they store completed tasks or thermal states. The grid only cares that demand arrived when supply needed it.

Price is supposed to coordinate this without a central planner knowing which task can move. The problem is that the retail price often describes the national average rather than the local margin. The authors frame the economic opportunity as a wedge between the rate consumers face and the near-zero local cost of generation that would otherwise be curtailed. Remove the wedge for an hour and households reveal some of their flexibility.

Market design determines who receives the benefit. Great Britain compensates some generators when they are instructed to curtail, while Spain’s arrangements differ. Levies charged per kilowatt-hour can also remain on a bill when the underlying energy is nearly worthless, muting the signal. In a [welfare analysis published by the Centre for Net Zero](https://www.centrefornetzero.org/news/lessons-from-gb-and-spain-on-the-economics-of-demand-turn-up), the researchers estimate that offering Scotland’s 2.55 million households a discount on the wholesale-energy portion during one year of curtailment could have produced £18.2 million in consumer surplus and £9.5 million in public savings. Those figures are modelled counterfactuals, not money already saved.

The limits are as important as the promise.

This is a working paper produced with an energy retailer and tested in two European markets. A day-ahead email is not the same thing as an automated grid service. People without an EV, heat pump, smart appliance, or control over their home have less load to offer. If the largest rewards flow to households that already own flexible electric equipment, a program advertised as universally cheap power may widen an equipment gap. Paying for consumption when generation was not actually constrained would simply subsidize extra use. And the authors calculate that residential participation alone cannot absorb many gigawatt-scale surpluses; storage, transmission, industrial loads, and some curtailment remain necessary.

There is also a human-factors trap. A perfectly granular price can become a second job. No one wants to watch a grid dashboard before making tea. The practical endpoint is likely automation with boundaries set by the resident: have the car ready by seven, keep the water above this temperature, never run the machine at night. Software can then respond to a local price while the person responds to life.

This creates a peculiar design obligation. The automation should make energy timing invisible without making its consequences illegible. A household needs to know why a device moved, how much was saved, and which comforts were protected. Otherwise a flexible grid becomes another system that acts upon people behind an interface they cannot question.

The deeper change is conceptual. Efficiency is often defined as less input for the same output. In a variable system, timing belongs in the denominator. The efficient dishwasher is not only the one that uses fewer kilowatt-hours; it is the one that can wait for an hour when those kilowatt-hours would otherwise be discarded. The efficient building can preheat without becoming uncomfortable. The efficient program knows which computation is urgent and which can follow the weather.

Software engineers will recognize the shape of the problem. Computing systems schedule background work when processors are idle, route traffic around congestion, and use queues to separate when a request arrives from when it must finish. Cheap renewable hours are slack in a physical network. Flexible loads are jobs with deadlines. A flat tariff is a scheduler that has thrown away the clock.

The analogy has a boundary. Electricity serves bodies, homes, and unequal institutions, not interchangeable processes. A delayed batch job does not get cold. An elderly resident may. Any scheduler worthy of the name must treat comfort, accessibility, and consent as hard constraints rather than pricing them as friction.

Still, the first principle survives: conservation is a strategy for matching demand to scarce supply, not a commandment against use. When clean electricity is locally abundant and perishable, preserving value can mean turning something on.

The grid of the future will need restraint. It will also need appetite with a clock.
