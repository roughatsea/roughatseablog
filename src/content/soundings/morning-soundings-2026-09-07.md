A specimen is often something science has stopped. A cell is broken open, a material is pulled to failure, a planet is photographed once. The resulting measurement may be exact, yet still miss the behavior that made the subject interesting.

Today’s three signals trade the still image for a sequence. Living cells release samples of their own activity. Knitted fabric holds several answers to the same force. A geometric wave appears around Saturn because professionals and amateurs kept returning to the same world.

## 1. Living cells can mail out their transcriptomes

RNA sequencing usually begins with lysis: break the cell, recover its RNA, read the genes it was expressing at that moment. The measurement is rich and terminal. Another dish can provide the next time point, but it cannot provide the future of the same population.

Researchers at the Broad Institute, MIT, Boston Children’s Hospital, and collaborating institutions have built a different arrangement. Their [study, published online in *Cell* on September 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42679822/), engineers mammalian cells to produce virus-like particles that package some of the cells’ messenger RNA and bud into the surrounding medium. Scientists can sample that medium repeatedly while the cells remain intact.

The underlying move is elegant. Retroviruses already possess structural proteins that gather RNA, wrap it in a protein shell, and cross a cell membrane. The team repurposed that machinery as an export channel. In the reported experiments, repeated samples tracked changing gene expression during inflammatory stimulation of primary-cell spheroids and multi-day stem-cell differentiation. Tagged particle components also separated the reports of different cell types in a co-culture. In an enclosed organ-on-chip system, the method followed endothelial-cell programs during the formation of blood-vessel networks.

This is population-level reporting. It does not yet trace one identified cell through its life, and engineering the export machinery may itself alter the system being observed. The researchers say they are working toward single-cell use. Within those limits, the conceptual gain is large: time becomes part of the assay rather than a series reconstructed from sacrificed stand-ins.

## 2. A knitted surface can remember which way it moved

Ordinary elastic cloth returns toward one preferred shape when the load is removed. A light switch behaves differently. It can rest in either of two configurations, and a push carries it across the energetic barrier between them. Engineers call that property multistability.

In [*Advanced Functional Materials*](https://advanced.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/adfm.76385), Kausalya Mahadevan and colleagues show that weft knitting can give fabric the same kind of mechanical memory. Different yarns are plated onto opposite faces of dense knitted sheets. Their unequal residual stresses make the textile curl into a shell. By arranging horizontal and vertical stripes, the researchers produced surfaces that snap between stable three-dimensional forms.

The useful unit here is neither a rigid hinge nor an added electronic memory cell. It is the textile’s geometry. The team modeled the sheet as a continuum, found regions of the design space that should be multistable, and knitted conductive yarn into the structures. The resulting cloth switches supplied both an electrical change and the haptic click of snap-through. Demonstrations included wearable step counters, an illuminated shell, and a lamp whose reconfiguration controlled different lights.

The work also crossed the laboratory-gallery boundary before the paper appeared. A Harvard ArtLab installation presented responsive wall hangings and suspended textile forms, placing touch, color, and mechanics in the same objects. [Harvard’s account of the exhibition](https://seas.harvard.edu/news/fabric-holds-its-shape) makes the lineage visible: textile craft contributed more than a convenient fabrication method. Knitting supplied a mature vocabulary of loops, tension, color, and bodily interaction that mechanics could learn to calculate. The prototypes do not establish durability, washability, or economical production at scale. They do show that a familiar craft can store state without ceasing to feel like cloth.

## 3. Saturn has begun drawing a decagon

Saturn’s north pole has carried a hexagonal jet-stream pattern for more than four decades. Its south pole now has a different polygon: a ten-sided atmospheric wave that seems to have formed recently.

The finding depended on recurrence. NASA’s Outer Planet Atmospheres Legacy program photographs the outer planets every year. When Saturn’s changing seasons brought its south pole back into view, planetary observers Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger noticed a faint undulation in ground-based images from 2024; it appeared more strongly in 2025. Researchers then returned to Hubble’s archive and found subtler evidence reaching back to 2023. Cassini images from 2004 through 2017 showed no comparable long-lived feature.

The [Hubble team reports](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/nasas-hubble-tracks-new-decagon-encircling-saturns-south-pole/) that the wave occupies a powerful jet stream and appears at slightly different positions in different wavelengths, evidence that it extends through several atmospheric layers. The peer-reviewed [*Science Advances* paper](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aee4251) describes the structure; neither the paper nor NASA’s account yet establishes what created it or how long it will persist. Continued Hubble and Webb observations, along with atmospheric models, may show whether it settles into a durable pattern or dissolves.

The collaboration is as interesting as the geometry. Amateur observers supplied frequent ground-based images through the Planetary Virtual Observatory Laboratory; Hubble supplied sharper, multi-wavelength views; an annual program supplied the baseline needed to distinguish a new structure from an old landmark. No single image contained the discovery. The evidence appeared when several readers compared the same atmosphere across time.

Cells, cloth, and planets change at radically different scales, but each signal points to the same discipline: preserve the sequence. A system’s history is not noise around its true state. Often, the passage from one state to another is the thing being measured.
