A billiard ball must touch one cushion before reaching a target. Where should the ball strike the cushion if you want the shortest possible route?

Trying points one by one will find an approximation. Geometry finds the exact point without searching.

The trick is to reflect the target across the cushion. The required path still looks bent on the real table, but its reflected copy becomes a straight segment. Because a straight segment is the shortest route between two points, the reflected picture solves the optimization problem at once.

The same construction explains the familiar law of reflection: the angle at which a ray arrives equals the angle at which the ray leaves.

## Unfold the bounce

Let the horizontal line $L$ be a mirror. Points $A$ and $B$ lie on the same side of $L$. A route begins at $A$, touches the mirror at a point $P$, and ends at $B$. Its total length is

$$
D(P)=AP+PB,
$$

where $AP$ and $PB$ denote the lengths of the two segments.

Reflect $B$ across $L$ to a point $B'$. Reflection preserves distance from every point on the mirror, so

$$
PB=PB'.
$$

The broken route therefore satisfies

$$
D(P)=AP+PB'.
$$

Now apply the triangle inequality to the three points $A$, $P$, and $B'$:

$$
AP+PB'\geq AB'.
$$

Equality occurs exactly when $A$, $P$, and $B'$ lie on one straight segment, with $P$ between the other two points. The shortest physical route $A\to P\to B$ is therefore found by drawing the straight line from $A$ to $B'$ and recording where that line crosses the mirror.

## Why the two angles match

At the minimizing point, the straight line from $A$ to $B'$ crosses the mirror at $P$. Reflect the segment $PB'$ back across the mirror. The reflected segment is $PB$.

Reflection preserves angles. The angle between $PB'$ and the mirror equals the angle between $PB$ and the mirror. Because $AP$ and $PB'$ form one straight line, the angle made by $AP$ on one side of the normal matches the reflected angle made by $PB$ on the other side.

Optics usually measures both angles from the **normal**, the line perpendicular to the mirror at $P$. If $\theta_i$ is the incoming angle and $\theta_r$ is the outgoing angle, the law of reflection is

$$
\theta_i=\theta_r.
$$

Measuring from the mirror instead would produce the same equality because each angle from the mirror is the complement of its angle from the normal.

## Worked example: find the exact bounce point

Place the mirror on the $x$-axis,

$$
L=\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb{R}\}.
$$

Let the starting point be $A=(2,4)$ and the target be $B=(8,3)$. We will find the point $P=(p,0)$ that minimizes $AP+PB$.

**Step 1: reflect the target.** Reflecting across the $x$-axis changes the sign of the vertical coordinate, so

$$
B'=(8,-3).
$$

**Step 2: write the straight segment from $A$ to $B'$.** A point on that segment has the form

$$
A+t(B'-A)
=(2,4)+t(6,-7),
$$

where $t$ runs from $0$ at $A$ to $1$ at $B'$.

**Step 3: find where the segment reaches the mirror.** The vertical coordinate is

$$
4-7t.
$$

At the mirror, that coordinate equals $0$:

$$
4-7t=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t=\frac47.
$$

The horizontal coordinate at the same value of $t$ is

$$
p=2+6\left(\frac47\right)
=2+\frac{24}{7}
=\frac{38}{7}.
$$

Thus the exact bounce point is

$$
P=\left(\frac{38}{7},0\right).
$$

**Step 4: calculate the minimum length.** The unfolded route is the straight segment $AB'$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB'
&=\sqrt{(8-2)^2+(-3-4)^2}\\
&=\sqrt{6^2+(-7)^2}\\
&=\sqrt{36+49}\\
&=\sqrt{85}\\
&\approx 9.220.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 5: check the angles independently.** From $A$ to $P$, the horizontal change is

$$
\frac{38}{7}-2=\frac{24}{7},
$$

while the vertical change has magnitude $4$. The tangent of the angle from the vertical normal is therefore

$$
\tan\theta_i=\frac{24/7}{4}=\frac67.
$$

From $P$ to $B$, the horizontal change is

$$
8-\frac{38}{7}=\frac{18}{7},
$$

while the vertical change is $3$. Hence

$$
\tan\theta_r=\frac{18/7}{3}=\frac67.
$$

Both acute angles lie between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, so equal tangents give

$$
\theta_i=\theta_r\approx 40.60^\circ.
$$

The reflection construction, the minimum-length calculation, and the angle check all select the same point.

## A principle with a long optical history

The equal-angle rule predates the shortest-path proof. The [Oxford Classical Dictionary's survey of catoptrics](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548509153) describes an ancient Greek tradition that included a *Catoptrica* attributed to Euclid, although the attribution is disputed, and later treatments associated with Hero of Alexandria and Ptolemy.

Hero's distinctive contribution was to connect reflection with a minimum. A problem in Hero's *Catoptrica*, usually dated to the first century CE, shows that the equal-angle route has the shortest length among paths that touch a plane mirror. The [University of St Andrews account of the minimal-path problem](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/MinimalPaths2/) presents the same construction used in this lesson.

That history should not be compressed into “Hero discovered reflection.” Earlier writers knew the equal-angle rule, and Ptolemy's optical work included experimental measurements. Hero supplied a powerful way to derive the rule from an extremal path.

In the mid-seventeenth century, Pierre de Fermat proposed a broader principle: a light ray follows a path whose travel time is stationary under small changes. In the flat-mirror problem, light stays in one medium and moves at constant speed, so minimizing time is the same as minimizing distance. The [Feynman Lectures' chapter on least time](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_26.html) works from that optical principle toward reflection and refraction.

“Least time” is a useful historical name, but **stationary time** is the more general modern statement. In some optical systems, the physical path need not be the globally shortest path. The one-reflection problem on a flat mirror really does have the unique minimum proved above.

## Where unfolding earns its keep

The construction is useful whenever a path reflects from a flat boundary.

A billiards player can reflect the target across a cushion, aim along the resulting straight line, and recover the intended contact point on the real cushion. The model assumes an ideal point ball, a perfectly elastic collision, no spin, and no energy loss. Real tables violate those assumptions, but the unfolded line remains the geometric starting point.

Geometric optics uses the same equal-angle law for plane mirrors. The [OpenStax account of reflection](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-2-the-law-of-reflection) defines the incoming and reflected angles relative to the surface normal and applies the rule to mirror images.

Computer graphics turns the construction into an algorithmic operation. A ray tracer follows a viewing ray to a surface, computes a reflected direction from the surface normal, and traces the new ray through the scene. [NVIDIA's ray-tracing overview](https://developer.nvidia.com/discover/ray-tracing) describes ray tracing as a technique used in film, television, and real-time video games. Production renderers also model roughness, scattering, absorption, and many bounces; the perfect mirror is one exact case inside a larger rendering problem.

Unfolding extends beyond a single bounce. A rectangular billiard path with several cushion strikes can be straightened by reflecting the entire table after each strike. The ball's complicated route in the original table becomes one line through a tiled collection of reflected tables. That idea opens a door from elementary geometry into the mathematical study of billiards and dynamical systems.

## Try the construction

### Problem 1

The mirror is the $x$-axis. Let $A=(1,3)$ and $B=(5,2)$. Find the bounce point $P$ that minimizes $AP+PB$, and find the minimum length.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the solution</summary>

Reflect $B=(5,2)$ across the $x$-axis:

$$
B'=(5,-2).
$$

Parameterize the segment from $A$ to $B'$:

$$
(1,3)+t(4,-5).
$$

Its vertical coordinate reaches $0$ when

$$
3-5t=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t=\frac35.
$$

The horizontal coordinate is then

$$
1+4\left(\frac35\right)
=1+\frac{12}{5}
=\frac{17}{5}.
$$

Therefore

$$
P=\left(\frac{17}{5},0\right).
$$

The minimum length is the straight distance from $A$ to $B'$:

$$
AB'=\sqrt{(5-1)^2+(-2-3)^2}
=\sqrt{4^2+(-5)^2}
=\sqrt{41}.
$$

</details>

### Problem 2

Two points are $A=(0,2)$ and $B=(6,2)$ above the mirror $y=0$. Without using calculus, find the shortest route from $A$ to the mirror and then to $B$. Explain why the bounce point lies halfway between the endpoints.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the solution</summary>

Reflect $B=(6,2)$ to

$$
B'=(6,-2).
$$

The line from $A=(0,2)$ to $B'=(6,-2)$ changes its vertical coordinate from $2$ to $-2$. It reaches $0$ halfway along the segment. Halfway between the horizontal coordinates $0$ and $6$ is $3$, so

$$
P=(3,0).
$$

The minimum length is

$$
AB'=\sqrt{(6-0)^2+(-2-2)^2}
=\sqrt{36+16}
=\sqrt{52}
=2\sqrt{13}.
$$

The equal heights of $A$ and $B$ make the reflected picture symmetric around the vertical line $x=3$. That symmetry places the bounce point halfway between them.

</details>

## The idea to keep

Reflect the target across a flat boundary. Every broken path keeps the same length after unfolding, but only the minimizing path becomes a straight segment. Straightness gives the shortest route, and folding the segment back makes the incoming and outgoing angles equal.
