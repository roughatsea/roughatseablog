Two ropes measure 1,071 centimeters and 462 centimeters. You want to cut both ropes into pieces of one common length, with no waste. What is the longest possible piece?

Testing every factor would work, eventually. A faster route begins with a different question: how many 462-centimeter pieces fit inside 1,071 centimeters, and what remains?

$$
1071=2\cdot462+147.
$$

Any length that divides both original ropes must divide the leftover 147 centimeters too. The pair $1071,462$ can therefore be replaced by the smaller pair $462,147$ without changing the answer. Repeat that replacement, and the numbers collapse.

## The rule

Let $a$ and $b$ be positive integers with $a\ge b$. Whole-number division writes $a$ in exactly one form

$$
a=qb+r,
$$

where $q$ is a nonnegative integer called the **quotient**, and the **remainder** $r$ satisfies

$$
0\le r<b.
$$

The **greatest common divisor** of $a$ and $b$, written $\gcd(a,b)$, is the largest positive integer that divides both numbers without a remainder.

The Euclidean algorithm rests on one identity:

$$
\boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)}
\qquad\text{when }a=qb+r.
$$

Why can the remainder replace the larger number? Take any common divisor $d$ of $a$ and $b$. Because $d$ divides both terms in $a-qb$, the same $d$ divides

$$
r=a-qb.
$$

Now run the reasoning backward. If $d$ divides $b$ and $r$, then $d$ divides

$$
qb+r=a.
$$

The pairs $(a,b)$ and $(b,r)$ have exactly the same common divisors. Their greatest common divisor must be the same.

If $r$ is not zero, divide again. Each positive remainder is smaller than the divisor immediately before it, so the decreasing sequence cannot continue forever. When a division finally has remainder zero, its divisor is the answer.

## Worked example: 1,071 and 462

Start by dividing the larger number by the smaller:

$$
1071=2\cdot462+147.
$$

The identity gives

$$
\gcd(1071,462)=\gcd(462,147).
$$

Now divide 462 by 147:

$$
462=3\cdot147+21,
$$

so

$$
\gcd(462,147)=\gcd(147,21).
$$

One more division finishes the chain:

$$
147=7\cdot21+0.
$$

The last nonzero remainder is 21. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\gcd(1071,462)=21}.
$$

The answer passes the original test:

$$
1071=51\cdot21,
\qquad
462=22\cdot21.
$$

Both ropes split into 21-centimeter pieces. No longer common length can work, because every common divisor survived each replacement and the final pair, $(147,21)$, has 21 as its largest common divisor.

## What the rectangle proves

The rectangle is more than a picture of the arithmetic. Suppose a square tile has an integer side length that covers an $a$-by-$b$ rectangle exactly. The tile's side must divide both $a$ and $b$. The largest possible square tile therefore has side length $\gcd(a,b)$.

For the 1,071-by-462 rectangle, two 462-by-462 squares leave a 147-by-462 strip. Three 147-by-147 squares leave a 147-by-21 strip. Seven 21-by-21 squares fill that strip. The final square side, 21, is the largest length capable of tiling every earlier region.

The same logic explains a practical habit: reduce a fraction by dividing its numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor. Because $\gcd(1071,462)=21$,

$$
\frac{1071}{462}=\frac{1071\div21}{462\div21}=\frac{51}{22}.
$$

The reduced fraction preserves the ratio while removing every factor shared by the two terms.

## Two ancient records, two mathematical settings

The procedure carries Euclid's name because Book VII, Proposition 2 of the *Elements*, composed around 300 BCE, gives a method for finding the “greatest common measure” of two numbers. [David Joyce's edition of Proposition VII.2](https://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVII/propVII2.html) preserves Euclid's geometric language. A [Mathematical Association of America study of the source](https://old.maa.org/press/periodicals/convergence/euclids-algorithm-for-the-greatest-common-divisor) notes that Euclid described numbers as lengths and that historians can read the repeated removals as subtraction or as the larger division steps used today.

A related greatest-common-divisor procedure survives in China's *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*. The text's authors and exact date are unknown; evidence inside the work suggests compilation shortly after 200 BCE. Its first chapter uses repeated subtraction while reducing fractions. Liu Hui added explanations in a commentary dated 263 CE. The [Mathematical Association of America's history of the *Nine Chapters*](https://old.maa.org/node/1635411) places those rules among calculations for fields, grain exchange, construction, wages, and taxes. The book later served mathematical education connected to China's imperial bureaucracy.

The surviving Greek and Chinese texts embed a similar operation in different projects. Euclid's *Elements* organizes propositions into deductive number theory. The *Nine Chapters* organizes worked rules around administrative and economic problems. The records do not justify a story in which one named person invented every version of the idea.

The English word **algorithm** has a separate lineage. It comes through medieval Latin versions of the name al-Khwārizmī, the ninth-century scholar whose writings helped transmit Hindu-Arabic arithmetic. The [MacTutor biography of al-Khwārizmī](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Al-Khwarizmi/) traces that word history. “Euclidean algorithm” therefore joins a Greek attribution to a word whose route into European languages runs through a Persian scholar working in Abbasid Baghdad.

## Where the same remainder chain works now

Computers use greatest common divisors to reduce exact fractions, normalize ratios, and decide whether two integers are **coprime**, meaning that their greatest common divisor is 1.

An extended version of the algorithm tracks how each remainder was assembled from the original numbers. That bookkeeping can find a **modular inverse**: a number that reverses multiplication when only remainders modulo a fixed integer matter. The connection is not historical decoration. Appendix B.1 of the current [National Institute of Standards and Technology Digital Signature Standard](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.FIPS.186-5) gives a reference inverse routine built from successive quotients and remainders. The standard uses such inverses in public-key signature procedures.

The site's lesson on [RSA encryption](/reckonings/daily-reckoning-2026-09-11/) shows why an inverse exponent can unlock a message. The Euclidean algorithm supplies the arithmetic needed to find inverses of that kind when they exist.

## Try the chain yourself

### Problem 1

Find $\gcd(252,198)$ with the Euclidean algorithm.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the solution</summary>

Divide repeatedly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
252&=1\cdot198+54,\\
198&=3\cdot54+36,\\
54&=1\cdot36+18,\\
36&=2\cdot18+0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last nonzero remainder is 18, so

$$
\gcd(252,198)=18.
$$

As a check, $252=14\cdot18$ and $198=11\cdot18$.

</details>

### Problem 2

What is the side length of the largest integer-sided square that can tile a 391-by-299 rectangle? How many of those largest squares cover the rectangle?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the solution</summary>

The square's side is $\gcd(391,299)$. Compute it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
391&=1\cdot299+92,\\
299&=3\cdot92+23,\\
92&=4\cdot23+0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The largest square has side length 23. The rectangle is

$$
391\div23=17
$$

squares long and

$$
299\div23=13
$$

squares wide. The total number is

$$
17\cdot13=221.
$$

</details>

## The idea to keep

Replacing $(a,b)$ by $(b,r)$ keeps every common divisor and makes the numbers smaller. The last nonzero remainder is therefore the greatest common divisor.
