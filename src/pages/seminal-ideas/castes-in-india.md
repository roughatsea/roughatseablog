## Orientation

A group can share a profession without requiring its members’ children to marry one another. What additional rules turn an association into a hereditary enclosure?

That question drives B. R. Ambedkar’s *Castes in India*. His early essay approaches caste through the work needed to preserve a boundary across generations. Naming a group’s occupation or describing its customs does not yet explain why membership becomes closed. Ambedkar concentrates on marriage: who may marry whom, what happens when a spouse dies, and how exclusion by one group changes the choices available to others.

Ambedkar later became a leading opponent of caste oppression and chaired the committee that drafted India’s Constitution. This paper belongs to his student years. Its vocabulary and speculative method should be read in that setting rather than treated as a complete statement of his later thought. [Context and edition.](#sources)

**The lasting question:** Which restrictions on actual people make an apparently self-perpetuating social order possible?

**The essential distinction:** An account of how a practice helps maintain a boundary is not yet evidence that the practice originally arose for that purpose. Ambedkar’s essay offers a powerful way to ask about maintenance and an ambitious historical explanation whose evidential gaps remain visible.

## Core lesson

### The argument in six moves

This is an editorial reconstruction of the paper:

1. Descriptions of caste collect features such as occupation, inherited membership, shared meals, and religious purity. We need to identify what makes a group closed within a larger social system.
2. Ambedkar selects endogamy, the restriction of marriage to members of a specified group, as the decisive mechanism.
3. He argues that caste endogamy was placed over older rules requiring marriage outside particular kin groups. Maintaining both sets of restrictions makes the pool of permitted partners narrower.
4. Death and unequal opportunities for remarriage create difficulties for a closed marriage circle. Ambedkar reads coercive customs affecting widows and girls as ways of managing those difficulties, with men’s interests receiving greater protection.
5. He proposes that a prestigious priestly class first enclosed itself and that other groups followed through imitation. Other people became enclosed because existing groups excluded them.
6. A caste therefore needs to be studied through its relations with other castes. Its persistence cannot be explained by describing an isolated community or crediting one lawgiver with inventing the whole arrangement.

The steps have different strengths. A conceptual distinction, a hypothetical mechanism, an observation about power, and a historical conjecture do not become equally established because they appear in one argument. [¶¶6–17, 19–25, 31–45.](#sources)

### Endogamy and exogamy

**Endogamy** means marrying within a specified group. **Exogamy** means marrying outside a specified group. They can coexist because the groups specified are different. A person might be required to marry within a caste while being forbidden to marry within a narrower lineage or kin circle. [¶¶10–15.](#sources)

**Editorial illustration:** Imagine a community containing several lineages. A rule permits marriage only within the community; another prohibits marriage within one’s own lineage. A permitted partner must satisfy both conditions. Saying merely that the community “marries within” leaves an important part of its marriage rules unexplained.

Ambedkar treats older exogamous rules as the background against which caste endogamy needs explanation. He discusses *gotra*, an inherited lineage designation, and prohibitions involving kinship. His sweeping assertions about their prevalence are claims of the essay; they should not be converted into a uniform description of every Indian community, past or present. [¶¶13–14.](#sources)

Why privilege marriage over occupation? Occupations exist in many societies. To explain occupational caste, Ambedkar says, we must explain how an occupational group loses openness and becomes hereditary. His compact formulation is:

> “A Caste is an Enclosed Class.”

—¶31.

Here **class** means a social grouping, which may be economic, intellectual, or otherwise; it does not mean only an income bracket. **Enclosure** names the restriction that makes group membership reproduce itself rather than remain open to changed qualification or association. [¶¶31, 37, 39–40.](#sources)

There is a limit to this selection. Ambedkar’s early model puts endogamy at the center; that does not prove that rank, work, religious authority, violence, or unequal resources are dispensable to a full explanation of caste. Nor is every tendency to marry someone similar sufficient to make a caste. His subject is enforced closure within an interconnected social order.

### Gendered enforcement of a boundary

An injunction to marry only within the group still needs practical enforcement. Ambedkar introduces an imaginary group and asks what happens when a husband or wife dies. The surviving spouse may seek another partner. If suitable partners are unavailable inside the permitted circle, marriage outside it threatens the boundary. [¶¶15–18.](#sources)

His terms **“surplus woman”** and **“surplus man”** refer to a widow or widower relative to the marriage opportunities assumed in this model. They do not establish that someone is excessive as a human being. The vocabulary adopts the standpoint of a group attempting to preserve its enclosure; that standpoint itself needs moral scrutiny.

Ambedkar examines four responses: burning a widow on her husband’s funeral pyre, enforcing permanent widowhood, imposing celibacy on a widower, and supplying a widower with a bride below marriageable age. These are his proposed mechanisms, not recommendations. In ¶29 he condemns the customs he is explaining. [¶¶19–29.](#sources)

The asymmetry does much of the intellectual work. Men possess greater authority, he argues, so the group is less willing to sacrifice their interests. Permanent celibacy also removes a man from family formation and can diminish the group’s numbers. In his account, the boundary is instead maintained by restricting women’s futures and recruiting girls into unequal marriages. [¶¶21–25.](#sources)

**Gendered enforcement**, our organizing label, means distributing the costs of a rule differently according to gender. The stated objective may be the same—preserve marriage within the group—while the remedies protect one set of members and constrain another. Asking only whether a custom promotes group continuity misses who pays for that continuity.

The model is deliberately narrow. It assumes paired heterosexual marriage, rough numerical balance, and strong pressure to marry. Actual marriage opportunities also depend on age, mortality, consent, household arrangements, and changing rules. The paper supplies no demographic dataset establishing that its four responses were necessary or exhaustive. Its analytical usefulness lies in making enforcement and unequal power visible; the universal necessity claim needs additional evidence.

### Means presented as ideals

Ambedkar distinguishes the practical effect of a custom from the elevated language used to honor it. **A means** is something that helps produce an outcome. **An ideal** is something presented as worthy of admiration or pursuit. A practice can perform the first role while being defended in the language of the second. [¶¶27–29.](#sources)

He considers praises of widow-burning and early marriage. An account of perfect devotion or sexual purity may explain why a custom is commended. It does not by itself explain why that custom exists or what social arrangements it helps maintain. Ambedkar proposes that moral celebration can give a coercive practice greater durability.

**Editorial example:** A fictional hereditary association praises members who forgo marriage outside the association as especially devoted. Its praise may be sincerely felt. It also helps preserve inherited membership. To understand the practice, examine both the members’ stated reasons and the effects of rewarding compliance or punishing departure.

Two cautions preserve the distinction. First, identifying a beneficiary does not establish that every participant secretly intended that benefit. Second, showing that a practice maintains an institution does not prove that it originated to do so. Ambedkar moves from function toward origin more readily than the available evidence warrants. A reader can retain his question about consequences while demanding better evidence for that movement.

### Imitation and exclusion

**Imitation** in this essay is the adoption of a prestigious group’s practices. Ambedkar draws on the social theorist Gabriel Tarde to propose that practices spread from admired groups to those in close social contact with them. He argues that Brahmin prestige helped marriage restrictions and associated customs become models for others. This is an attributed historical explanation, not a validated universal law of psychology. [¶¶41–43.](#sources)

**Exclusion** provides a different route to closure. If established groups refuse admission to people expelled from their original group, those people may have little opportunity to join any existing marriage circle. They can become a separate closed group without having sought the prestige of separation. Ambedkar therefore distinguishes groups that closed their own doors from people who found doors closed against them. [¶¶40, 44–45.](#sources)

**Editorial illustration:** In an imaginary society with three marriage circles, two prohibit marriage with the third. Even if the third welcomes outside partners, its members cannot obtain those partners by unilateral willingness. Their restricted choices may resemble a preference for separation when they are actually consequences of other people’s refusals.

This is a relational explanation: one group’s rule changes another group’s possibilities. It also limits an account based only on individual prejudice. Removing one person’s hostility may leave the pattern of exclusion intact if everyone else’s rules continue to block admission.

Ambedkar calls the process mechanical and at points inevitable. The illustration establishes a constraint under specified conditions. It does not prove that every expelled group must become a new caste: other arrangements, coalitions, or changes in admission rules remain conceivable. The historical question is which alternatives were actually available.

### Mechanism and historical origin

A **mechanism** explains how interacting rules and actions produce an outcome. An **origin account** explains when and how that arrangement first developed. A successful mechanism can suggest an origin hypothesis, but the hypothesis still needs historical evidence.

Ambedkar argues that the Brahmin class first established enclosure, and that the distribution of customs among other groups supports their later imitation. He explicitly admits that he cannot answer the initial-origin question directly. His evidence is an inferred relationship between prestige, strict observance, and the pattern of diffusion. [¶¶32, 39, 43.](#sources)

There is a possible circle: if other groups’ practices are called derivative because Brahmins are assumed to be the original model, the derivative character cannot then independently prove the original model. Chronology and comparative records would need to do that work. Likewise, strict present observance does not by itself establish historical priority.

His rejection of a single lawgiver’s invention remains valuable. He argues that Manu, the figure associated with an influential legal-religious code, upheld and codified an existing arrangement rather than creating an entire society by decree. He also distinguishes a priestly group’s initial self-enclosure from its ability to impose the whole system on everyone else. The essay should not be reduced to the claim that one person or group consciously designed every caste. [¶¶33–38.](#sources)

The practical takeaway is a method of inquiry: identify the boundary, inspect its enforcement, distinguish voluntary imitation from constrained separation, and ask what evidence establishes the history. Those questions can survive criticism of the paper’s particular answers.

## Deep exploration

### A map of the paper

| 26–30 | Applies the model to customs and distinguishes function from idealization | Has maintenance been confused with original purpose? |
| 31–40 | Proposes initial enclosure and rejects single-author explanations of the system | What dates and records would establish the proposed sequence? |
| 41–46 | Explains diffusion through imitation and exclusion | How can the two processes be distinguished in particular cases? |
| 47 | Offers the theory as revisable | What evidence would change the explanation? |

The early four-class schema in ¶39 belongs to Ambedkar’s reconstruction. It should not be silently equated with the many particular caste groups that the essay seeks to explain. Its historical sequence is an argument to investigate, not a chronology established by this lesson.

### A powerful diagnosis in inherited categories

Ambedkar challenges racial explanations of caste and the mistake of treating occupational descriptions as explanations. Yet ¶5 adopts the ethnological vocabulary of his period, sorting populations into large inherited categories and asserting a deep cultural unity. Paragraphs 11 and 13 also make sweeping comparisons between societies and stages of civilization.

A careful reading should keep both facts in view. His criticism of racial reduction does not make every category he uses adequate. His argument also depends on the premise that caste divides an already culturally unified population. If cultural relationships were more varied, the proposed history would need revision. That possibility does not make enforced closure harmless; it changes what must be explained about its development.

This is an editorial criticism of the paper’s premises. Establishing alternative histories for particular regions and communities requires historical scholarship beyond the sources consulted here. The lesson does not substitute a new all-India origin story for the one it questions.

### Does a focus on marriage explain enough?

**Objection:** A marriage rule does not explain the unequal value assigned to groups, control of work and resources, or the coercion that makes the rule effective. Treating endogamy as the essence of caste risks turning a many-sided hierarchy into a single institution.

**Reply:** Ambedkar’s restricted question is productive: it asks what makes membership persist across generations. His focus directs attention to family arrangements often treated as private or secondary. But a central reproductive mechanism need not be a sufficient explanation of the entire social order. We can ask how marriage rules interact with rank, material dependence, and authority rather than requiring one feature to do all the explanatory work.

The distinction also prevents careless analogy. Professional credentialing, a friendship circle, and hereditary caste may each exclude people, but their admission rules, penalties, historical organization, and consequences differ. Use an analogy to isolate a mechanism; do not use it to erase those differences.

### The language of “surplus” deserves examination

**Objection:** Even when the author condemns coercion, describing widows as a demographic problem can reproduce the perspective that makes their independent lives disappear. Why assume that remarriage opportunities must be balanced by regulating women rather than revising the boundary itself?

**Reply:** That pressure is part of what makes the model revealing: the group preserves its rule by treating members’ futures as variables to manage. A reader should reverse the standpoint and ask whose wishes, consent, and livelihood the account leaves unexamined. The boundary is a social requirement, not a natural fact that makes a widow’s existence a problem.

Durba Mitra’s 2021 article, in its publicly accessible abstract, examines how the concept of endogamy developed within colonial social thought and how its use could naturalize control of female sexuality. The abstract specifically engages Ambedkar’s formulation. It supplies a reason to scrutinize the analytical category as well as the practices it describes. Only the abstract and bibliographic record were consulted here; no uninspected full-article argument is attributed to Mitra. [Contextual scholarship.](#sources)

### What would count as evidence?

Several claims need different tests:

| Kind of claim | Example | Evidence or reasoning required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conceptual | Endogamy and exogamy can coexist at different group levels | Specify the groups and check whether the rules are consistent |
| Mechanistic | Closing marriage circles can restrict others’ options | Show the rules, sanctions, actual alternatives, and resulting constraints |
| Historical | A prestigious group first adopted customs that others copied | Establish chronology, transmission, variation, and rival explanations |
| Empirical | A particular custom maintained closure in a particular population | Examine practice as well as prescription, with appropriate records and comparisons |
| Ethical | Preserving a group does not justify coercing its members | Give an argument about persons, consent, power, and permissible obligations |

The ethical conclusion does not wait for a complete origin theory. A custom can be condemned for coercing people even when historians disagree about how it began. Conversely, moral condemnation does not establish a particular causal history.

### Why study this early work?

Its enduring intellectual contribution, in our evaluation, is to ask how inherited boundaries are actively reproduced and to place control of marriage near the center of that question. It makes the treatment of women part of an account of social structure. It also asks how a group’s exclusionary conduct can constrain people outside it.

Ambedkar’s later public role and the scholarly attention still paid to this paper help explain its place in a catalogue of seminal ideas. They do not establish that this essay caused particular reforms, that all his later positions were already present here, or that its historical propositions have been confirmed. Paragraph 47 explicitly offers the explanation for revision or rejection if it proves untenable.

### Comparisons within the catalogue

These are editorial comparisons of arguments, not claims of direct influence.

- [Tagore on social freedom](/seminal-ideas/nationalism/#social-freedom) warns that political independence can leave social subjection intact. Ambedkar supplies a more focused inquiry into the rules that sustain hereditary enclosure. Read his model beside Tagore’s partly reassuring account of caste’s origins; neither reconstruction should be treated as established history.
- [Wollstonecraft on educated dependence](/seminal-ideas/a-vindication-of-the-rights-of-woman/#educated-dependence) asks how institutions form habits later called natural. Ambedkar asks how institutions preserve a boundary later treated as an inherited given. Both direct attention to the social work concealed by an appearance of inevitability, while examining different institutions and histories.
- [Smith on division of labour](/seminal-ideas/the-wealth-of-nations/#division-of-labour-and-productivity) analyzes specialization. Ambedkar’s question about occupational groups becoming closed adds a different test: can people change their social position, and which rules make membership hereditary? Productive differentiation does not by itself explain enforced enclosure.

### Common distortions to avoid

- **“Endogamy means marrying a close relative.”** It means marrying within a specified group. A simultaneous exogamous rule may prohibit particular kin marriages.
- **“Every preference for a similar partner is caste.”** The paper concerns maintained hereditary closure within a system of groups, not similarity alone.
- **“Ambedkar recommends the practices he lists.”** He examines their proposed function and condemns them; explanation is not endorsement.
- **“A custom’s function proves its original purpose.”** An effect can help explain persistence without establishing origin or conscious intention.
- **“One lawgiver invented the entire system.”** The essay explicitly rejects that account and distinguishes initial enclosure from wider diffusion.
- **“Excluded groups freely chose separation.”** Ambedkar’s second process concerns choices constrained by others’ refusals.
- **“This paper is contemporary scientific proof.”** It is an early theoretical contribution with strong assumptions and limited historical demonstration.

5. What is missing from an occupational description when offered as an explanation of caste?
6. What evidence would test the claim that one group’s customs spread to others through prestige?

### If you read only a little

Use the bracketed paragraph numbers in [Pritchett’s transcription](https://franpritchett.com/00ambedkar/txt_ambedkar_castes.html). They are editorial finding aids specific to that text.

1. **¶¶6–14:** Read the competing definitions and the transition to endogamy and exogamy. Write down what Ambedkar selects as essential and what he treats as secondary.
2. **¶¶15–25:** Follow the imaginary group. List the assumptions before evaluating its proposed responses; mark the unequal treatment of women and men.
3. **¶¶27–29:** Compare moral praise of a practice with Ambedkar’s explanation of what it accomplishes. Ask where function becomes an origin claim.
4. **¶¶31–34:** Read the enclosed-class formulation, the indirect origin argument, and the refusal of a single lawgiver’s invention. These claims belong together.
5. **¶¶40–45:** Separate imitation from exclusion. In ¶44, test the symbolic two-group example before treating it as a complete historical explanation.
6. **¶¶46–47:** Compare the author’s summary with his willingness to revise the theory. Which claims would you retain, modify, or investigate further?

Then read the intervening paragraphs. The entire paper is short enough for a second sitting; its qualifications are easiest to miss when only its compact formulations are remembered.

### Where to go next

Return to [Tagore’s discussion of society’s coercive power](/seminal-ideas/nationalism/#society-can-be-a-machine-too). Compare the social relations each author wants to explain and the evidence each gives for their histories.

For a practical editorial exercise, select a group with an explicit membership rule. Record the rule, who enforces it, what departure costs, and whether apparently voluntary separation is constrained by other groups. Keep this a question about specified mechanisms; do not rename every closed organization a caste. The decisive next step is to ask the affected people what options the rule actually leaves them.
