## Orientation

A school tells a girl that she may join the advanced class. The class was organized for boys. Their future work is treated as important enough to deserve financial support; she must earn her board while keeping up.

This is the structure of an experience Anna Julia Cooper recounts near the end of “The Higher Education of Women.” Permission to sit in the room has not given the students equal conditions for learning. Nor has it given them equal reason to expect that anyone wants what they might become.

Cooper’s essay begins with the broad case for women’s intellectual development. It then refuses the comfort of a general success story. Improvements for some women do not establish what has become possible for Black women. She asks colleges for figures, revisits her own schooling, and ends by calling for money and scholarships.

**The lasting question:** What would an institution have to provide before “you are welcome to try” becomes a credible opportunity?

**The essential tension:** Cooper argues for women’s intrinsic development and public contribution, yet often grounds that contribution in a distinctive feminine capacity for sympathy and in Christian service. The lesson takes her institutional criticism seriously while examining those gendered assumptions.

## Core lesson

### Admission and the terms of education

**Admission is permission to enter an institution. Educational opportunity includes the conditions under which someone can actually undertake its work.** That distinction is our working vocabulary for a pattern Cooper develops.

She opens with a hostile proposal against women’s literacy, attributed to the French writer Silvain Maréchal, then turns to an American college that admitted women while distinguishing a “Ladies’ Course” from a more demanding B.A. course. In her account, women’s participation in the latter exposes the inadequacy of giving an inferior course a sex-specific name. [Pp. 48–50.](#sources)

The episode’s argumentative purpose is larger than celebrating exceptional students. A course label can present a restriction as appropriate provision. Calling something education for women does not settle whether it offers the scope, challenge, or recognition available to men.

Cooper’s irony matters. She depicts anxious authorities expecting catastrophe from an educational experiment, only to find women doing the work. Their alarm becomes evidence about the expectations they brought to the classroom, rather than evidence against the students.

**Editorial example:** Two students are admitted to a technical academy. One is encouraged to study theory, design experiments, and prepare for research. The other is directed toward a short course in operating equipment because the academy assumes that people like her will not need the theory. An admissions count would register two places. It would not show the different futures the courses prepare them to pursue.

That imagined example does not establish that every differentiated program is unjust. Programs can serve different purposes chosen by students. The question is who chooses, what justifies the distinction, and whether a group is assigned a narrower future in advance.

The reasoning reconstructed is:

1. Intellectual capacities develop through opportunities to exercise them.
2. Institutions decide which opportunities to provide and encourage.
3. An institution can admit a group while restricting its curriculum and expected destination.
4. The resulting differences cannot straightforwardly establish the group’s natural limitations.
5. Assessing access therefore requires examining the terms of participation.

Cooper does not present this numbered argument. It reconstructs the connection between her opening college narrative and her later account of girls’ schooling.

**Takeaway:** Ask what someone is permitted to study, what support accompanies it, and what future the institution imagines for that student.

### Educated influence and the worship of power

**Social contribution means what a person’s developed abilities can bring to shared life.** Cooper argues that excluding women impoverishes the society that excludes them.

Her case is not limited to the economic usefulness of graduates. She wants women to reason, express judgments, and act upon the intellectual forces of their time. Religion, literature, science, law, and economic thought should become answerable to needs that a culture organized around domination neglects. [Pp. 50–61.](#sources)

She attacks the treatment of strength as proof of right. In a passage on racial power, she quotes a prediction by Percival Lowell that East Asian peoples will disappear before the advancing West. The prediction belongs to the writer she is criticizing. Cooper ridicules the confidence with which one group converts its ascendancy into a verdict on the future of others.

The distinction is clear even where her historical narrative is sweeping: possessing power does not establish moral superiority. A civilization’s capacities for conquest and accumulation do not settle how well it cares for people.

Cooper assigns women a special corrective role through sympathy and care. **Sympathy here means responsiveness to another person’s suffering or need.** She associates it especially with the feminine side of human life, while associating the pursuit of abstract truth more strongly with the masculine. She says both are needed, both should enter children’s education, and neither should be ranked simply as inferior. Her defense of women’s education nevertheless rests partly on that division.

A difficult episode tests the claim. Cooper reports that reformer Mary A. Livermore described boys mistreating a Chinese laundry worker and expressed little concern because he did not fight back. Cooper objects to this contempt for vulnerability. She then protects her account of feminine sympathy by arguing that a woman’s deeper influence can differ from the harsh opinions she voices. [Pp. 53–56.](#sources)

We should resist making that move invisible. Her objection to the treatment of the worker is morally forceful. Her explanation of Livermore’s deeper character is less secure: if contrary behavior can always be attributed to an inauthentic influence, what could count against the theory?

**Editorial example:** A school recruits women to make its leadership more humane, then assumes that a woman leader must naturally be caring. The expectation can obscure both a woman’s actual conduct and a man’s demonstrated care. Exclusion deserves criticism; inclusion does not remove the need to evaluate individuals.

**Takeaway:** Learn from Cooper’s challenge to domination without treating sex as a reliable measure of anyone’s compassion, reasoning, or fitness to lead.

### Self-development beyond pleasing others

**Self-development is the cultivation of a person’s powers and understanding for a life that is their own.** In Cooper’s argument it is a moral claim, not merely a strategy for becoming more marketable.

She recalls earlier learned women, then answers an objection: if educated women existed before, why expect wider education to transform society now? Exceptional cases do not establish widespread conditions. A few women could attain learning while most encountered ridicule and discouragement. [Pp. 62–66.](#sources)

Her criticism reaches the standard by which women are valued. If their worth is measured only by the pleasure they give others, their own work, aspirations, and duties to themselves disappear. On p. 65 she insists upon an inherent value beyond being an ornament or a source of amusement.

This claim complicates an easy summary of the essay as “educate women so they can serve society better.” Cooper does argue for service. She also disputes the idea that a woman’s life has only a relative value, defined by someone else’s satisfaction.

The essay next confronts the objection that learning might damage women’s chances of marriage. Cooper’s reply gives education several consequences. The ability to earn a livelihood reduces dependence on marriage for physical support. Intellectual life, friendship, literature, and attention to nature enlarge the sources of interest in a life. Affection need not disappear, but it need not bear the entire burden of existence. [Pp. 68–72.](#sources)

Her answer is neither a rejection of marriage nor a modern account of every possible household. She accepts marriage and motherhood as important and cites educated women who deny that their learning makes them unwilling or unfit for those roles. At the same time, she reverses the demand that women diminish themselves to become desirable: men should develop sufficiently to meet educated women’s standards.

**Editorial example:** A student is told to abandon a difficult subject because becoming too independent might make her less appealing to a future partner. The advice treats another person’s anticipated preference as a limit on her intellectual future. Cooper’s argument asks why her growth, rather than that preference, should be put on trial.

The strongest claim does not require proving that every course improves every relationship. A person’s entitlement to develop cannot reasonably depend on a promise that nobody else will find the development inconvenient.

**Takeaway:** Separate “Will this please an observer?” from “What does this person have reason to learn, become, and contribute?”

### The people missing from a progress story

**Uneven progress means that an improvement described at the level of a broad group may leave important parts of that group excluded.** Cooper makes the point by changing the essay’s scale.

After describing the promise of women’s education, she says she cannot end on the high ground of generalities. Her specific concern is the higher education of Black women. She writes to colleges admitting women and asks how many Black women have completed their B.A. course over the institutions’ histories. [Pp. 73–75.](#sources)

She reports twelve at Fisk, five at Oberlin, four at Wilberforce, three each at Ann Arbor and Wellesley, two at Livingstone, one at Atlanta, and none as yet at Howard. These are returned figures in her essay. They are not a current count, a complete national census, or comparable graduation rates.

A rate would require a suitable denominator: how many people enrolled, in which years, under which definitions and conditions? Cooper’s question instead makes a neglected group visible inside an achievement story. Its purpose is to disturb confidence that opportunities enjoyed by some women have become common to all.

She also challenges the interpretation of an apparent gap between Black men’s and women’s educational attainment. Differences in contact with the wider world, public visibility, encouragement, and expectations can shape what observers see. One cannot jump from a visible result to a fixed difference in ability.

**Editorial example:** A university announces that women now make up half its entering class. That figure cannot by itself tell us whether low-income women can afford to remain, whether particular groups are being directed away from demanding programs, or whose achievements become visible. The aggregate may be accurate while leaving those questions unanswered.

The example is editorial and does not report a particular university. Its point is logical: a true broad statement does not answer every question about its members.

Cooper’s move is seminal because it changes what a general claim must account for. The question becomes which women, under which racial and material conditions, with what support.

### Encouragement made material

**Material support means resources that make sustained study possible: money, time, living costs, and relief from incompatible demands.** Cooper’s closing proposal makes this concrete.

Her school recollection is carefully chosen. The school professed to prepare teachers, train candidates for ministry, and offer collegiate instruction. She recalls wanting more intellectual work and being allowed into classes organized around the male ministerial students. When a Greek class was formed, she joined gratefully. [Pp. 76–77.](#sources)

Participation did not erase the contrast. A boy’s intention to study theology could bring him encouragement, exemption from work, and dignity in advance of achievement. A self-supporting girl had to teach during the summer, work after school to meet board bills, and contend with discouragement. When such a girl proposed college, the ambition was treated as nearly absurd. Cooper presents this as experience, not invented fiction.

The institutional pattern matters more than an isolated unkind remark. A recognized male vocation served as a route to resources. Girls’ ambitions lacked an equivalent route. Praising their determination without changing that arrangement would leave the burden where it was.

The argument reconstructed is:

1. Study requires resources as well as willingness.
2. A recognized future role can make resources available before a student has completed training.
3. Girls are asked to demonstrate merit while carrying burdens from which supported boys are relieved.
4. The disparity helps reproduce the scarcity of advanced educational achievement.
5. A remedy must change the allocation of support, rather than merely advise girls to try harder.

Cooper asks for public encouragement, then becomes explicit: raise money and establish scholarships for self-supporting young women to balance the aid available to boys studying theology. Her closing formula calls for more for girls without demanding less for boys. [Pp. 78–79.](#sources)

That formula does not make scarcity disappear. The essay supplies a direction for institution-building, not a complete budget. It does make clear that her remedy goes beyond polite inclusion.

**Takeaway:** Translate a statement of encouragement into the resources and arrangements needed to act on it. Then ask who can actually obtain them.

## Deep exploration

### Two reasons for education, and a tension between them

Cooper offers both an argument from the person’s worth and an argument from shared benefit.

The first begins from a life that has its own aspirations and duties. Intellectual growth cannot be justified only as an improvement in someone’s attractiveness or usefulness to another person. The second asks what families, communities, and public thought lose when women’s powers remain undeveloped.

The arguments can reinforce each other. A person develops abilities through relationships and can use those abilities in service. But they can also pull apart. If education is justified only by expected service, what happens to a student whose interests do not fit the community’s preferred role?

Cooper’s ending privileges a trained Christian woman’s contribution as teacher, homemaker, wife, mother, or even quiet influence. Those roles help her argue against institutions that recognize the male theologian as a worthy investment. They also leave a critical question: must a woman promise a particular kind of service before her learning deserves support?

**Editorial evaluation:** The claim of inherent worth is stronger if it protects development even when the eventual occupation or household differs from the benefactor’s expectation. Duties to others remain real; they should not become an unlimited claim upon the student’s future.

This evaluation extends a tension within the essay. It is not a claim that Cooper supplied a fully secular or unrestricted theory of educational choice.

### Complementary capacities without fixed destinies?

**Gender essentialism is the claim that women and men possess defining characteristics because of their sex.** The label is modern editorial vocabulary. Cooper’s language about feminine sympathy and masculine reason gives us grounds to examine the issue.

She does not simply say that all women feel and all men think. She acknowledges exceptions, rejects a ranking of the two contributions, and wants boys to develop tenderness and girls to develop strength and self-reliance. Those qualifications matter. So do passages that treat feminine sympathy as a deeper truth even when a woman voices contempt.

Three questions need separating:

| Question | Cooper’s approach in this essay | A limit to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Should women receive demanding education? | Yes; restriction wastes powers and denies development. | The case need not depend on an inherent feminine temperament. |
| Does society need sympathy as well as abstract reasoning? | Yes; power without care becomes destructive. | Both capacities can be cultivated across sexes. |
| Does women’s presence reliably supply sympathy? | She argues for a distinctive feminine influence. | Presence alone cannot establish conduct or institutional consequences. |

Her defense of a corrective feminine force can overturn the claim that women have nothing important to contribute. It can also load women with responsibility for repairing the harms of a society they did not control.

The strongest use of the argument asks institutions to cultivate neglected forms of judgment and to listen to excluded people. It does not appoint women as permanent moral caretakers or excuse men from learning care.

### Reading the Livermore episode against the theory

The scene involving the laundry worker is an unusually useful place to evaluate the essay because its evidence resists Cooper’s preferred conclusion.

At one level, Cooper is confronting racism and an admiration of force in a woman she otherwise respects. Loyalty does not stop her from objecting. At another, she insists that the deeper sympathetic influence remains intact.

That second move risks insulating the theory from correction. If a woman’s kindness confirms feminine sympathy and her cruelty merely conceals it, the theory has no clear test in the conduct being described.

A charitable reading can understand Cooper as distinguishing someone’s best possibilities from an inherited vocabulary of domination. A critical reading should still ask what evidence establishes the possibilities in this particular person. The essay does not give us direct access to Livermore’s private heart.

The lesson is methodological as well as ethical: do not rescue a cherished generalization by describing every awkward case as unrepresentative without explaining why.

### The evidence and what it can bear

Cooper combines satire, historical examples, reported speech, a small institutional inquiry, personal recollection, and moral argument. These forms do different work.

- **Satire** exposes the absurdity of a standard by pressing it or describing it with irony. The frightened professors are part of Cooper’s rhetorical presentation, not a measured survey of faculty emotions.
- **Reported speech** gives her an occasion for criticism. We can analyze how she uses a statement without claiming that we have independently verified the exact words of every speaker.
- **Institutional figures** disclose the narrow reach of opportunity among the responding colleges. They do not isolate the causal effect of one barrier.
- **Recollection** gives a concrete account of unequal arrangements. It is evidence of her narrated experience, not a representative sample of every school.
- **Moral reasoning** asks whether a standard can be justified. A scholarship count cannot settle the value of a person’s self-development.

Her broad claims about centuries of masculine rule and the civilizing effects of women’s influence exceed what these examples independently establish. Studying the essay need not mean repeating that large causal history as settled fact.

The educational conclusion is more secure at a narrower level: where a student must work for board while another receives support, the institution has supplied unequal conditions. Whether a particular reform works as intended would require evidence about its design, reach, and effects.

### Is the scholarship proposal enough?

A scholarship directly addresses a cost that admiration leaves unpaid. It does not necessarily resolve an exclusionary curriculum, disrespect, limited career routes, or the pressure to justify learning through an approved vocation.

**Editorial exercise:** Imagine a scholarship for a student whose classes end at five. Her required job begins at four. Funding tuition helps, but the arrangement still prevents her from attending the final hour. A useful review would ask about the timetable, living costs, work expectations, and the authority to change them.

Now imagine the sponsor requires every recipient to enter one occupation, regardless of the recipient’s developing interests. The aid opens a door while controlling what lies beyond it. Some directed scholarships can be reasonable agreements; their terms should be made explicit and assessed alongside the alternatives genuinely available.

These examples extend Cooper’s attention to resources and expected futures. They do not establish that her proposal was ineffective. The essay calls for a remedy; it does not provide a later evaluation of its implementation.

Her demand for additional support also should not be converted into a claim that everyone without a degree lacks intelligence or value. The argument concerns access to a particular form of development that institutions were prepared to finance selectively.

### Why read this essay as a seminal work?

The importance of Cooper’s work is not exhausted by her biography. This essay makes readers reconsider the evidence used to rank abilities, the people hidden within declarations of progress, and the difference between recognizing a capacity and financing its development.

The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anna-julia-cooper/) places her scholarship within Black feminist philosophy and the philosophy of education, attending to the combined constraints of race and sex. The [National Park Service’s historical account](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/cooper-a-voice-from-the-south.htm) likewise emphasizes the book’s attention to Black women’s education and agency. Those are contextual judgments of significance, not proof that every claim in the essay is correct.

This lesson makes no claim that a particular later thinker took an idea directly from this essay. Identifying an important earlier formulation and establishing a documented chain of influence are different research tasks.

### Comparisons within the catalogue

- [Wollstonecraft on educated dependence](/seminal-ideas/a-vindication-of-the-rights-of-woman/#educated-dependence) examines how the formation imposed on women becomes evidence for keeping them dependent. Cooper adds a pointed question about the funding and expected futures of Black women. This is a comparison, not a claim of direct influence.
- [Du Bois on freedom after emancipation](/seminal-ideas/the-souls-of-black-folk/#freedom-after-emancipation) distinguishes legal change from the institutions needed to make freedom effective. Cooper’s scholarship proposal similarly asks what a declared opportunity requires in practice; her attention to gender also prevents “the race” from becoming an undifferentiated subject.
- [Zitkala-Sa on education and forced assimilation](/seminal-ideas/the-school-days-of-an-indian-girl/#education-and-forced-assimilation) shows why useful learning cannot by itself justify the conditions imposed upon a learner. Together the works invite questions about what education enables, what it demands in return, and who gets to decide.

### Common distortions

- **“Cooper asks only that women be allowed into classrooms.”** She examines curriculum, recognition, financial support, and the futures students are encouraged to pursue.
- **“She values women only as mothers.”** Family and care are prominent, but she also argues for inherent worth, intellectual expression, and self-development.
- **“Her argument is identical to a modern gender-neutral defense of education.”** Her account of complementary feminine and masculine influence is substantive and contested.
- **“Progress for women proves equal progress for Black women.”** The essay explicitly refuses that inference and turns to a more specific inquiry.
- **“The returned college counts are graduation rates.”** They are historical completion totals reported by named institutions, without the denominators needed for rates.
- **“Her challenge to Black men’s sexism denies racial oppression.”** She treats the advancement of Black women as part of the community’s task, not an alternative to it.
- **“A sympathetic intention is sufficient support.”** Her closing demand names money and scholarships.
- **“A scholarship settles every educational injustice.”** It addresses an important barrier; other conditions still require scrutiny.

### Test your understanding

1. How can a school admit women while maintaining unequal educational opportunity?
2. What is wrong with treating a group’s power as proof of its moral superiority?
3. Which claim about feminine sympathy does the Livermore episode put under pressure?
4. Why do earlier examples of learned women not answer Cooper’s case for expanded education?
5. How does self-development differ from learning only to please someone else?
6. What can the returned B.A. counts show, and what can they not show?
7. Why does the school recollection matter beyond the principal’s discouraging remarks?
8. What changes when encouragement becomes a scholarship, and what might remain unresolved?

### If you read only a little

Use the printed page markers in [the 1892 text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/61741/61741-h/61741-h.htm). Do not substitute the page numbers quoted by a modern anthology.

1. **“Our Raison d’Être,” opening pages I–III:** Identify who is speaking, who has been spoken about, and why a missing witness matters to the adequacy of a public judgment.
2. **Pp. 48–50:** Follow the movement from hostility to literacy to the distinction between a women’s course and the regular B.A. Ask who defined the educational need.
3. **Pp. 53–61:** Read the Livermore episode and Cooper’s account of complementary capacities together. Mark the evidence that complicates her own theory.
4. **Pp. 64–72:** Look for the claim of inherent worth, then follow the answer to the marriage objection. Separate self-development from a promise of usefulness to a partner.
5. **Pp. 73–79:** Read without stopping at the college figures. The school recollection and final scholarship demand explain what Cooper wants changed.

For an editorial exercise, take one sentence of institutional encouragement—“We welcome your ambition”—and list the named person or process responsible for funding, protected study time, academic guidance, and living costs. Leave a blank wherever the promise has no arrangement behind it.
