*Edition and scope: Alfred Russel Wallace’s essay appeared as part III of the joint Darwin–Wallace communication in the Journal of the Proceedings of the Linnean Society of London, Zoology, volume 3, pp. 53–62. The communication was read on July 1, 1858 and published August 20, 1858. The essay closes “Ternate, February, 1858”; that is its stated composition place and date, not its publication date. This lesson reads the complete original-printing transcription at Western Kentucky University, checked against Darwin Online’s reproduction. The English text has no translator. All page references below are to the original journal pagination. Later reprints, notably the 1870 Contributions to the Theory of Natural Selection, add subheadings and notes; those additions are not silently assigned to 1858. The focus is this essay, not Wallace’s entire career or Darwin’s complete theory. Definitions, examples, numerical exercises, and evaluations are editorial unless attributed.*

## Orientation

Suppose a population contains several slightly different forms. One gets through a recurring shortage more reliably than the others. Its descendants carry the difference forward. After enough generations, the population can look different even though no animal chose its descendants’ characteristics.

Alfred Russel Wallace turns that possibility into an argument against fixed species. His question is unusually precise: why assume that a variety must eventually return to an original form? Perhaps the conditions that preserve one form can instead preserve a departure from it, then another departure from that.

The essay’s route matters. Wallace starts with an objection drawn from domestic animals, examines the limits on wild populations, proposes differential persistence among varieties, and returns to domestication with a new explanation. He then applies the principle to features such as concealment and to the apparent balance of an animal’s organs.

**The lasting question:** How can repeated differences in survival produce an organized result without foresight?

**The essential limit:** Wallace’s essay supplies an influential causal proposal and illustrative reasoning. It does not measure inheritance, demonstrate every proposed adaptation, or establish that evolution always produces greater complexity, moral worth, or lasting superiority. Reading the mechanism carefully makes those stronger claims harder to accept. [Pp. 53–62.](#sources)

## Core lesson

### Varieties and the fixed-type objection

**A variety, in this essay, is a distinguishable form within or closely related to a species whose members tend to reproduce that form.** Wallace’s vocabulary does not map neatly onto a modern genetic category. He moves between differences among individuals and competition among varieties. Keep both scales visible.

**A fixed type is an assumed original form beyond which lasting change is supposed to be impossible.** Wallace addresses an argument for such a limit: domestic breeds can lose their distinctive characteristics when human care ends; therefore, departures from a species’ original form must be temporary. The objection grants some variation while denying that variation can accumulate indefinitely. [Pp. 53–54.](#sources)

His first reply concerns the comparison. Domestic and wild animals face different conditions. An animal protected and fed by people may retain a feature that would be costly without that support. Its later loss cannot establish a universal tendency to return to an original form. We first need to understand which conditions maintain or remove the feature.

**Editorial example:** Imagine a sheltered population of beetles in which a conspicuous inherited color has no effect on reproduction. Release the beetles onto a background where predators readily see that color. If the color becomes rare, the result could reflect changed survival conditions. It would not demonstrate that nature recognizes an ancestral color and tries to restore it. This is an invented explanatory example, not Wallace’s experiment or a report of measured beetles.

The logical lesson is broader than biology. An outcome observed under one set of conditions does not establish the same outcome under materially different conditions. But showing a comparison is inadequate does not yet prove Wallace’s alternative. He still needs a process capable of sustaining departures.

### Population checks and the struggle for existence

**A population check is a condition that prevents potential reproduction from becoming sustained population growth.** Food shortages, predators, and severe weather are among Wallace’s proposed checks. His **struggle for existence** includes obtaining food, escaping danger, and keeping offspring alive. It need not mean animals fighting one another. [Pp. 54–57.](#sources)

Wallace asks why animals capable of producing many offspring do not increase without limit. If birds repeatedly produce young but bird numbers do not rise accordingly, something must prevent all that potential increase from persisting. He moves from reproduction to mortality and from mortality to the circumstances that decide who survives.

The argument contains a sound conditional insight: limited resources and continued reproduction can create repeated differences in survival. Some of Wallace’s surrounding statements are much stronger. He treats populations as broadly stationary, makes sweeping claims about the relation between fertility and abundance, and sometimes writes as though the weakest must always die. Those statements should not be turned into universal ecological laws.

**Editorial calculation:** Suppose 100 breeding pairs produce 400 young in a simplified generation. If the next generation again contains only 200 breeding adults, many potential recruits have not become breeders. That arithmetic alone cannot tell us how many died, failed to breed, or left the area. It also cannot identify which trait affected the outcome.

Wallace’s bird calculation on p. 55 dramatizes unrealized growth. It should be read as a hypothetical, not a census. We do not rely on its exact large-number estimate; a later reprint added a numerical correction. The mechanism requires a difference between possible increase and realized persistence, not a spectacular particular number.

Wallace also contrasts the abundant passenger pigeon with more prolific but less abundant birds. He attributes its abundance to food over a large area and sustained flight. This is his explanatory account, not a controlled demonstration that those factors alone determine abundance. [Pp. 55–56.](#sources)

**Takeaway:** Count reproductive possibilities and realized outcomes separately. Then investigate the causes of the difference rather than treating the count itself as the explanation.

### Differential persistence and inherited difference

**Differential persistence means that some forms continue or multiply more successfully than others under specified conditions.** To explain lasting change, the relevant differences must also recur in descendants. A temporary advantage acquired by one animal cannot simply be assumed to become a characteristic of the next generation.

Wallace begins with differences that can affect obtaining food or escaping enemies: color, hair, limbs, and other bodily features. He proposes that a variety with better powers of preserving existence can become more numerous. A severe change—drought or the arrival of a predator—can remove less successful forms. A formerly minor variety may then occupy the place of its parent form. [Pp. 57–58.](#sources)

His central reasoning can be reconstructed in five steps:

1. Organisms differ in ways that can affect their lives.
2. Not every organism or form persists equally well under the same conditions.
3. Some differences are reproduced across generations.
4. Repeated differences in persistence can change the relative numbers of those forms.
5. A surviving form can itself produce further varieties, allowing cumulative departure.

Step three is indispensable, but the essay offers no worked-out mechanism of inheritance. Its opening takes reproducing varieties as a fact to explain from, while its later argument concentrates on what happens to them. We should neither omit inheritance from our reconstruction nor pretend Wallace supplied a genetic theory.

**Editorial numerical model:** Begin with 50 individuals of form A and 50 of form B. Suppose each A leaves, on average, two descendants counted at the next census, while each B leaves one. Assume descendants retain their parental form and ignore all other processes. The next census contains 100 A and 50 B: A’s share rises from one-half to two-thirds.

The model shows how unequal contribution can change proportions without anyone planning the result. Its assumptions do the explanatory work. If the forms are not inherited, if the advantage disappears, or if other processes dominate the census, the calculation no longer predicts the outcome. It is not a measured rate, a complete population model, or a numerical result given by Wallace.

**Natural selection**, as used in this guide, names the process by which inherited differences associated with unequal survival or reproduction change a population across generations. That definition is an editorial aid. Wallace’s essay places particular emphasis on survival, abundance, and the replacement of varieties; it should retain that historical shape.

### Divergence without a destination

**Divergence means increasing difference among related forms.** Wallace argues that a successful variety can generate further varieties; some of those can in turn persist and depart farther from the earlier form. Repetition removes the need to posit a fixed boundary beyond which variation can never go. [Pp. 58–59.](#sources)

“Indefinitely” in the title does not promise infinite size, unlimited lifespan, or a continuous climb toward perfection. It challenges an assigned limit to departure from an original type. Wallace’s conclusion speaks of minute steps in various directions, constrained by the conditions under which organisms can exist. [P. 62.](#sources)

His language nevertheless creates a real tension. He calls some forms superior, improved, or more highly organized. These expressions can suggest a universal ranking. Yet he explicitly allows environmental change to reverse an advantage: the newer form may disappear while an older form flourishes. He also allows differences that have no perceptible effect on survival to continue alongside a parent form or revert. [P. 59.](#sources)

**Editorial example:** In our imagined beetle population, a dark color may conceal individuals on one surface and expose them on another. Calling dark beetles simply “better” erases the condition on which the judgment depends. A change in the background can reverse the advantage without either form becoming more complex.

Wallace’s own caveat is a useful restraint on his more confident language. The durable claim is conditional persistence, not an environment-independent ladder of excellence.

### Domestication and changed conditions

Wallace now returns to his opening objection. Domestic animals receive food, shelter, protection, and sometimes help with offspring. Features maintained under that support need not help an animal living without it. He argues that release can favor a form closer to the wild stock or lead to extinction. [Pp. 59–61.](#sources)

His response changes what “return” means. An apparent return toward an earlier form can result from the removal of poorly suited departures. It need not be an internal rule that commands all varieties to approach a permanent original.

But his contrast is overstated. He says no inferences about wild varieties can be drawn from domestic ones and describes domestic variation as radically unlike variation in nature. The important criticism of a careless analogy does not justify rejecting every possible comparison. We would need to identify precisely which conditions differ and which processes remain comparable.

The joint publication makes that disagreement visible. Darwin’s accompanying letter abstract treats breeding as a route into understanding cumulative selection. Wallace uses domestication chiefly to expose a misleading inference about limits. Read both arguments before assuming the two authors reached their proposals by an identical route. [Wallace, pp. 59–61; Darwin’s letter abstract, pp. 50–52.](#sources)

**Useful question:** When support is removed, what exactly changes—food, danger, reproduction, movement, or the value of a trait? Naming the changed condition is more informative than saying an organism has become “natural” again.

### Adaptation without intention

**An adaptation is a feature considered in relation to how it helps an organism live or reproduce under particular conditions.** Explaining a feature as an adaptation requires more than noticing that it seems useful.

Wallace contrasts his proposal with an account in which animals’ efforts or desires produce the needed organs. He invokes retractile talons, giraffe necks, and concealing colors. In his account, different forms occur, and forms with relevant advantages last longer. The animal need not anticipate the advantage or design its descendants. [Pp. 61–62.](#sources)

Those examples illustrate a proposed mechanism; they do not independently demonstrate the complete history of any feature. Wallace’s giraffe discussion should not be repeated as a fully tested account of neck evolution. His brief representation of Lamarck is likewise an opponent’s characterization, not a sufficient reading of Lamarck’s own work.

The explanatory gain is substantial even with those limits. Organization can arise through cumulative differences in persistence. A purpose in a breeder’s mind is no longer required for every apparently purposeful result.

**Editorial application:** When a trait seems perfectly suited to a task, ask three questions: What alternatives actually existed? Did their differences affect survival or reproduction? Were those differences inherited? A story about usefulness becomes an investigation only when those questions can encounter evidence.

## Deep exploration

### What the original publication lets us establish

This essay belonged to a joint communication assembled by Charles Lyell and Joseph Dalton Hooker, alongside two pieces of Darwin’s earlier writing. Their covering letter is dated June 30, 1858. It describes independently conceived proposals and explains their decision to present the material together. The Linnean Society records the presentation on July 1. [Joint communication, pp. 45–46; Linnean Society context.](#sources)

That is enough to establish a consequential shared publication, not enough to settle every later dispute over intention, priority, or consent. The covering letter is evidence from the people arranging publication, not an omniscient account of everyone’s private understanding. We do not need a theft narrative, or a story of frictionless agreement, to read the arguments.

The title of the whole communication differs from Wallace’s title. References to the joint paper’s pp. 45–62 should not turn into a claim that Wallace authored its entire contents. His contribution occupies pp. 53–62.

The later reprint matters for interpretation too. Added subheadings can divide a continuous argument into concepts that look more familiar to a modern reader. This lesson follows the original printing and supplies its own teaching headings. The guide’s labels are not evidence that Wallace organized his essay in exactly these terms.

### A population explanation needs bridges

A difficult passage moves from survival among individuals to abundance among species, then from species to varieties. Wallace presents these as closely related cases. A careful reader should ask what is preserved during each move. [Pp. 56–58.](#sources)

An individual that escapes a predator has survived an event. A variety that increases has changed its representation in a population. A species that persists has continued through time. None of those observations alone specifies the mechanism connecting them.

For example, a variety could become common because many members arrive from elsewhere. Another could remain rare because it has only recently appeared. Those are editorial alternative explanations; the essay does not systematically exclude them. The proposed survival advantage needs evidence that distinguishes it from such possibilities.

The strongest reading of Wallace therefore treats the transitions as claims to investigate. The essay identifies an important process capable of cumulative change. It does not establish that every difference in abundance must have that explanation.

This is also where reconstruction must stay honest. It is tempting to replace every occurrence of “variety” with “individual carrying an allele,” meaning a version of a gene. Doing so would import a later explanatory vocabulary and hide Wallace’s own shifts of scale. His text can be illuminating without being rewritten into a textbook he did not author.

### Can a small advantage guarantee success?

Wallace allows individual exceptions but argues that large numbers and long periods make a slight advantage effective. The appeal to averages is essential: selection does not require every better-suited animal to survive every encounter. [Pp. 58–59.](#sources)

The word “must” remains too strong if it is detached from assumptions. A rare form can disappear in a chance event. A favorable condition can end before the advantage accumulates. A trait can help in one respect while costing something in another. Repeating the phrase “long enough” does not establish that a particular population will have the needed time.

These are logical objections to inevitability, not findings measured by this lesson. To evaluate an actual case, we would need population sizes, inheritance patterns, environmental conditions, and reproductive outcomes over time. The essay supplies no such dataset.

Our numerical model in the core lesson deliberately removes chance and competing processes. Its clarity comes from simplification. A good explanation tells the reader which simplifications make its conclusion follow.

### The steam-engine governor: preservation and change

Near the end, Wallace compares the process to the centrifugal governor of a steam engine. A governor regulates an engine by responding to departures from its operating state. **Feedback** means that a system’s results affect its subsequent operation; **negative feedback** tends to counteract a departure. These are editorial terms for examining his analogy. [P. 62.](#sources)

Wallace uses the comparison to explain why an unbalanced deficiency is unlikely to persist. A disadvantage brings consequences that remove it. That makes the mechanism seem conservative: it preserves viable organization.

Yet the title concerns continued departure. The tension is resolved only when the relevant conditions are included. What preserves a form under one set of conditions can favor a different form when conditions change. Persistence and transformation can be outcomes of the same selective process.

The analogy has limits. An engineered governor has a designed arrangement and operating target. Wallace’s biological argument does not require an intended future form. We should carry over the relation between variation and corrective consequence without carrying over a designer’s purpose.

**Editorial example:** A thermostat maintaining a chosen temperature helps explain corrective feedback. It would mislead if used to suggest that a population has a chosen future body shape. Analogies transfer selected relationships; they do not transfer every property of their source.

### What remains unproved by a plausible mechanism?

A mechanism can be possible, active in a case, or sufficient to explain a particular history. Those are different claims.

Wallace’s examples often show why a feature could affect persistence. Establishing that the feature actually arose through that process would require evidence about historical variation, transmission to descendants, and differential outcomes. Establishing a general contribution across life requires a much broader body of evidence than this short essay.

This guide consequently does not use the essay’s fame as empirical validation. Nor does it treat identifying a gap in 1858 as a refutation of evolutionary biology. The object being evaluated is a particular argument at a particular stage of inquiry.

Several limits are visible directly in the text: no genetic mechanism, no comprehensive account of how varieties originate, no systematic measurement of reproductive differences, and no developed test for when diverging forms have become separate species. A theory of species change must eventually confront those questions; Wallace’s short publication does not finish that work.

The distinction rewards readers of any scientific classic. Ask what the author made newly explainable, then ask which evidence would be needed to support the explanation in a particular case.

### Biological persistence supplies no moral permission

Wallace’s words “superior” and “inferior” concern his proposed conditions of persistence. Even if we could measure those conditions precisely, the measurement would not decide what people owe one another.

**A moral judgment evaluates what ought to be done or valued.** A causal account explains how an outcome occurs. Moving from “a trait spreads” to “we ought to encourage its spread” introduces a moral premise. The causal description cannot supply that premise by itself.

**Editorial example:** A harmful practice might spread because it benefits those already able to impose it. Explaining its persistence would not justify the practice. Likewise, helping someone survive changes a condition of existence; it does not violate a moral command issued by nature.

The essay’s contrast between domestic support and wild struggle must not become a prescription to withhold care from people. Wallace is attempting a biological explanation here. This guide neither attributes a complete social philosophy to the essay nor treats evolutionary success as human worth.

- [The Great Learning’s root and branches](/seminal-ideas/the-great-learning/#root-and-branches) asks what an outcome depends on. Wallace offers a contrast between an intended program of cultivation and cumulative change without a guiding intention.

### Common distortions

- **“The strongest always survive.”** Wallace sometimes overstates the case, but strength is only one possible advantage. His examples include concealment and access to food; his averages permit individual exceptions.
- **“An animal changes because it needs to.”** The essay distinguishes the occurrence of varieties from their subsequent persistence. Need is not a demonstrated mechanism producing an inherited solution.
- **“Evolution always climbs toward perfection.”** Wallace’s environmental-reversal caveat blocks a simple universal ranking, even though some of his language invites one.
- **“A useful feature must have this history.”** Present usefulness is not a complete demonstration of origin.
- **“Domestic reversion proves a fixed species boundary.”** Changed support and changed consequences can explain an apparent return without a universal rule of restoration.
- **“Wallace and Darwin published identical arguments.”** The joint communication lets us compare distinct emphases, especially their uses of domestication.
- **“What persists is what deserves to persist.”** That adds a moral premise the causal explanation does not establish.

### If you read only a little

Use the page markers in the [original-printing transcription](https://people.wku.edu/charles.smith/wallace/S043.htm). The essay has no numbered chapters; the following page ranges and paragraph topics identify the route.

1. **Pp. 53–54, opening objection:** Write the missing premise between domestic reversion and fixed species. Decide what would make that comparison legitimate.
2. **Pp. 54–57, population checks:** Separate the conditional argument about unrealized increase from Wallace’s stronger claims about abundance and mortality.
3. **Pp. 57–59, varieties and changed conditions:** Mark each move between individuals, varieties, and species. Locate the environmental-reversal and unimportant-variation caveats on p. 59.
4. **Pp. 59–61, domestication:** Ask whether the argument defeats one careless inference or justifies Wallace’s sweeping rejection of comparison.
5. **Pp. 61–62, organs and the governor:** Separate an illustrative explanation of possible adaptation from evidence establishing a feature’s history.
6. **Joint communication, pp. 50–52:** Read Darwin’s accompanying letter abstract in [Darwin Online](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1858_species_F350.html). Compare its use of breeders with Wallace’s. This is a specific textual comparison, not a substitute for reading Darwin’s later book.

Then read Wallace’s ten pages continuously. Choose one of his examples and write down the observation, the proposed process, and the evidence still needed. Keep the third item visible even when the explanation is persuasive.
