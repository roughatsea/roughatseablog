## Orientation

A man reads a history filled with declarations of virtue. After staring at the page through the night, he believes he has found what the declarations conceal: people eating people.

Lu Xun makes this reader the writer of a disturbing diary. He suspects neighbors, his doctor, and his own brother. Glances become warnings; ordinary meals become threats. His certainty outruns the evidence he can give us.

Yet dismissing his fears does not make the story’s society innocent. He asks a question that survives our doubts about his account: does a practice become right because people have always accepted it?

The short story, published in the Chinese journal *New Youth* in May 1918, moves between personal terror and social criticism. Its ending also unsettles the diarist’s division between innocent victim and guilty world. He may have participated in what he condemns.

**The lasting question:** How do we question a harmful inheritance without assuming that the critic stands outside it?

**The essential limit:** The story is fiction, not a clinical case report or a historical census of cannibalism. Its diary writer, framing editor, and author are different voices. Neither the writer’s suffering nor his apparent illness makes every claim automatically true or false.

## Core lesson

### The frame and the meaning of recovery

**A frame narrative is a story that presents another story and shapes the conditions under which we receive it.** Here an unnamed acquaintance introduces the diary as a document left by a man who has recovered from an illness.

The acquaintance says that he went to visit two former school friends after hearing that one was ill. He encountered the elder brother, who told him that the younger had recovered and gone elsewhere to await an official appointment. The brother produced two volumes of diary material. The visitor describes the writing as confused, identifies a persecutory illness, and selects a sequence for medical study. He also says he has changed personal names while preserving mistaken expressions. [Preface.](#sources)

Before the diarist speaks, then, other people have assigned his words a status: symptoms to be studied. The framing voice has the calm of someone arranging a record. The diary itself speaks in alarm, conjecture, and direct appeal.

The language difference helps produce that relation. The preface uses literary or classical Chinese; the diary uses a vernacular style closer to ordinary speech. **Vernacular** names a written register drawing more closely on everyday language. It does not mean that every reader would find the text effortless or that Chinese fiction had never used vernacular language before.

The frame supplies reasons to doubt the diary’s literal account. It also gives us a reason to question what recovery means. If the diarist had begun to condemn the accepted order, his return to a career within that order could be read as accommodation. That is an interpretation of the framing arrangement, not an established report that he abandoned every conviction.

Two possibilities remain in tension: he may have recovered from a disabling condition; he may also have ceased to trouble the world he feared. The story does not require us to choose one description and forget the other.

**Editorial example:** An institution archives a complaint under “personal distress.” That classification could identify a real need for support. It would not, by itself, establish that nothing in the complaint requires investigation. Conversely, a complaint about a real injustice need not make every accompanying allegation accurate.

**Takeaway:** Read the document and the classification of the document separately. Ask who gets to define normality, and which questions that definition leaves open.

### Moral words and the image of eating people

**An allegorical reading treats concrete events or images as carrying a further pattern of meaning.** Eating human beings in this story can be read as an image of people consuming one another’s lives under socially respectable rules.

The story does not begin by announcing that interpretation. In section I, the diarist regards a dog’s glance as a reason for fear. In II, neighbors and children seem to share an intention to harm him. In III, he recalls a tenant’s report that villagers killed a man and ate his heart and liver. He then connects the report with a woman threatening to bite her child and with the teeth of people who laugh. [I–III.](#sources)

These connections are not equally warranted. A threatening phrase, an account of violence, and a dog’s gaze are different kinds of evidence. The diary turns them into a single plan directed against its writer.

The central reading scene makes that procedure into social criticism. The diarist finds the respectable words 仁義道德—roughly, benevolence, righteousness, and morality—across the pages of history. Between the lines, he believes, the real message is 吃人: eating people. [III.]

The force of the scene lies in the gap between declared virtue and what a practice costs its participants. Moral vocabulary can describe genuine obligations. It can also protect arrangements in which one person’s sacrifice is treated as another person’s entitlement.

**Editorial example:** A workplace praises its “family spirit” while repeatedly making a junior employee absorb unpaid emergencies. The praise does not establish the arrangement’s fairness. To evaluate it, we would need to examine expectations, compensation, choice, and who bears the cost. This is an invented analogy, not Lu Xun’s setting and not a claim that every workplace using family language is abusive.

The story’s image is stronger and more disturbing than an ordinary charge of hypocrisy. The diarist comes to see respected education, family relations, and historical language as implicated in the same pattern. The institutions meant to preserve human life appear to feed upon it.

That is a literary challenge, not a demonstrated proposition that all morality is merely predation. The image makes a reader look again; it does not spare the reader the work of distinguishing obligations from their abuses.

### Custom is not justification

**A custom is an established practice. A justification is a reason that could make a practice acceptable.** Repetition can explain how a practice became familiar without establishing that it ought to continue.

Section VIII turns that distinction into a conversation. The diarist asks a young visitor whether eating people is right. The replies evade, deny, change the subject, then appeal to longstanding practice. When the diarist asks whether always having done something makes it right, the reply shifts toward silencing him: speaking about it is itself treated as wrong. [VIII.](#sources)

The dialogue compresses several ways a difficult question can be avoided:

1. Treat the alleged harm as a joke.
2. Deny that it occurs.
3. Admit its occurrence while invoking custom.
4. Replace judgment of the practice with judgment of the person who names it.

The sequence is not evidence that an actual visitor secretly admitted literal cannibalism. We receive the conversation through the diarist, and its ending unsettles the scene’s reality. As constructed dialogue, however, it exposes a recognizable failure of argument.

The ethical point can be reconstructed more narrowly:

1. A practice may persist because people learn and repeat it.
2. Persistence is compatible with serious harm.
3. Therefore persistence alone cannot establish rightness.
4. A challenge about harm needs an answer about reasons and consequences, rather than only an account of age or acceptance.

**Editorial example:** A school requires newcomers to endure humiliating initiation because earlier students endured it. That history explains the ritual’s continuity. It does not establish a benefit sufficient to justify the humiliation. A separate defense would need to answer what the ritual does, whether participation is freely chosen, and whether its purpose can be achieved otherwise.

Lu Xun does not have to make the diarist infallible for that reasoning to matter. A questionable witness can pose a question others still need to answer. Equally, a powerful question does not validate every factual accusation the witness makes.

**Takeaway:** When a defense answers “why is it right?” with “it has always happened,” identify the missing argument.

### Shared fear and the critic’s complicity

**Complicity is participation in, contribution to, or benefit from a harmful arrangement, even when someone is not its sole designer or direct agent.** The story makes complicity harder to place safely in other people.

In section IX, the diarist imagines people who want to eat others while fearing that others will eat them. Family members, friends, teachers, students, enemies, and strangers are caught in mutual suspicion and restraint. The practice persists even though abandoning it appears to promise relief.

Read allegorically, the passage asks how domination can survive when its participants also suffer from it. Someone may fear losing their place if they stop doing what everyone else expects. Mutual fear can reproduce the conduct that creates the fear. This is an editorial reconstruction of the image, not a social-scientific result established by the story.

In X, the diarist appeals to his brother to change. He even recalls that the brother could refuse a tenant’s request to reduce rent: the capacity to say no exists, but has been exercised against the person with less power. The appeal asks that capacity to work differently.

The sharper turn comes in XI and XII. Remembering his little sister’s death, the diarist becomes convinced that his brother ate her. He recalls a lesson in filial devotion involving a child’s flesh offered to an ill parent. Then he suspects that food containing his sister’s flesh might have been served to him without his knowledge.

The sister’s literal fate is not independently confirmed. What changes is the diarist’s moral position. He can no longer confidently describe himself as untouched by the history he condemns. His experience of danger does not guarantee his innocence.

**Editorial example:** A person protests a cruel institutional rule, then realizes that they once applied it routinely and gained standing by doing so. The realization need not invalidate the protest. It does change what responsible criticism requires: acknowledgment, a changed practice, and attention to those who paid the cost.

The story prevents two evasions. “I am threatened” does not establish “I have never helped sustain the threat.” But “I may be implicated” does not establish “there is no point in changing.”

### Children and an uncertain future

**Social reproduction means the passing on of practices, expectations, and roles through everyday life.** In the story, it is visible in children’s learned hostility long before the final appeal.

In II, the diarist wonders why children who were not born when his supposed offense occurred look at him like the adults. He concludes that their parents taught them. In VIII, he fears that a young visitor may already be teaching the next generation. The diary is concerned with how a pattern persists, not only with the conduct of a few elders. [II; VIII.](#sources)

The final section is extremely short. It asks whether there may still be children who have not eaten people, then pleads for their rescue. The uncertainty comes before the appeal. The children are a possibility of interruption, not proof that innocence has survived intact.

The ending also changes the diarist’s aim. Much of the diary asks how he can survive. The final request reaches beyond his own safety. Yet it does not provide a curriculum, an organization, or an accomplished rescue.

**Editorial example:** An adult rejects a humiliating initiation but continues joking that the new students must eventually learn their place. Removing the ceremony may leave its lesson in ordinary conversation. A change in inheritance requires attention to what gets praised, rehearsed, and passed along.

That example applies a question from the story. It is not an empirically tested program for ending abuse. The text gives us an urgent demand and a reason to examine transmission; readers must supply and evaluate any practical proposal.

**Takeaway:** Do not confuse a hopeful addressee with a guaranteed happy ending. Ask which habits would have to change for the appeal to become possible.

## Deep exploration

### An unreliable narrator need not be an empty witness

**An unreliable narrator gives an account the reader has grounds to question.** The term describes a relation between narration and evidence. It need not mean that everything the narrator says is false.

Lu Xun gives us concrete reasons for caution. A dog’s glance becomes proof of conspiracy. Fish appear indistinguishable from human flesh. A medical examination is interpreted as an assessment of how much meat the diarist carries. Beams and rafters seem to press down upon him. [I; IV; X.]

Those details constrain a literal reading. We cannot responsibly treat the diary as transparent documentation of a village-wide plot. But the ethical force of its questions does not rest upon accepting the plot. The story asks whether ordinary respectability can coexist with coercion, whether authority can dismiss a challenge by naming its speaker, and whether an established practice can justify itself.

Four levels help keep the reading disciplined:

| Level | Example in the story | What the reader can conclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reported occurrence | A doctor visits and examines the diarist. | This is an event within the diary’s account, not an independently observed case. |
| Diarist’s inference | The examination is preparation for eating him. | The inference exceeds what the described examination establishes. |
| Literary pattern | Family, medicine, and historical authority appear joined in threat. | The story makes protective institutions available for critical examination. |
| Editorial evaluation | An institution needs ways to hear and assess challenges. | This is a defensible application to test, not proof supplied by the fictional plot. |

The distinction avoids both credulity and wholesale dismissal. A reader can doubt a causal explanation while attending to suffering, language, and the conditions under which someone can contest an authority.

### Is the preface the final authority?

The preface sounds orderly. It also receives the diary from the brother, whom the diary later suspects. The visitor has not presented an independent investigation into every allegation. His editorial choices produce the document we read.

This does not prove that the visitor or brother is deceitful. It means their position is part of the fiction rather than an authorial certificate placed outside it. The framing editor has a vocabulary, a social relationship, and a purpose: presenting selected material for medical inquiry.

The diary’s undated entries also resist the appearance of a neatly recovered chronology. The frame identifies different inks and handwriting as signs that the material was written at different times, then selects passages with some connection. The work’s order is an arrangement, not an untouched flow of private consciousness.

The statement about the diarist seeking office makes the beginning consequential for the ending. His appeal to the children is the last word inside the diary, while the frame has already told us of his later pursuit of an official appointment. A reading that stops at the appeal can miss the question the preface leaves suspended: what became of that demand?

Two outcomes can therefore coexist in our interpretation: recovery may be welcome for a suffering person, while social readjustment may leave the object of his criticism unchanged. The story does not provide enough evidence to collapse one into the other.

### The danger in a righteous counterattack

In X, the diarist imagines people progressing beyond cannibalism into a fuller humanity. He also warns that people who refuse to change will be destroyed like wolves or left behind like lower creatures.

The warning complicates his appeal. A speaker resisting dehumanization begins to divide people into the truly human and those whose removal the future will permit. His urgency can draw on the language of elimination.

This is not a reason to equate a threatened person’s protest with every form of power he opposes. It is a reason to examine the means and categories a protest borrows. If the aim is a world where people no longer consume one another, treating some people as expendable requires scrutiny.

The passage’s movement from creatures to humans should also not become a biological lesson. Its imagery serves a moral and historical argument within a character’s speech. It does not establish a scientific ladder from bad people to good people or a mechanism of evolutionary change.

**Editorial question:** Can the proposed reform protect those threatened by an institution without creating a new category of people whom nobody needs to hear? The story gives reasons to ask, not a complete answer.

### What is being criticized in “morality”?

The attack on eating people behind declarations of virtue is commonly read as a criticism of oppressive traditional relations, especially those legitimated through Confucian moral language. The publication setting supports taking that challenge seriously: *New Youth* was a forum of the New Culture Movement’s disputes about inherited authority and literary form.

The story should not become a factual claim that Chinese people are inherently cruel or that all Chinese ethical thought has one content. Its particular form directs criticism from within a culture, using a troubled speaker who cannot exempt himself. That differs from assigning an unchanging defect to a people.

Compare [The Great Learning on family, example, and reciprocity](/seminal-ideas/the-great-learning/#family-example-and-reciprocity). That earlier Confucian work asks a person to examine partiality and the mismatch between professed standards and conduct. Lu Xun’s fiction puts respected family and moral authority under pressure. Reading the two together exposes a dispute about what ethical relations require; it does not turn either into the exhaustive voice of China.

Nor must a reader accept every inherited obligation to resist the opposite simplification that all obligations are predatory. The hard question is which relationships sustain people’s development and which use moral prestige to prevent those bearing the costs from objecting.

### Why the language and publication matter

J. D. Chinnery’s scholarly article locates the story’s appearance in the May 1918 issue of *New Youth*, during the New Culture Movement’s challenges to Confucian morality and literary language. That is the immediate context used here; the story precedes the student protest of May 4, 1919.

Bonnie S. McDougall and Kam Louie’s literary history treats the story as a turning point in the serious use of modern vernacular fiction. They also note that vernacular fiction existed centuries earlier. Its importance should therefore not be inflated into the invention of all Chinese vernacular storytelling. [Contextual sources.](#sources)

The story’s own contrast between classical preface and vernacular diary makes language part of the contest over who may speak and what counts as an intelligible account. That last sentence is our interpretation of the form, rather than a claim that one register always liberates and the other always dominates.

These sources establish context and scholarly judgments of significance. They do not establish that the story alone caused later literary or political changes. This guide makes no unsupported chain-of-influence claim and does not require one to justify close reading.

### A practical exercise in separating claims

Choose one passage from III, IV, or VIII and make three notes:

1. What does the diarist say he perceived?
2. What conclusion does he draw from it?
3. What further question does the story make available to the reader?

For IV, one answer might be: a physician examines him; he concludes that the physician is an executioner assessing his flesh; the scene asks how care and threatening authority can become indistinguishable within his experience. That interpretation does not establish the physician’s guilt.

For VIII, distinguish the visitor’s purported appeal to custom from the validity of the diarist’s factual premise. Even if the conversation does not establish literal eating, longstanding acceptance would not be sufficient to justify an actual harmful practice.

Now apply the same distinctions to an editorial example: a student says that an academic panel wants to ruin her future because it rejected her appeal. The rejection alone does not establish malicious intention. It also does not establish that the procedure was fair. Different claims need different evidence.

The exercise is a method of reading and reasoning. It offers no clinical diagnosis and no rule for dismissing people in distress.

### Connections within the catalogue

- [Gilman on testimony and the cost of disbelief](/seminal-ideas/the-yellow-wall-paper/#testimony-and-the-cost-of-disbelief) likewise requires readers to take suffering seriously while questioning an explanation. The narrators’ circumstances and the works’ political settings differ.
- [Clifford on sincerity, truth, and responsible inquiry](/seminal-ideas/the-ethics-of-belief/#sincerity-truth-and-responsible-inquiry) separates conviction from adequate grounds. Lu Xun’s fiction adds a problem Clifford’s rule alone does not settle: how a compromised account can still expose a neglected ethical question.
- [Cooper on the people missing from a progress story](/seminal-ideas/the-higher-education-of-women/#the-people-missing-from-a-progress-story) asks what a reassuring general description leaves out. Lu Xun’s disturbing image likewise makes respectable language answer to concealed costs. This is an editorial comparison of methods, not evidence of direct influence.

### Common distortions

- **“The diary records Lu Xun’s own illness.”** It is a constructed story with distinct framing and diary voices.
- **“Every suspicion is vindicated.”** The narration supplies repeated reasons to doubt its literal inferences.
- **“A diagnosis settles every ethical question.”** Classifying the speaker does not evaluate every practice he challenges.
- **“The madman is a pure outsider.”** The late suspicion about his sister implicates him in the history he condemns.
- **“The story proves that every Confucian teaching is cannibalistic.”** A powerful critical image is not an exhaustive account of a tradition.
- **“The last line guarantees a new generation’s innocence.”** It is preceded by a question about whether children who have not participated remain.
- **“The preface gives a simple happy ending.”** Recovery and seeking office leave the relation between personal adjustment and social criticism unresolved.
- **“This was the first Chinese fiction written in ordinary language.”** Its place in modern literary history does not erase older vernacular traditions.

### If you read only a little

Use [the Chinese 1918-version transcription](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%8B%82%E4%BA%BA%E6%97%A5%E8%A8%98_%281918%E5%B9%B4%E6%9C%AC%29) or an identified translation that includes the preface and all thirteen sections. Our numbering follows the Chinese text; English wording and page numbers vary by translator.

1. **Preface, then I–II:** List what the frame claims to know, then distinguish the diarist’s perceptions from his conclusions.
2. **III–V:** Follow the movement from a reported violent incident to reading history and interpreting care. Do not give each kind of evidence equal weight.
3. **VIII–IX:** Read the question about custom beside the account of mutual fear. Separate the moral argument from the alleged conspiracy.
4. **X:** Read the appeal to the brother and the threat against people who will not change. Ask whether the language of reform escapes domination.
5. **XI–XIII, then the preface again:** Trace the loss of personal innocence, the question about children, and the report of later recovery. Notice what none of the three resolves.

For a small editorial exercise, choose one accepted practice you regard as harmful. Write down its actual effects, its defenders’ strongest reasons, your own participation, and a change that would reduce the harm. The story’s unsettling image should sharpen those questions, not answer them on your behalf.
