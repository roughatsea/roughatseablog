*Edition and scope: Enchiridion, also spelled Encheiridion, means a handbook. This Greek compilation of Epictetus’s teaching is traditionally credited to his student Arrian. Its exact date is uncertain; “second century CE” is a historical placement, not a verified year of first publication. We read all 53 sections in W. A. Oldfather’s English translation, The Discourses as Reported by Arrian, the Manual, and Fragments, volume II, first printed in 1928. Wikisource reproduces a later reprint; its edition record identifies the 1959 printing. References below are the Manual’s numbered sections, not pages of the Discourses. Oldfather’s vocabulary, including “moral purpose” and “externals,” needs interpretation. We use the digital transcription and its notes, without claiming a separate examination of the print facsimile. The ancient text and this English translation are public domain in the United States. Other translations may divide paragraphs or render technical words differently.*

## Orientation

You prepare carefully for a meeting. The other person cancels. Your preparation was yours to undertake; their attendance was never yours to guarantee. What follows from that difference? You might rearrange the meeting, question the relationship, or change your plans. Must you also conclude that your day, or your worth, has been ruined?

That editorial example introduces Epictetus’s question about freedom. For him, freedom depends on learning where to place the good of a life. If approval, property, or survival must be secured at any cost, whoever can withhold them acquires power over what you will do.

Epictetus taught Stoicism, an ancient philosophical tradition that makes excellent judgment and conduct central to living well. He was born in Hierapolis in Asia Minor, lived enslaved in Rome, later obtained freedom, and taught at Nicopolis. Those facts give his discussion of dependence a setting; they do not prove his conclusions. [Stanford Encyclopedia, “Epictetus,” §1.](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/)

**The lasting question:** Can you care seriously about what happens while refusing to purchase success through conduct you cannot defend?

**The essential limit:** The handbook’s radical account of moral freedom must not become an accusation that people consent to violence, illness, or grief. Its strongest promises require philosophical evaluation, not automatic acceptance.

## Core lesson

### What is up to us

**What is up to us means what belongs to our exercise of judgment and choice. Externals are things outside that exercise, including our bodies, possessions, reputation, and public position.** The distinction concerns the source of moral responsibility. It is more exacting than a list of things we can influence.

Section 1 places judgment, desire, aversion, and our own doing on one side, and body, property, reputation, and office on the other. Oldfather translates the contrast as things under our control and things not under our control. Modern readers can easily hear “control” as guaranteed management of events. Epictetus is identifying a sphere of agency that he regards as intrinsically free, even when the world obstructs its projects. [§1.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Epictetus,_the_Discourses_as_reported_by_Arrian,_the_Manual,_and_Fragments/Manual)

**Moral purpose** is Oldfather’s recurring translation of *prohairesis*: the capacity for choosing and the orientation expressed through its use. It is broader than a passing wish. Margaret Graver’s [Stanford account, §4.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/), emphasizes the capacity rather than isolated decisions.

The handbook’s argument can be reconstructed in six moves:

1. Other people and circumstances can obstruct the outcomes we seek.
2. If obtaining an outcome is necessary for living well, our good depends on whoever or whatever can obstruct it.
3. That dependence makes threats, flattery, envy, and resentment powerful.
4. Epictetus locates genuine good in the excellent use of our own agency instead.
5. An external loss need not require dishonest, cruel, or servile conduct.
6. Someone trained to preserve that distinction can act without surrendering their moral standard to the outcome.

The fourth move is a substantive ethical claim. The first three do not prove it. A critic may agree that integrity matters while also holding that health, attachment, and material security are genuine constituents of a good life. Epictetus offers a stronger position: their possession does not constitute moral goodness. Sections 14 and 19 connect that position to freedom from other people’s power over our desires.

**Editorial example:** A grant applicant can research carefully and submit honestly. They can influence a committee by making a better case; they cannot guarantee its decision. Refusing to falsify evidence preserves a standard for their action. It does not establish that losing the grant has no material consequences.

**Takeaway:** Separate responsibility for the quality of an undertaking from a demand that the world deliver its result. Influence still gives reasons to prepare and act.

### Impressions and assent

**An impression is how something initially presents itself to the mind. Assent means accepting what it presents as true.** The gap between them creates a task: examine the apparent meaning before letting it direct conduct.

In §1, Epictetus asks the learner to test a harsh impression rather than accept its appearance immediately. Section 20 recommends gaining time when provoked. Section 45 distinguishes noticing that someone drinks much wine from judging, without understanding their judgment, that they drink badly. The discipline includes restraint in attributing meaning to others’ actions. [§§1, 20, 45.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Epictetus,_the_Discourses_as_reported_by_Arrian,_the_Manual,_and_Fragments/Manual)

Section 5 makes the more sweeping claim that judgments, rather than things themselves, disturb us. Its example is death: Socrates did not judge death as others did. This demonstrates the possibility of differing evaluations. It does not, by itself, establish that every disturbance has one cause or that everyone can change it at will.

**Editorial example:** A colleague does not answer a message. The observed event is an unanswered message. “They despise me” is an interpretation. “I must embarrass them before they embarrass me” adds a proposed response. Checking whether they received the message addresses the uncertainty without pretending there is no relationship to investigate.

The useful practice is to distinguish observation, interpretation, and proposed action. That three-part exercise is editorial. The handbook supplies the demand to examine impressions; it is not a modern experimental account of perception or a clinical treatment manual.

Section 44 offers a related check on inference. Greater wealth does not establish a better person; greater eloquence does not establish better character. A real advantage in one respect can fail to justify the judgment attached to it.

**Takeaway:** Before acting on an impression, state the claim you are accepting. A feeling’s urgency does not supply the missing evidence.

### Desire and action with reservation

**Desire aims to obtain something; aversion aims to avoid something. Acting with reservation means pursuing an appropriate action while acknowledging that its completion depends on conditions beyond one’s agency.** It is not a secret method of ensuring success.

Section 2 advises beginners to redirect aversion toward faults in their own agency and, for the time being, suspend desire. The qualification matters. This is severe training advice addressed to a learner, not a casual instruction to abolish every preference permanently. Action continues, but lightly and with reservation.

Section 4 gives the memorable case of visiting a public bath. The bather should anticipate splashing, jostling, insults, and theft. The undertaking has two aims: bathing and keeping one’s moral purpose in accordance with nature. A disruption can defeat the first aim without making retaliation necessary. [§§2–4.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Epictetus,_the_Discourses_as_reported_by_Arrian,_the_Manual,_and_Fragments/Manual)

**According to nature**, in this philosophy, means living as a rational and social being within the order of the world. It does not mean doing whatever impulse feels spontaneous. Keith Seddon’s [Internet Encyclopedia account, §§3–4](https://iep.utm.edu/epictetu/), places the practical advice within Stoic virtue and divine order.

**Editorial example:** A volunteer intends to deliver supplies before a storm. They check the route, prepare a backup, and follow safe instructions. If a bridge closes, they seek another means of helping. Their reservation concerns the guarantee of arrival, not the seriousness of the commitment. Abandoning preparation because roads are external would misunderstand the distinction.

Section 25 asks a different question about desire: what price buys the social advantage you envy? An invitation may cost praise and attendance on a powerful host. If you refuse that price, remember what you have retained. This can expose a hidden bargain. It does not prove that every distribution of opportunities is fair.

**Takeaway:** Name both the desired result and the standard governing your pursuit. Decide what you will refuse to trade for the result before pressure makes the bargain attractive.

### Roles and appropriate action

**A role is a relationship or position that carries expectations of conduct. Appropriate action is action fitting one’s responsibilities as a rational, social person in a particular situation.** Stoic attention to one’s agency therefore need not mean withdrawal from other people.

Section 24 asks what help a friend or country actually needs. If acquiring money requires becoming unfaithful, the supposed assistance destroys something valuable in the helper. Yet the section permits obtaining money and holding a civic position while preserving integrity. Section 32 says that danger to oneself does not settle whether one should help a friend or country. [§§24, 32.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Epictetus,_the_Discourses_as_reported_by_Arrian,_the_Manual,_and_Fragments/Manual)

That reasoning blocks an easy excuse: “Their welfare is external, so I owe nothing.” What happens to another person is not wholly up to you; how you treat them remains ethically significant.

**Editorial example:** A friend asks you to conceal their dangerous mistake. Loyalty can require helping them correct it and face its consequences. Concealment might preserve their immediate comfort while betraying both the injured person and the friend’s longer-term responsibility.

The handbook also contains a serious limit. Section 30 tells a child to submit even to a father who strikes them; §17 compares life with an assigned theatrical role. Those passages can preserve hierarchical relationships at an unacceptable cost. They must not be quietly translated into modern advice about healthy boundaries. Protecting oneself, leaving an abusive situation, or seeking institutional remedies can be justified; those judgments require an ethical argument beyond obediently applying §30.

**Takeaway:** Ask what a relationship requires of your conduct, then examine whether the claimed requirement is defensible. A role’s existence does not establish the justice of every demand made in its name.

### Practice rather than display

**Practice means bringing a principle into conduct repeatedly, especially when doing so costs something. Display means securing the appearance of understanding or virtue.** They can come apart even in someone who explains the philosophy accurately.

Section 29 compares entering philosophy with preparing for athletic competition. Consider the training, difficulties, and possible defeat before taking on the identity. Section 46 uses digestion: sheep produce wool and milk rather than exhibit their undigested food. The learner should show what their principles produce. Section 49 distinguishes interpreting Chrysippus’s books from living in accordance with their teaching. [§§29, 46, 49.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Epictetus,_the_Discourses_as_reported_by_Arrian,_the_Manual,_and_Fragments/Manual)

Section 52 makes the order explicit. Applying a principle comes first in necessity; explaining its justification and examining proof serve that application. He does not call reasoning worthless. He objects to becoming expert at proving that lying is wrong while continuing to lie.

**Editorial example:** A manager can give a fluent account of accepting criticism and still punish the employee who corrects them. The relevant test arrives in the next correction. Changing the response matters more than improving the presentation about openness.

Practice also requires proportion. The athletic analogy does not establish that greater physical deprivation always produces better character. Section 47’s deliberately austere exercises belong to its historical training regime, not to a general recommendation about health.

**Takeaway:** Choose a recurring occasion on which your stated principle becomes inconvenient. Identify what different conduct would look like there.

## Deep exploration

### A handbook leaves some reasoning outside the book

The short sections make principles portable. They also compress the explanation that would help a reader judge them. The *Discourses*, a longer record of teaching associated with Arrian, often supplies context absent from the handbook. Neither work should be treated as an unmediated recording of every historical utterance. [Stanford Encyclopedia, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/), discusses the attribution and warns about reading the epitome in isolation.

Our core lesson supplies an order of argument across sections that are often exhortations rather than continuous proofs. That order is editorial. The handbook itself moves among insults, meals, bereavement, social ambition, reasoning, and religious commitment. Its form tries to make a judgment available at the moment of temptation.

This helps explain its seminal character. It transmits an unusually concentrated program for connecting judgments about value with everyday choices. Oldfather’s introduction also documents the later commentary by Simplicius and Christian paraphrases. Those are concrete signs of reception, not evidence that all later practices of self-examination descend from this book or that its ethical theory is true.

### Does an external loss really leave the person unharmed?

Epictetus’s position depends on the meaning of good and harm. **Virtue** means excellence of judgment and character; **vice** means their corruption. When he treats external events as neither good nor evil, he is assigning them a place in that moral ranking. He is not reporting that a blow leaves no bruise or that a confiscated farm still belongs to its former holder.

But clarification alone does not remove the objection. In §§3 and 11, the mortality of a child and the image of returning what was lent are meant to transform loss. A reader may find mortality worth remembering while rejecting the inference that grief is an erroneous evaluation. Loving relationships can be valuable partly because particular people are irreplaceable. Their loss may damage a life without demonstrating defective character.

The dispute is philosophical: does living well consist entirely in the excellent use of agency, or can it also depend on vulnerable goods? The grant applicant may preserve integrity and still lose an opportunity genuinely important to their life. We need not deny the achievement to recognize the deprivation.

Section 16 complicates the caricature of the cold Stoic. Oldfather’s text says:

> “Do not, however, hesitate to sympathize with him so far as words go”

Epictetus allows joining another person’s outward lament while warning against inwardly adopting their judgment of external evil. The distinction supports companionship, but it raises another objection: can sympathetic words ring hollow if the speaker judges the mourner’s evaluation mistaken? A charitable reading sees steadiness that helps someone remain present. A critical reading asks whether the theory permits enough acknowledgment of what the person has lost. Both readings must face the actual qualification in §16.

**Editorial use:** Attend to the grieving person before making their loss into an exercise in your philosophy. This is our ethical recommendation, not an alternative translation of the handbook.

### Agency is not an unlimited mental command

Section 9 distinguishes a bodily impairment from damage to moral purpose. That can challenge the assumption that disability determines a person’s moral worth. It does not establish that every mental event is voluntarily controllable, or that pain, trauma, and illness leave the capacity for judgment untouched.

The handbook offers a demanding account of agency and training. It gives no clinical evidence about depression, neurological injury, or the treatment of trauma. Using its categorical language to blame someone for symptoms would convert a philosophical ideal into an unsupported medical claim.

Even its practical exercises acknowledge development. Section 20 recommends delay; §48 describes a person making progress as someone recovering who must avoid disturbing what has not yet healed. Improvement takes attention, rather than one successful command to feel differently.

**Editorial exercise:** After a setback, write down what happened, what you presently believe it means, and one next action available with your actual support and capacities. If the action requires help, include the help. This modest exercise borrows a distinction from the text; it does not validate the book’s universal claims or replace treatment.

### Providence and the problem of accepting an assigned world

**Providence is the belief that a divine intelligence orders events.** It is explicit in §31’s account of the gods and in §53’s closing appeal to Zeus and Destiny. The world is not merely a random setting in which Epictetus happens to recommend composure.

Section 8 asks the reader to wish events to happen as they do. Section 17 assigns the role to the playwright. These images make sense within confidence in an ordered whole. A secular reader can adopt a limited distinction between agency and outcome while withholding that confidence. The resulting practice is a selective adaptation, not the whole original philosophy.

The political problem remains. A role can be unjustly imposed. The handbook speaks from within a world of slavery and male household authority; §§12, 30, 33, and 40 expose those assumptions. Epictetus’s experience of enslavement does not turn every use of slavery as a metaphor into an adequate theory of emancipation. Inner resistance and legal freedom answer different questions.

**Editorial example:** An employee can refuse to lie while also organizing to change a rule that rewards lying. Accepting that yesterday’s instruction occurred does not settle whether tomorrow’s arrangement should remain. The handbook helps examine participation; institutional reform requires additional inquiry into power, incentives, and rights.

This is a limit of treating the work as sufficient social philosophy, rather than a reason to discard every exercise in judgment it contains.

### Where this meets other entries

[Plato’s Apology on care of the soul](/seminal-ideas/apology/#care-of-the-soul-and-the-order-of-goods) gives a close comparison: injustice can damage the agent in a way that another person’s threats cannot compel. Here the connection is also textual. Epictetus invokes Socrates in §§5, 33, 46, and 51, and §53 ends with sayings associated with him. This establishes an explicit model within the handbook; it does not make every Stoic commitment a doctrine of Plato’s courtroom speaker.

[The Dhammapada on training the mind](/seminal-ideas/the-dhammapada/#training-the-mind) also asks what practice changes in our response. Compare its [warning about recitation without practice](/seminal-ideas/the-dhammapada/#practice-and-the-limits-of-borrowed-knowledge) with §§46 and 52. These are editorial comparisons. Buddhist liberation and Stoic life according to divine nature have distinct accounts of persons and ultimate aims; resemblance does not establish transmission between the texts.

[Follett on responsibility without servility](/seminal-ideas/the-giving-of-orders/#responsibility-without-servility) shifts attention toward the organization of shared work. Epictetus asks what another person’s demand can make you choose. Follett asks how people can inquire together into what should be done. Reading them together keeps personal integrity and the conditions of coordination in view without asserting historical influence.

### Common distortions

- **“You can control everything inside your head.”** The account of moral agency is not evidence for unrestricted command over symptoms, sensations, or every thought.
- **“Externals do not matter, so do nothing.”** Sections 24 and 32 require action for others; its quality matters even when its outcome is uncertain.
- **“Stoicism teaches that suffering is the victim’s fault.”** The handbook’s account of evaluative disturbance must not be used to erase another person’s wrongdoing or real injury.
- **“Acceptance proves an institution is just.”** Recognizing an event and endorsing its continuation are different judgments. The handbook’s own hierarchical assumptions deserve criticism.
- **“Never feel affection or accompany grief.”** Section 16 permits sympathy. Its restrictive account of inward response remains open to objection.
- **“The book is a secular, scientifically established method.”** Providence is explicit. Practical plausibility, philosophical argument, and empirical validation are separate matters.
- **“Epictetus invented Stoicism.”** He taught within a much older school. His distinctive force does not make the entire tradition his invention.

### Test your understanding

1. How can an outcome be external while preparation remains important?
2. Which premise goes beyond the observation that outcomes can be obstructed?
3. Distinguish an unanswered message, its interpretation, and an action based on it.
4. Why does action with reservation differ from making a halfhearted effort?
5. What prevents the distinction about externals from automatically excusing neglect of a friend?
6. What does §16 permit, and what does it continue to restrict?
7. Why is protecting agency different from proving that illness never impairs judgment?
8. How does §52 place reasoning in relation to conduct?

### If you read only a little

Use [Oldfather’s numbered Manual](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Epictetus,_the_Discourses_as_reported_by_Arrian,_the_Manual,_and_Fragments/Manual). The following sequence samples its central demands and their hardest cases.

1. **§§1–5:** Identify the opening distinction, the advice to beginners, the bath, and the claim about judgments. Separate practical exercises from universal promises.
2. **§§9–11 and 16:** Put impairment, loss, and sympathy together. What can be retained from the distinction about moral worth, and what would you reject about grief?
3. **§§24–25 and 30–32:** Follow assistance, social costs, family duty, and danger. Locate the passage that supports helping others and the passage you would resist applying to abuse.
4. **§§44–46 and 49–53:** Test the inferences about superiority, observation, and practice. End with the religious commitments rather than skipping them as decorative poetry.

At the next disrupted undertaking, name the result you still have reason to pursue and the conduct you would refuse to use to obtain it. Whether you can keep that distinction when the result matters is the handbook’s practical test.
