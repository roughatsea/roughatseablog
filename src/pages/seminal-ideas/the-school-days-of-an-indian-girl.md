The adjacent “Impressions of an Indian Childhood” and “An Indian Teacher Among Indians” are separate works. This is a retrospective autobiographical narrative: remembered events and reconstructed dialogue reach us through an adult writer’s selection and language. ## Orientation

A girl goes East expecting apples and an exciting train ride. On arrival, someone lifts her into the air as though welcoming a small child. She experiences the gesture as an indignity. Her crying brings food, although food is not what she wants.

That scene begins a question running through Zitkala-Sa’s account of schooling: what happens when people with power supply their own explanation of someone else’s needs and cannot hear the correction?

Zitkala-Sa, also known as Gertrude Simmons Bonnin, was a Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and advocate. Her essay follows an eager child through bewildering rules, forced hair cutting, punishment, illness, estrangement at home, and later success in public speaking. The girl acquires abilities. The acquisition does not cancel the costs.

**The lasting question:** Can an institution call its work education if gaining its approval requires a child’s existing relationships and ways of life to count as obstacles?

**The essential limit:** This is one writer’s testimony and literary construction, not a statistical survey of boarding schools or a complete account of all Indigenous experiences. Nor does its criticism imply that literacy, learning across cultures, or personal ambition is inherently a betrayal.

## Core lesson

### Education and forced assimilation

**Education develops abilities and understanding. Assimilation means adopting the practices of a dominant society; forced assimilation makes abandoning existing practices a condition imposed by power.** These are this guide’s working definitions. The essay asks readers to distinguish them by showing what happens to a child, rather than beginning with a formal theory.

In section I, “The Land of Red Apples,” the promised destination belongs to the children’s imagination of abundance and freedom. The actual journey exposes them to strangers who stare and point. At the school, the narrator’s fear and wish to stand on her own feet do not govern how adults handle her. An older girl who speaks her language supplies comfort that the institution’s bustle has not provided. [I.](#sources)

Section II, “The Cutting of My Long Hair,” makes the loss of control unmistakable. The narrator explains the meanings she has learned for different forms of short hair. The planned cutting therefore threatens something more than a preferred appearance. Judéwin, a companion who knows some English, warns her. She hides beneath a bed, is found, restrained in a chair, and shorn despite resistance.

Her account contrasts this treatment with her mother’s practice of reasoning with her. The comparison undermines an assumption that schooling brings reason to a previously unreasoning life. Here the people administering the school use force where the child remembers explanation. [II.](#sources)

The reasoning we can reconstruct is:

1. A child arrives with relationships, language, expectations, and standards of dignity.
2. School practices can override those standards without attempting to understand them.
3. The adults control both the rule and the means of enforcing it.
4. Compliance under those conditions cannot establish that the child accepted the rule’s meaning or benefit.
5. Teaching a useful ability therefore does not, by itself, justify the entire arrangement through which it was taught.

The fifth step is an editorial evaluation of the narrative’s challenge. The essay does not prove that every shared rule is oppressive. It makes the justification of a rule answer to its purpose, means, and effects on the person required to obey.

**Editorial example:** A language program might help students acquire English while also allowing them to speak with family in their first language. Another might treat that first language as shameful and punish its use. Both could report improved English scores. Those scores alone would not distinguish the two educational projects. This is an imagined comparison, not a claim about a particular current school.

### Meaning before judgment

**Interpretation is deciding what an action or expression means.** A person with authority can punish an interpretation as though it were an observed fact.

Section II’s breakfast scene gives a small example. Bells coordinate standing, sitting, prayer, and eating. The child tries to infer their meanings from others’ movements and repeatedly gets the sequence wrong. A familiar routine to insiders is an unexplained test to a newcomer. Her uncertainty does not show an inability to learn; it shows that the procedure has not become intelligible to her. [II.](#sources)

In section III, “The Snow Episode,” the stakes rise. After the girls forget an order about playing in snow, Judéwin coaches them to answer a reprimand with the English word “No.” Her advice is mistaken. Thowin repeats the word in response to a question about future obedience and receives further blows. When the adult changes the question to whether she will fall in snow again, the same answer finally fits.

Notice the mismatch. For the adult, the answer can sound like refusal. For the child, it is the only answer available. More punishment does not supply the missing understanding. The scene gives readers access to the gap because the narrator tells us what the girls rehearsed before the questioning began. [III.](#sources)

The narrator cautiously imagines that the woman may have recognized the failure of force. That speculation is not access to the woman’s mind. The observable change in the account is that the beating stops and the other girls are not punished.

**Editorial example:** A supervisor asks a new employee, “You haven’t submitted this yet?” The employee has learned that a nod signals politeness and nods without understanding the negative question. Treating the nod as an admission of negligence would confuse a communication failure with a settled account of conduct. The appropriate next step is to establish comprehension. The analogy concerns interpretation; an adult workplace disagreement is not equivalent in vulnerability or historical setting to the punishment of these children.

**Takeaway:** Before judging refusal, distinguish what someone heard, what they could express, and what you inferred. Understanding a rule, agreeing with it, and being able to comply are separate questions.

### Routine and the disappearance of need

**An institutional routine is a repeated way of organizing many people’s actions.** Routines can coordinate necessary work. Trouble begins when following the procedure becomes sufficient evidence that the people within it are receiving what they need.

In section V, “Iron Routine,” bells regulate waking and assembly. A pencil records lateness whether delay accompanies a headache or a serious cough. The narrator contrasts the speed of classification with the lack of time for attention to its cause. She recalls continuing through the day while ill because she does not expect an appeal to be heard. [V.](#sources)

The section then recounts a classmate’s death. The narrator censures neglected physical illness and the undifferentiated dispensing of medicine to differently ailing children. This is her accusation as a witness remembering the school. The narrative does not identify a medically established cause of death or allow us to calculate what a particular intervention would have changed.

Her description of the caregiver complicates a simple villain story: she can be hardworking and well-meaning while also ignorant and cruel in effect. Intention and adequacy are different measures. Even the child’s feelings change: after feeling better, she can smile again at someone she had blamed. That moment preserves an unstable relationship rather than a clean division between permanent hatred and forgiveness.

Reconstructed as a practical argument:

1. A record captures a selected feature, such as punctuality.
2. The selected feature can have several causes.
3. A procedure that reacts identically to those causes can miss consequential differences.
4. Efficient completion of the record therefore cannot establish adequate care.

**Editorial example:** A tutoring service logs every missed session as lack of motivation. Some absences concern transport, some illness, and some a student’s decision not to attend. The same mark can conceal different problems requiring different responses. The lesson supplies a question to investigate; it supplies no empirical finding about tutoring services.

**Takeaway:** Ask what a measure records and what decisions require knowledge beyond that measure. A register can be accurate about absence while inadequate as an explanation.

### Resistance and its costs

**Agency is the capacity to act and affect events.** The essay shows agency persisting under constraint, without making every resistant act wise or every attempt successful.

Hiding from the scissors is direct refusal. The end of section III offers a different response: ordered to mash turnips as punishment, the child continues after they are already pulped until the jar’s bottom breaks. She experiences the absence of turnips at dinner as a small victory, while admitting some regret about the broken vessel. [II–III.](#sources)

An adult reader can recognize why this act matters without turning destruction into a general educational ideal. It changes a small outcome and lets the girl experience herself as effective. It does not give her control over the school or make the original communication problem disappear.

Section IV, “The Devil,” brings the struggle into imagination. A teacher connects a frightening pictured devil with punishment for disobedient girls. The child dreams of him pursuing her in her mother’s home. In the morning, she attacks the picture with a slate pencil. The figure comes from a lesson; the fear travels beyond the classroom; her retaliation returns to the page. [IV.](#sources)

This sequence makes the reach of institutional authority visible without requiring us to endorse the child’s understanding of how an image works. The adult writer can recount an earlier belief without presenting it as a present claim about supernatural causation.

**Editorial reading exercise:** For each act of resistance, ask three questions: What can the child change? What remains beyond her control? What might the act cost? This avoids making her either entirely powerless or magically liberated by refusal.

### Achievement without belonging

**Recognition means being acknowledged by others; belonging involves a more enduring sense of relationship and acceptance.** A prize can provide recognition without settling where someone can live without estrangement.

Section VI, “Four Strange Summers,” complicates the idea that leaving school simply restores home. The narrator connects her turmoil both to her eastern schooling and to adolescence. Her brother still treats her as a younger child. Other young people have adopted English conversation and clothing associated with school, while she lacks the clothes for their gathering. The division is present within her home community as well as between school and home. [VI.](#sources)

Her mother offers a Bible to comfort a daughter who can now read. The narrator rejects its help; later she hears her mother grieving for her. Their difficulty does not establish that her mother lacks love, or that literacy itself prevents understanding. It shows a relationship in which the tools of consolation no longer reach the particular distress.

In section VII, “Incurring My Mother’s Displeasure,” she pursues further study against her mother’s wishes. The college initially brings loneliness and prejudice. Then classmates give flowers and celebrate her victory in a speaking contest. She acknowledges this friendliness as a correction to her own hard feelings. The text lets new evidence modify an earlier judgment. [VII.](#sources)

A later contest among colleges brings both racist mockery and a prize. These are two competitions: she wins first place at her college, while the subsequent event gives two prizes and she receives one. The essay does not identify that second award here as first place.

The final scene withdraws from public triumph. Alone, she thinks of her mother, and the recognition does not satisfy her. This ending challenges a convenient accounting in which a successful student’s prize retrospectively pays for every injury along the way.

**Takeaway:** Evaluate an educational success through several questions: What abilities grew? What treatment occurred? Which relationships were damaged or sustained? What choices became available? One favorable answer does not settle the others.

## Deep exploration

### How autobiography makes an argument

**Retrospective narration means telling earlier experience from a later standpoint.** The child encounters stairs as a strange upward structure; the adult supplies their ordinary name. The child struggles with English; the writer addresses readers in accomplished English. The distance between those standpoints is part of the essay’s force.

The form also organizes comparisons. An affectionate-seeming welcome becomes frightening from the recipient’s perspective. A routine understood by adults becomes bewildering when followed through a child’s attempts to interpret it. A public prize becomes insufficient when placed beside a private scene.

These reversals do argumentative work. They ask readers accustomed to the institution’s explanation to judge it against an experience it discounts. The essay does not consist merely of an assertion that schooling was painful.

Yet testimony is not a recording device. Dialogue has been selected and reconstructed. The narrator distinguishes some memories from gaps: she explicitly cannot recall the journey’s meals, and does not know what made the searchers look beneath the bed. Taking those limits seriously strengthens rather than disqualifies the reading. A writer can claim knowledge of what happened to her without claiming knowledge of every cause or thought.

For historical inquiry, compare such testimony with administrative records, other students’ accounts, correspondence, and material evidence. Those additional sources could corroborate, complicate, or correct particulars. This guide has not conducted that wider archival investigation. Its close reading establishes how this published account reasons, not a complete history of the school system.

### A consequential early critique

The [National Humanities Center’s bibliographic guide](https://www.nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/gilded/people/text10/text10link.htm) dates the essay’s magazine publication to February 1900. That matters: this criticism reached a national periodical through an Indigenous writer’s account, rather than existing only as a later observer’s judgment.

The [National Park Service’s contextual account](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/learning-from-zitk%C3%A1la-%C5%A1%C3%A1.htm) situates her schooling at White’s Indiana Manual Labor Institute and her later teaching at Carlisle. These are different institutions and roles. Her childhood school experience should not silently become an account of childhood attendance at Carlisle.

NPS describes assimilation through restrictions on language, dress, and customary practices, and connects her writing to advocacy. This context helps explain why the essay belongs in a catalogue of formative ideas: it makes the alleged beneficiary’s account a source for evaluating an institution’s civilizing claims. That statement of intellectual importance is this guide’s judgment. No documented chain from this particular essay to a later policy or thinker is asserted.

### The strongest objection: education did give her power

An objection deserves more than a ceremonial acknowledgment. The author’s ability to publish the essay in English, compete as an orator, and acquire further learning is real within the account. Some classmates respond with generosity. She returns to study rather than abandoning it. Would an emphasis on injury deny her ambition and the benefits she gained?

It would, if the interpretation required every educational result to be worthless. The better distinction is between a valuable ability and the necessity or justice of the conditions attached to acquiring it. The fact that someone uses a skill against an institution’s assumptions does not establish that humiliation was required to teach the skill.

A further question remains open: what alternatives were actually available to this girl and her family? Judging those constrained choices requires more historical evidence than the essay alone provides. Moral criticism can reject coercion without pretending that families faced an easy menu of equally funded, equally accessible possibilities.

Our editorial evaluation favors education that adds usable capacities while allowing students to maintain relationships and exercise judgment about inherited practices. That is a normative position: a view about what should count as good education. The text does not experimentally prove which institutional design best achieves it.

### Respecting culture without freezing a person inside it

The opposite distortion makes home entirely harmonious and departure entirely passive. Section VI refuses that simplification. The narrator feels restless, pursues possibilities, and does not always respond generously to others’ concern. Section VII shows her defying her mother as well as confronting prejudice.

A defense of cultural continuity need not require every member to repeat every practice. A person might value a language, change a belief, pursue a profession, and disagree with relatives. The important question is whether change can be chosen and discussed without an authority making cultural rejection the price of access.

Section VII’s account of the protective roots she carries also complicates any simple contrast between one side’s reason and the other’s superstition. The adult recalls her own earlier reliance on a charm and eventual loss of faith. The essay is more searching than a demand that one group’s inherited explanations escape all scrutiny.

### Compare distinct arguments

[Du Bois on double consciousness](/seminal-ideas/the-souls-of-black-folk/#double-consciousness) considers the pressure of seeing oneself through a society’s contempt. Zitkala-Sa’s contests bring public achievement into contact with racial humiliation. This is a thematic comparison, not an assertion that their experiences or concepts are identical, or that either work borrowed from the other.

[Wollstonecraft on reason and virtue](/seminal-ideas/a-vindication-of-the-rights-of-woman/#reason-and-virtue) connects education with the development of judgment. The breakfast and punishment scenes here ask whether an institution is making its rules intelligible to its pupils. Their historical subjects differ; a shared concern about judgment does not erase those differences.

[Gilman on resistance without recovery](/seminal-ideas/the-yellow-wall-paper/#resistance-without-recovery) helps separate an effective refusal from a restored life. Gilman constructs a fictional journal; Zitkala-Sa offers retrospective autobiography. Similar questions about authority do not make their kinds of evidence interchangeable.

### Common distortions

- **“The essay attacks learning itself.”** The narrator pursues further education and values achievements; the account challenges what acquiring them is made to cost.
- **“She went willingly, so later coercion was authorized.”** An eager expectation of apples and travel cannot establish informed acceptance of every subsequent practice.
- **“A haircut means the same thing to everyone.”** Section II supplies the meanings the narrator had learned. They must not be generalized into a single rule for every Indigenous people.
- **“Thowin refuses to obey.”** The reader knows her answer was rehearsed without sufficient understanding of the questions.
- **“All the adults are malicious.”** Section V explicitly allows good intentions alongside damaging ignorance.
- **“All resistance succeeds.”** Hiding fails to prevent the cutting; breaking the jar produces a limited victory.
- **“Winning proves the institution was justified.”** Ability, fair treatment, and belonging remain separately assessable.
- **“Home offers a perfectly recoverable past.”** The later sections depict change within the community, adolescence, disagreement, and continuing ambition.

### Test your understanding

1. Why does the narrator’s account of hair matter to evaluating the cutting?
2. What does the reader know about Thowin’s answer that the punishing adult misses?
3. How can a punctuality record be accurate and still support a bad decision?
4. What changes when the child breaks the turnip jar, and what does not?
5. Why should the two speaking competitions be kept separate?
6. Which evidence resists a claim that all classmates remain uniformly hostile?
7. Does using English to criticize assimilation justify forced assimilation?
8. What can this autobiography establish that a survey cannot, and what can it not establish alone?

### If you read only a little

Use the seven numbered sections within [“The School Days of an Indian Girl” in Gutenberg’s collection](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10376/pg10376.html). Do not start at the collection’s first section I and assume it is this essay.

1. **I, “The Land of Red Apples,” and II, “The Cutting of My Long Hair”:** Set each adult action beside the meaning it has for the child. Track who can explain, who can understand, and who decides.
2. **III, “The Snow Episode”:** Read through the turnip jar. Compare misunderstood speech with a later act performed in partial understanding.
3. **IV, “The Devil,” and V, “Iron Routine”:** Follow a lesson into a dream, then a schedule into illness. Ask how authority reaches beyond its immediate instruction.
4. **VI, “Four Strange Summers”:** Keep the references to adolescence and changes within the community. They prevent a simple school-versus-home diagram from replacing the text.
5. **VII, “Incurring My Mother’s Displeasure”:** Mark both contests, the welcome as well as the insult, and the shift from audience to solitude in the last paragraph.

Return to the opening welcome after reading the ending. The girl wanted to stand on her own feet. The accomplished speaker can command a stage. The essay requires the reader to ask what kind of freedom exists between those two moments.
