## Orientation

A shop window breaks. The glazier gets paid. Someone watching concludes that the accident has helped the economy by creating work.

Frédéric Bastiat asks us to stay with the shopkeeper. What would the money have bought if the window had remained intact? A pair of shoes, perhaps. The glazier’s customer is visible. The shoemaker’s missing customer never arrives to explain the loss.

The question travels beyond accidents. A public project employs builders; machinery removes a task; a loan guarantee helps someone obtain equipment. Each produces a story with identifiable beneficiaries or casualties. Each also changes possibilities elsewhere.

Bastiat’s short essay makes those alternatives harder to forget. It also makes stronger claims: spending is redirected rather than increased, savings find productive employment, displaced labor finds other work. Those conclusions require assumptions about available resources, competition, timing, and institutions. We should examine them with the same care he demands of his opponents.

**The lasting question:** Compared with what feasible alternative, and for whom, is this an improvement?

**The essential limit:** Identifying a cost does not establish that a project is worthless. Imagining an unseen effect does not establish that it happened. The essay teaches a discipline of comparison; its examples do not supply a universal employment forecast.

## Core lesson

### A missing alternative and opportunity cost

**Opportunity cost is the value of the best feasible alternative forgone when a choice uses a resource.** An hour, a plot of land, a skilled worker’s attention, or a sum of money can have competing uses. Choosing one can prevent another. This modern term helps organize Bastiat’s reasoning; we need not pretend it is the English phrase he uses.

The introduction contrasts an immediately apparent effect with effects that require foresight. But the essay’s examples concern more than delayed consequences. A purchase prevented today can be unseen today. The important absence is often a missing alternative, not simply an event far in the future.

**A counterfactual is an account of what would have happened under a specified alternative.** It gives the comparison a shape. “Without the expenditure” is still incomplete: would the resources have funded another project, remained unused, been saved, or gone to different people?

An editorial reconstruction of Bastiat’s recurring argument runs as follows:

1. Identify the visible recipient and the benefit being claimed.
2. Trace the money and the real resources supporting that benefit.
3. Ask what their owners or users would otherwise have done.
4. Include people whose transactions or opportunities are prevented.
5. Compare what the two arrangements provide, rather than count activity in only one.

**Editorial example:** A museum assigns its only conservator to prepare a spectacular new display. The opening draws praise. Treating the conservator’s time as free hides the restoration postponed elsewhere. Yet listing every conceivable restoration would exaggerate the cost: the relevant alternative is the best one actually available under the same constraints.

This framework disciplines attention. It cannot determine the alternative’s value without further judgment and evidence. An alternative that sounds desirable but could never have been undertaken is a poor basis for comparison.

### The broken window and net wealth

**Wealth here means useful goods and capacities available to people. Net change means the result after relevant losses as well as gains are included.** Payments and work are part of the story; their existence does not by itself show an increase in wealth.

In section I, [“The Broken Window,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c1) Bastiat imagines a child breaking James B.’s window. Repair costs six francs. The glazier receives income and performs useful work. Bastiat accepts that visible benefit. His objection concerns the conclusion drawn from it.

Without the breakage, James could retain his window and buy something else. With the breakage, his money restores the window and the other purchase disappears. The proper comparison is therefore an intact window plus the additional good against a restored window alone. Repair has value after the damage; that does not make causing the damage beneficial.

The missing third participant is the producer who would have supplied the additional good. This person is easy to overlook because the transaction never occurs. A photograph of busy repairers cannot show the canceled purchase beside it.

**Editorial example:** A community hall has set aside money for new chairs. Vandalism forces it to spend that money replacing a door. The carpenter does necessary, paid work. The hall ends with a usable door and old chairs rather than a usable door and new chairs. Celebrating recovery is compatible with recognizing a loss.

The reasoning assumes that the alternative purchase was feasible. If the carpenter would otherwise have lacked work, that changes the employment comparison. It does not recreate the destroyed door or establish that destruction was the best way to provide work. A serious alternative might employ the carpenter on something additional and useful.

**Takeaway:** Separate three questions: Did someone receive income? Was the repair worth doing after damage occurred? Was the damage itself beneficial? An affirmative answer to either of the first two does not answer the third.

### Public spending and the value of its service

**A public service is an activity supplied through public institutions; taxation raises resources from people under legal authority.** Bastiat asks what a service supplies in return for its cost. Counting its employees’ wages cannot settle that question.

In III, [“Taxes,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c3) he explicitly allows that a useful official can provide a valuable service in exchange for payment. In II, “The Disbanding of Troops,” he puts the need for security ahead of an employment argument. Necessary defense may justify expenditure; the soldiers’ purchases alone do not establish that necessity.

V, [“Public Works,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c5) carries the distinction to roads and other construction. If a road is useful, argue from that use. Merely paying people to dig and refill holes has not created the same benefit. Private spending prevented by a tax may also have supported workers.

Here is the central inference, reconstructed: a wage paid through one channel is a real receipt, but it cannot automatically be counted as an addition to society’s resources if the payment removes an alternative use elsewhere. To justify the project, specify the additional service or outcome that makes the redirection worthwhile.

**Editorial example:** A town proposes drainage works and advertises the construction jobs. Those jobs matter to their recipients. The project’s case also requires the flooding it may prevent, the land and maintenance it needs, the burdens of financing, and the best competing use of those resources. Benefits to households beyond the direct purchasers belong in the comparison too. Such effects on others are often called **external effects**. This is a decision example, not a cost-benefit estimate.

Bastiat’s IV, “Theatres, Fine Arts,” helps expose another shortcut. Disputing a subsidy does not amount to disliking art. Equally, showing that public support has a cost does not show that the artistic or educational benefit is smaller. His attack on the claim that arts employment alone justifies support should be distinguished from an evaluation of every possible cultural purpose.

His broader assertion that public expenditure only displaces an equal quantity of private employment is stronger than this comparison rule. It assumes that the alternative spending would occur and employ resources on the relevant timescale. Even V allows that temporary public works in an exceptional crisis may be useful. That qualification does not turn Bastiat into a modern theorist of recession policy, but it matters to an accurate reading.

**Takeaway:** Replace “it creates jobs” with a fuller account: which people gain work, what is produced, what is given up, who bears the cost, and why the result is worth it.

### Machinery and the speed of adjustment

**Productivity means output relative to an input, such as the goods produced in an hour. Displacement means losing a particular use or position; adjustment means the process of finding a different one.** Increased productivity and a displaced worker’s welfare are connected questions, not identical outcomes.

In VIII, [“Machinery,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c8) Bastiat considers an employer who uses an invention to accomplish with one worker what previously required two. One worker loses that job. The employer also saves a franc. Bastiat argues that spending the saving creates demand for work elsewhere. Competition can subsequently pass the saving to consumers through lower prices, leaving them able to purchase other things.

His argument does not require sales of the original product to expand enough to rehire everyone. He specifically rejects that as the only defense of machinery. If hats become cheaper, people can buy the same number of hats and use the saving elsewhere. The possible gain is more satisfaction from the same total effort.

But Bastiat treats the meeting of an available worker and available spending with extraordinary confidence. That is the vulnerable step. A worker trained for one occupation may lack the skills, location, health, or time needed for another. A saving spent elsewhere need not reach that person, or arrive promptly. The essay’s reasoning about a possible aggregate gain supplies no measurement of those difficulties.

**Editorial example:** A warehouse automates a task and customers eventually pay less for deliveries. Some use the saving for meals out. That possibility cannot establish that a dismissed warehouse worker can become a cook, that nearby restaurants will hire, or that the worker can afford months without wages. Each link requires evidence.

Compare [Smith on repetitive work and public education](/seminal-ideas/the-wealth-of-nations/#repetitive-work-and-public-education). Productive organization can enlarge total possibilities while creating responsibilities toward the people inside it. This is an editorial comparison, not a claim that Smith’s educational remedy answers every displacement problem.

**Takeaway:** Ask separately about total output, the distribution of benefits, the duration of losses, and the means of adjustment. A potential gain is not compensation already received.

### Credit, saving, and real resources

**Credit lets someone obtain resources now on a promise of repayment. A guarantee promises to bear an obligation if the borrower does not pay. Saving means leaving some income unconsumed; what happens next depends on how it is held and used.** **Productive capital means equipment and other resources used to produce goods or services.** These definitions help keep financial claims distinct from the equipment, materials, and work they can command.

IX, [“Credit,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c9) reduces the problem to one plough and two would-be borrowers. Without a state guarantee, the owner lends to John; with one, he lends to James. James’s improved access is visible. John’s lost access and the taxpayers’ new exposure are less conspicuous. One existing plough has been reassigned; two have not appeared.

That example establishes a constraint, not the effect of every credit institution. Bastiat himself recognizes that credit arrangements help lenders and borrowers find one another. His general conclusion additionally assumes that the available capital is already employed. A guarantee that changes risk-bearing, access, or future production needs a fuller investigation. The question is whether additional useful activity occurs, at what risk and cost, compared with what would otherwise happen.

XI, [“Frugality and Luxury,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c11) turns to a spendthrift and a careful saver. The spendthrift’s purchases are easy to see. The saver’s resources can reach workers through gifts, loans, or the people selling assets. Bastiat wants readers to trace those routes before declaring saving hostile to employment.

His short formulation is:

> “To save is to spend.”

Within his example, the saving is placed with others or exchanged for assets, and someone else spends. It is not buried indefinitely. The qualification is central. Purchasing existing land transfers ownership; it does not itself demonstrate that new productive equipment was built. Nor has the example established when proceeds will be spent or that all savings finance useful investment.

**Editorial example:** A loan guarantee lets a workshop obtain a scarce machine. If another workshop loses access, the announced loan total overstates the addition. If financing instead enables an otherwise idle manufacturer to build another machine, the comparison changes. Investigate both the additional production and the guarantee’s risk; the announcement alone proves neither success nor failure.

**Takeaway:** Trace finance through to real resources and credible alternatives. A new financial promise is not automatically a new machine, but financial arrangements can affect what machines are subsequently made.

## Deep exploration

### The third person in the story

The essay’s most memorable device is not a technical formula. It is an absent person. The observer sees the glazier and the shopkeeper, the subsidized producer and the government, or the successful borrower and the lender. Bastiat repeatedly asks who else must enter the scene.

VII, [“Restrictions,”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c7) imagines a domestic iron producer seeking to exclude cheaper Belgian iron. The producer’s increased receipts and employment are visible. Customers must now pay more for the same iron, leaving less for other purchases. Bastiat also traces the domestic production that could have been exchanged for imports. The comparison must include those activities, not treat foreign goods as material obtained without payment.

The enduring question is who benefits from a rule and whose alternatives it narrows. A protected firm’s survival does not alone establish a gain for everyone. But the essay’s simplified example does not settle every proposed trade restriction: a case based on security, environmental damage, or learning would need its own evidence and comparison. Naming such purposes does not establish that a particular restriction achieves them.

VI, “The Intermediates,” makes a related point about work that disappears from view. Removing the merchant does not eliminate the need to transport, store, or obtain information about grain. Consumers, private specialists, or public officials must still perform those functions. This is a useful challenge to an apparently effortless saving. It is not proof that every existing middleman deserves every fee, or that competition is effective in every market.

**Editorial exercise:** For a proposed change, write three short accounts: the direct beneficiary’s, the payer’s, and the person whose opportunity may disappear. Then ask whether any affected people remain outside all three accounts. A tidy cast of characters is a prompt for investigation, not a complete social inventory.

### When a fixed-resource example meets an idle economy

The broken window comparison is especially clear when the lost purchase really would have occurred. The one-plough example is especially clear when no other plough can be supplied. Bastiat often extends that clarity into a general assurance that alternative employment and spending will appear.

The objection is not that resources become unlimited. It is that unused resources, delayed decisions, financing conditions, and the time horizon can change the feasible alternative. If trained builders and equipment are idle, a project need not replace another project one for one. It still consumes materials, time, and administrative capacity, and may create future obligations. “There is slack” and “there are costs” can both be true.

**Fiscal policy** means using government spending and taxation to influence the economy. Mark Horton and Asmaa El-Ganainy’s [IMF primer](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/fiscal-policy) explains why its effects depend on economic conditions, financing, timing, and composition. It also discusses limits to stimulus. This supplies modern context for rejecting a universal one-for-one employment rule; it does not supply an effect estimate for our hypothetical town.

The editorial conclusion is narrower than either slogan. Bastiat is right to demand an alternative. His illustrations have not established that the relevant alternative always consists of immediately equivalent private spending. Conversely, an argument for public spending must explain how it changes actual output or well-being, rather than merely invoke an economy-wide benefit.

### Cost is not a complete theory of justice

Opportunity cost identifies something forgone. It does not tell us whether the original distribution of resources was fair or whose needs deserve priority. A payment can be costly to one person and justified by a larger benefit, an urgent need, or a defensible obligation to another. Those judgments need reasons beyond an expenditure total.

Bastiat does make moral arguments. He favors voluntary decisions and demands justification when taxation prevents someone from choosing a use for earnings. In XII, whose full heading is “He Who Has a Right to Work Has a Right to Profit,” he turns the criticism against protected business as well as demands for public employment. A producer who requires others to guarantee a profitable occupation is also asking society to absorb a cost. His satire should not be flattened into solicitude for every business interest.

X, “Algeria,” exposes another limit. Bastiat tracks French taxes, colonial construction, emigration, and the private activity displaced in France. His challenge to a celebratory spending story is valuable. Yet this is not an adequate account of colonized people’s rights, losses, consent, or political independence. He explicitly sets aside the expenditure’s intrinsic merits before offering general objections. Extending his demand to consider missing people requires asking questions that his own treatment leaves underdeveloped.

**Editorial example:** A port increases commerce and tax revenue while taking homes from people excluded from the decision. Counting construction costs and commercial receipts cannot settle whether the taking was justified. Ask who has standing, what alternatives were available, and what rights constrain the choice. This hypothetical is an ethical extension of the comparison method, not an episode established by Bastiat’s account of Algeria.

### How would we test an unseen effect?

An unseen effect is a claim about the world, not permission to invent a convenient number. Distinguish a demonstrated logical constraint from a hypothesis about behavior. One indivisible machine cannot be in two workshops at once. It does not follow without evidence that a particular guarantee prevented a particular competing purchase.

A modern investigation would specify the outcome, population, timescale, financing, and alternative. To assess an employment claim, distinguish jobs announced from additional jobs relative to that alternative. Check whether work moved between firms or places, whether other spending changed, and whether those changes would have happened anyway. To assess social value, include the service supplied, not just payroll.

[Snow’s account of action under uncertainty](/seminal-ideas/on-the-mode-of-communication-of-cholera/#action-under-uncertainty) supplies a useful methodological comparison. Removing a pump handle and then observing decline does not reveal what the outbreak would otherwise have done. Likewise, observing business activity after a subsidy does not identify the activity caused by it. This is an editorial comparison of reasoning, not a documented influence between authors or an assertion that the two settings admit identical methods.

### Why this essay belongs here

Bastiat turns an abstract demand for comparison into stories that readers can reconstruct and challenge. Its continued teaching life is directly visible in the Online Library of Liberty’s [separately distributed broken-window extract](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/bastiat-the-best-of-bastiat-3-2-the-broken-window) and Liberty Fund’s [2017 collected volume](https://about.libertyfund.org/books/economic-sophisms-and-what-is-seen-and-what-is-not-seen/). These document later editorial circulation; they do not establish that Bastiat invented every associated concept or that all modern economics descends from him.

Liberty Fund’s publisher account also situates his polemics amid free-trade disputes and the upheavals of 1848. Its favorable presentation is useful context, not independent empirical confirmation of the essay’s policy conclusions. Reading beyond the opening parable reveals a writer arguing for voluntary exchange, not a neutral spreadsheet of every possible outcome.

### Common distortions

- **“The unseen means only the distant future.”** A canceled purchase can be a present alternative; timing is only one source of invisibility.
- **“The glazier’s income is imaginary.”** The income is real. It does not establish a net gain from causing damage.
- **“A project with a cost must be rejected.”** A valuable service can justify its cost; the comparison must examine both.
- **“Bastiat says useful officials provide nothing.”** III explicitly recognizes useful public service as an exchange.
- **“Machinery instantly compensates everyone it displaces.”** VIII asserts a confident route from savings to employment; it provides no evidence of automatic individual compensation.
- **“Saving is always immediate new investment.”** XI assumes transmission through other people’s spending. Asset purchases, new production, and timing remain distinct questions.
- **“An unseen loss can be assigned any number.”** A plausible mechanism still needs evidence about whether it operated and how much it mattered.
- **“Accounting for resources settles justice.”** Distribution, rights, and obligations require additional argument.

### Test your understanding

1. Why is a repaired window alone the wrong comparison with an intact window plus shoes?
2. Can a public road have an opportunity cost and still be worth building?
3. Why does Bastiat’s machinery argument not require people to buy twice as many hats?
4. What assumption makes the one-plough example insufficient to evaluate every credit guarantee?
5. Does buying existing land prove that new productive capital was created?
6. What would you need before reporting a subsidy’s announced jobs as additional jobs?

### If you read only a little

Use the essay’s introduction and Roman-numbered sections in the [1874 English reading text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html). The volume contains several other essays; “Government” marks the next work, not another chapter of this one.

1. **Introduction and [I, “The Broken Window”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c1):** Read the complete parable. Write both possible end states and identify the missing supplier. Separate delayed effects from purchases prevented now.
2. **[III, “Taxes”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c3), and [V, “Public Works”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c5):** Mark the allowance for useful public services and the qualification concerning exceptional temporary works. State the employment argument he rejects without erasing those qualifications.
3. **[VII, “Restrictions”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c7), and [VIII, “Machinery”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c8):** Track the customer’s remaining purchasing power. In VIII, underline the step where available money and available labor are presumed to meet. Ask what would delay or prevent it.
4. **[IX, “Credit”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c9), and [XI, “Frugality and Luxury”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c11):** Identify the fixed supply of real resources in one example and the assumed circulation of savings in the other. Distinguish what each example demonstrates from what it presumes.
5. **[X, “Algeria”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c10), and [XII, the right-to-work/right-to-profit comparison](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15962/pg15962-images.html#e2-c12):** Ask whose costs enter the account, whose remain underdeveloped, and where a moral judgment is doing work that arithmetic cannot do.

For the next attractive success story, write down the best feasible alternative before deciding whether to agree. Then identify which parts of both stories you actually know.
