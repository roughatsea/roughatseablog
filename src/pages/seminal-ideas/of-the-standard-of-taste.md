## Orientation

You play a favorite song for a friend. Your friend hears the same recording and finds it dull. Nobody has mistaken which song was played. Is there anything left to discuss?

David Hume’s essay begins from the extraordinary variety of human taste. It also notices that people who declare taste beyond dispute still distinguish perceptive criticism from careless dismissal. A feeling really occurs when someone feels it. Yet a response formed through distraction, unfamiliarity, or hostility need not be as useful a recommendation as one formed through patient attention.

Hume tries to hold those thoughts together. He locates beauty in sentiment, our felt response, while proposing conditions under which a response deserves greater critical authority. Practice, comparison, sensitive discrimination, understanding, and freedom from prejudice matter. His account ends by preserving some differences that even good criticism cannot properly eliminate.

**The lasting question:** What would make a judgment worth learning from, beyond its agreement with your own?

**The essential limit:** Hume offers a philosophical account supported by analogies and observations, not controlled evidence that all properly trained people converge. His cultural hierarchies and religious hostility also give readers reasons to question whether he meets his own standard.

## Core lesson

### Sentiment and critical authority

**A sentiment is a felt response, such as pleasure, admiration, or dislike. Taste is the capacity to respond appreciatively or disapprovingly.** Neither word here means merely fashionable consumption. Hume is examining how people judge writing and other arts, with moral evaluation also entering the argument.

His opening warns that shared praise words can conceal substantive disagreement. Two people may both value simplicity but admire different works as simple. Saying that a work is elegant has not settled which features deserve that word. General agreement about virtue can likewise hide disagreement about particular conduct. [ST 1–5.](https://davidhume.org/texts/empl1/st)

In ST 7, Hume gives the case against a standard its strongest opening: unlike a factual belief, a sentiment does not have to match an independent object to be a real feeling. If a passage delights me, another person cannot disprove that delight merely by disliking the passage. Beauty, in this account, belongs to the mind’s response rather than existing as a freestanding quality waiting to be measured.

But ST 8 introduces a competing conviction. Hume assumes some comparisons between writers are so unequal that treating the responses as equally authoritative would be absurd. His chosen rankings are assertions to examine, not proofs we must inherit. The philosophical problem survives even if we reject his examples: how can we distinguish better and worse appreciation without denying the reality of anyone’s experience?

Our reconstruction separates three claims:

1. A person experienced pleasure or displeasure.
2. The person has noticed and understood the work’s relevant features.
3. The person’s response provides a dependable basis for recommending or ranking the work.

The first claim does not establish the other two. Conversely, discovering that someone missed an important detail does not mean the original feeling never occurred.

**Editorial example:** A listener finds a recording monotonous after hearing it through a phone speaker while answering messages. A second listener identifies changes in rhythm and quiet instrumental exchanges. The second account gives the first listener something to investigate. It does not establish an obligation to enjoy the song after those features become audible.

The useful distinction is between reporting a response and claiming authority for it. Hume’s essay concerns the second task without requiring us to falsify the first.

### Delicacy as discrimination

**Delicacy of taste means noticing small differences and distinguishing elements within a combination.** It is not a synonym for being easily offended, preferring fragile objects, or knowing prestigious names.

Hume illustrates the idea through a story he attributes to *Don Quixote*. Two tasters approve a cask of wine with reservations: one detects leather, the other iron. The cask later yields a key attached to a leather thong. Their apparently eccentric reactions correspond to something other people failed to notice. This lesson follows Hume’s use of the story; it does not treat a fictional anecdote as experimental evidence. [ST 14–17.]

The analogy makes a valuable point. Agreement is not the only sign of perceptiveness. A minority response can disclose a real feature. Good criticism may help an audience hear or see something previously missed.

Hume then proposes a more complicated analogue for art. A critic points to a principle or pattern the dissenter already appreciates in clear examples, and shows how it operates in the disputed work. Recognition can become more discriminating through comparison and demonstration. [ST 16.]

**Editorial example:** A reader calls a scene sentimental because its narrator explicitly names grief. Another reader distinguishes the naming from the scene’s quieter pattern: the character keeps setting out a second cup. Returning to the wording lets both readers ask whether the explanation overwhelms an effective detail. They can disagree about the balance while becoming more precise about what they are judging.

There is also a limit to the wine analogy. Finding iron can settle whether iron was present; identifying repetition in a poem cannot alone settle whether the repetition is powerful or tiresome. A feature can be detectable while its artistic value remains disputed. Hume’s analogy helps explain discrimination more readily than it proves a final ranking.

**Takeaway:** Ask a critic what they noticed, where it occurs, and how it contributes. A verdict becomes educational when it gives another person a way to attend.

### Practice, comparison, and good sense

**Practice is repeated, attentive encounter. Comparison places a work beside alternatives. Good sense is the understanding needed to follow relations, purposes, and reasoning.** These are connected qualifications, rather than separate badges that add up mechanically to expertise.

Hume argues that unfamiliar works initially produce confused responses. Experience helps distinguish kinds and degrees of effect. He also insists that an important individual work should be revisited: a first encounter can obscure how its parts belong together, while a superficially attractive effect can lose force on rereading. [ST 18–19.]

Comparison supplies a scale. Someone who has encountered only one kind of writing may mistake a modest achievement for the highest possible achievement. Hume therefore asks the critic to encounter works admired across periods and nations. His own dismissive description of supposedly unsophisticated audiences in ST 20 requires criticism; the useful demand for a wider comparison does not justify his cultural ranking of people.

Good sense adds something more than exposure. A critic must understand how parts relate and what a work is attempting. Hume distinguishes persuasion, instruction, and imaginative pleasure as purposes of different kinds of writing. He also asks whether fictional people reason and act plausibly within their circumstances. [ST 22.]

**Editorial example:** Two endings leave a conflict unresolved. One has carefully shown that the characters cannot obtain the information needed to decide; the other simply stops before an established confrontation. Calling both endings open does not evaluate them. We need to examine preparation, purpose, and what the refusal of closure achieves.

Rules of composition are consequently answerable to experience. Hume denies that they can be deduced independently of experience, as though a poem were a geometrical proof. His discussion of Ariosto is especially revealing. He initially explains pleasure as persisting despite alleged faults. Then he allows that, if the supposedly faulty feature actually produces the pleasure, the fault may lie in the critical rule. [ST 9.]

That concession prevents the standard from becoming a rigid checklist. A rule is a claim about what contributes to an effect. A successful counterexample should make the rule harder to maintain.

**Editorial exercise:** Choose one feature you habitually condemn, such as repetition or an abrupt change in tone. Find a work where it succeeds. Describe the conditions of that success before revising the rule. This exercise illustrates Hume’s proposal; it does not prove that every convention can succeed anywhere.

### Prejudice and the required point of view

**Prejudice here is a prior attachment or hostility that prevents a work from receiving the kind of attention its situation requires.** Hume names personal friendship, enmity, rivalry, and the limitations of one’s age and country.

An oration addresses particular people under particular circumstances. To judge its persuasion, a later reader must understand what those listeners knew, wanted, feared, and expected. Assessing only whether the speech would persuade us now misses its task. Similarly, liking an author as a friend should not determine a judgment of the author’s writing. [ST 21–22.]

This is an imaginative discipline, not the elimination of all knowledge about context. We may need more context to loosen prejudice. Nor does understanding an intended audience require endorsing everything that audience valued.

**Editorial example:** A satire seems to repeat an absurd position without challenging it. Learning which public slogan it echoes may reveal the target of the joke. The reader can then evaluate its irony. That discovery does not settle whether the joke is humane, effective, or worth making.

Hume’s distinction becomes difficult near the essay’s end. He allows unfamiliar but innocent customs, yet refuses to approve cruelty simply because a period admired it. A work’s treatment of conduct can affect its merit. Depicting wrongdoing and asking the reader to admire wrongdoing are different artistic acts. [ST 31–36.]

The strongest practical reading is to distinguish understanding from assent. Learn enough to understand the work’s invitation; then decide whether accepting that invitation would require a moral response you have reason to reject. Hume’s own prejudices make it unsafe to assume that whatever a critic calls moral clarity is automatically justified.

### The true judge and blameless disagreement

Hume gathers his qualifications into the **true judge**, a critic whose understanding, delicate response, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice make a verdict authoritative. ST 23 names the joint verdict of such critics as the standard. It does not name popularity, professional employment, inherited status, or your favorite reviewer as sufficient qualifications.

Hume immediately raises the hard question: how do we identify these people? He treats their qualifications as matters for factual inquiry and argument, while admitting that the inquiry can be disputed. His standard offers an account of what deserves authority more readily than an easy procedure for finding an unquestionable authority. [ST 24–27.]

Then he preserves differences. Temperament can make one person respond more to tenderness and another to wit. Life stage can change preferred writers. Cultural distance can also alter the strength of a response. Where no defect justifies condemning either side, diversity remains. Hume specifically distinguishes favoring a kind of writing from condemning every other kind. [ST 28–31.]

**Editorial example:** Two readers understand a long, reflective novel’s form and achievement. One prefers a compact comedy for ordinary evening reading. That preference need not imply failure to perceive the novel’s strengths. An account of excellence and a choice of what to enjoy tonight answer different questions.

The essay therefore supports a demanding but limited ambition. Criticism can correct inattention, misunderstanding, and unfair comparisons. It need not produce one compulsory favorite. Exactly how far Hume expects qualified critics to converge remains an interpretive problem, discussed further below.

## Deep exploration

### Reconstruct the argument without hiding its burdens

The essay’s reasoning moves through several kinds of claim. Keeping them distinct makes it easier to assess the result.

| Step | Hume’s proposal | What still needs justification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ST 1–8 | Taste varies, yet we distinguish critical authority. | Our familiar rankings might encode prejudice. |
| ST 9–13 | Shared human tendencies and favorable conditions support principles of appreciation. | Literary examples do not establish a universal psychological law. |
| ST 14–22 | Discrimination, practice, comparison, perspective, and understanding improve response. | Detecting more features does not mechanically fix their value. |
| ST 23–27 | Qualified critics’ joint verdict supplies a standard. | Identifying qualification must avoid assuming the desired verdict. |
| ST 28–36 | Some differences remain innocent; some moral demands resist accommodation. | The boundary between prejudice and justified refusal needs argument. |

Hume’s use of experience should not be confused with contemporary empirical validation. He cites enduring admiration for Homer and other writers as evidence that merit survives changes in fashion. He does not provide representative samples, independently measured qualifications, or controlled tests. [ST 10–12, 26–27.] His observations generate a philosophical problem; they do not settle all the empirical premises of his answer.

### Can the judge be identified without a circle?

A circular test would say: good works are whatever good judges approve, and good judges are whoever approve the good works. Such a test would never allow an inherited list to be challenged.

Hume’s qualifications offer a possible way out. We can ask whether a critic has read the work carefully, can recognize its structure, knows relevant comparisons, understands its setting, and can separate judgment from a personal grudge. These inquiries can produce evidence beyond agreement with a favorite verdict.

But the escape is incomplete. What counts as a relevant comparison? How do we identify delicate appreciation without already judging the response? A new assessor may need an assessor in turn. Theodore Gracyk’s [Stanford Encyclopedia discussion, section 4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-aesthetics/), maps both the circularity objection and the problem of a regress of assessments. It also records disagreement over whether Hume’s qualified judges are actual fallible critics or an idealized standard.

Our evaluation retains the qualifications as publicly challengeable reasons for trust, rather than certificates of infallibility. A critic earns confidence by making attention and reasoning available for correction. That is an editorial application, not a claim that Hume solved the identification problem this way.

### Survival is evidence with a selection problem

Hume trusts admiration that survives time and crosses borders. Distance can weaken personal rivalry and temporary fashion. His proposed test has an intelligible rationale. [ST 11.]

Yet survival depends on which works are copied, translated, taught, funded, and made available. Imagine two equally accomplished poems, one preserved in schools and one lost when its only copy is destroyed. Posterity cannot compare what it never receives. This constructed example shows a logical limitation; it is not a claim about a particular lost manuscript.

Even cross-border admiration may travel along institutions that teach a shared ranking. Hume’s inference from durable fame to merit therefore needs evidence about access and independent reception. Fame can give a work a reason to be investigated without making exclusion of other works reasonable.

This objection need not imply that all surviving reputations are fraudulent. It limits what survival alone can prove. To evaluate a work, return to what the work makes available to attention.

### Hume must face his own test

The essay condemns provincial judgment, yet ST 4 offers a hostile dismissal of the Qur’an and its author. ST 20 uses a culturally hierarchical contrast to illustrate supposedly untrained appreciation. ST 35 generalizes about Catholicism in a way that makes religious hostility part of the argument itself.

These passages are Hume’s judgments, not neutral descriptions endorsed here. They matter philosophically because an author who demands freedom from prejudice must allow that demand to be applied to his own categories and examples. We cannot simply omit them and present the remaining procedure as if it had already escaped exclusion.

At the same time, his refusal to suspend moral judgment raises a substantial issue. Should a work’s invitation to enjoy cruelty diminish its achievement? A reader might distinguish technical success from ethical failure; Hume permits moral disapproval to diminish literary merit. Another reader may argue that a morally troubling work can expose a disturbing attraction without endorsing it. The decision requires close reading of how the work organizes approval, not a rule that unpleasant characters automatically make bad art.

Our proposed test asks: who is invited to admire whom, for doing what, and with which consequences concealed or disclosed? That question does not erase disagreement. It makes the disagreement answerable to more than a declaration of disgust.

### Why the essay remains a foundational problem

Hume makes the relation between felt response and justified criticism difficult to evade. A standard external to all experience would seem to miss what beauty means for him; pure equality of verdicts would make cultivated attention irrelevant. The essay gives both positions a serious opponent.

Gracyk places the essay within Hume’s broader thinking about human nature, emotion, and moral judgment, and records Kant’s reading of it in translation. That is a specific contextual claim about reception, not proof that later aesthetics merely repeats Hume or that influence validates his conclusions. The encyclopedia also cautions that this one essay cannot represent all of Hume’s aesthetics. This lesson has that deliberately limited scope.

### Comparisons within the catalogue

These comparisons concern related questions, not documented lines of borrowing.

- [Okakura on cultivated appreciation](/seminal-ideas/the-book-of-tea/#appreciation-as-a-cultivated-encounter) asks what a spectator brings to an encounter and challenges admiration performed for prestige. Hume supplies a more explicit account of qualifications, while both accounts must explain why cultivation should authorize a judgment.
- [Woolf on experiment and exclusions](/seminal-ideas/modern-fiction/#experiment-and-its-exclusions) tests a method by what it reveals and leaves out. Read her beside ST 9: an unexpected achievement can challenge a rule instead of proving the achievement defective.
- [James on partial knowledge and restraint](/seminal-ideas/on-a-certain-blindness-in-human-beings/#partial-knowledge-and-the-duty-of-restraint) asks an observer to respect unfamiliar goods. Hume asks how wider experience might improve judgment of those goods. James’s emphasis on limits presses against a critic too confident of complete freedom from prejudice.

### Common distortions

- **“Hume says every critical verdict is equally good.”** He distinguishes the reality of sentiment from authority and spends the essay examining unequal qualification.
- **“Experts can tell you what you actually enjoy.”** A ranking does not negate a real response or eliminate innocent preferences.
- **“The standard is a majority vote.”** His qualified critics are rare; popularity alone does not supply the qualifications.
- **“Art follows rules deduced in advance.”** ST 9 grounds rules in experience and allows successful effects to challenge particular rules.
- **“A sensitive critic notices only faults.”** Delicacy concerns noticing beauties as well as defects, including small contributions to a whole.
- **“Removing prejudice means ignoring context.”** Hume requires attention to the audience and situation a work presupposes.
- **“Historical context excuses every moral invitation.”** His closing paragraphs explicitly limit accommodation, though his own boundaries need criticism.
- **“Long fame scientifically proves universal beauty.”** His appeal to durable admiration is an argument with untested assumptions and problems of selection.

### Test your understanding

1. How can a feeling be real while its associated recommendation remains poorly grounded?
2. What does the wine story illustrate, and where does its analogy to art become uncertain?
3. Why does rereading matter beyond accumulating hours of experience?
4. How could a successful work count against a critical rule?
5. Why might understanding a satire require context rather than freedom from context?
6. What makes the identification of a true judge potentially circular?
7. Give a disagreement Hume could regard as innocent. How does it differ from condemning an entire unfamiliar genre?
8. Why can durable reputation give a reason to read a work without settling its value?

### If you read only a little

The [complete numbered essay](https://davidhume.org/texts/empl1/st) is short enough to read in full. For a focused return:

1. **ST 7–9:** Put the strongest case for equal sentiments beside the argument for unequal authority. Keep the concession about rules and unexpected pleasure.
2. **ST 15–16:** Follow the key-and-leather story into its artistic application. Mark where detection becomes evaluation.
3. **ST 18–23:** List what each qualification adds. Describe a possible failure that a different qualification would not repair.
4. **ST 24–25, then 28–30:** Ask how a judge is identified and which differences remain beyond correction.
5. **ST 31–36, returning to 4 and 20:** Distinguish historical understanding, moral refusal, and the author’s own prejudices. Decide where his practice falls short of his demand.

Then revisit one work you dismissed quickly. Record one feature you had missed, one comparison that sharpens your judgment, and one preference that remains your own. You may finish with the same dislike. The gain is knowing more precisely what the dislike answers to.
