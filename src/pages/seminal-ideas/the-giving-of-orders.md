## Orientation

A supervisor tells an experienced worker to speed up a task. The worker explains that the proposed method will damage the material. The supervisor hears resistance. The worker hears an instruction to abandon the knowledge that makes the work reliable.

This invented case introduces Mary Parker Follett’s question: what should make an instruction authoritative? Rank alone cannot establish that the instruction fits the task. Yet avoiding every difficult instruction leaves coordination undone. *The Giving of Orders* looks for an alternative to both bullying and managerial evasion.

Follett proposes that people study their shared situation and discover what it requires. The worker’s knowledge can then correct the supervisor’s proposal. Coordination becomes an activity to which both contribute, rather than a test of whose will prevails.

Her argument grows out of a wider career in community organization, democratic thought, and management. It is an influential proposal for organizing human relationships, not a modern experiment demonstrating that one management technique always works. [Historical context.](#sources)

**The lasting question:** What would allow the person receiving an instruction to help determine whether it is a good instruction?

**The essential difficulty:** A situation contains facts, but it also contains contested purposes, unequal power, and different costs. Calling an order necessary cannot by itself establish whose judgment or interests it embodies.

## Core lesson

### The argument in six moves

This is an editorial reconstruction of Follett’s reasoning:

1. Issuing an instruction does not supply the habits, training, or working arrangements needed to carry it out.
2. An order also changes a relationship. Humiliation, distrust, or unexamined deference can obstruct intelligent cooperation.
3. The alternative to arbitrary command is not abandoning the supervisory task. The people concerned should investigate the work and its conditions together.
4. An instruction should express the requirements discovered through that investigation. Relevant knowledge can come from the worker as well as the manager.
5. Carrying out the instruction changes the situation, which should in turn change the instruction. Communication therefore needs a returning influence, not just transmission downward.
6. Responsibility becomes more coherent when people participate in forming a decision before being required to execute it, and when methods are tested against their consequences.

Follett’s argument does not dissolve every conflict or abolish authority. Near the end, she expressly says participation belongs before an order is given and that the subordinate must obey afterward. That qualification must remain beside her more expansive account of shared inquiry. [PDF pp. 2–4, 7–13, 16–17.](#sources)

### Training before commands

A **habit pattern** is a learned disposition to perceive and act in a familiar way. Follett borrows psychological language to explain why intellectual agreement alone may not change conduct. Someone can understand a proposed method without yet having the practiced ability or organizational permission to use it. [PDF pp. 2–3.](#sources)

She distinguishes building an attitude, providing an opportunity for its expression, and reinforcing the resulting response. In her example, salespeople or bank clerks need to see a new method’s value, work under rules that permit it, and have opportunities and examples through which it becomes established.

**Editorial example:** A team asks staff to report errors promptly but keeps a performance measure that penalizes anyone associated with an error. Announcing the new expectation leaves the old incentive intact. Training, reporting procedures, and the response to an actual report must be examined together.

Follett’s claim that orders cannot replace training is stronger than advice to explain things politely. The manager must ask what makes the requested action possible. A repeated failure may reveal a missing skill, an incompatible rule, or an unrealistic expectation rather than refusal.

That question does not establish that every failure is involuntary. Follett sometimes makes broad claims about what lifelong habits prevent people from doing. The essay’s observations do not measure those limits or eliminate deliberate misconduct. The defensible practical lesson is to investigate ability and conditions before treating a failure as disobedience.

There is also an ethical problem. Shaping people’s attitudes can become manipulation. Follett later revises the emphasis: the aim should be an attitude suitable for cooperative study and decision, rather than engineering the exact attitude the manager already wants. That later passage changes how the opening psychology should be read. [PDF pp. 9–10.](#sources)

### The law of the situation

The **law of the situation** means the requirements that a shared investigation discovers in the actual work and its conditions. “Law” here is neither a statute nor a manager’s private wish renamed as objective necessity. Follett wants an instruction’s warrant to come from the task rather than from the speaker’s superiority. [PDF pp. 7–9.](#sources)

Her example begins with managers of equal standing: sales and production cannot simply command one another. They must examine the circumstances they share. She asks why this relation should not also govern the interaction between a supervisor and a worker.

She calls the change **depersonalizing orders**: removing arbitrary personal domination from their justification. She immediately adds that the deeper meaning is repersonalizing. People and their relationships belong inside the whole situation; they are not inconvenient feelings to be stripped away. [PDF pp. 8–9.](#sources)

**Editorial example:** A delivery team must decide how to pack a fragile object. A manager proposes a faster arrangement. A packer shows where vibration would damage it. Together they examine the object, packaging, available time, and handling requirements, then revise the plan. The reason for revision is available for both to inspect.

The example preserves a consequential feature of Follett’s account: a worker can point out the requirement to an employer. She illustrates this through a cook or stenographer correcting her own instruction. Recognition of relevant knowledge need not follow the organizational chart. [PDF p. 8.](#sources)

Still, a situation does not announce every decision. Fragility may constrain packing; it does not settle who should bear the cost of a missed deadline. Our evaluation must distinguish discovering a factual constraint from choosing between purposes. Follett’s proposal gives people a method for examining those questions together, but does not guarantee that one uncontested answer will emerge.

### Integration and joint inquiry

**Integration**, as used in this lesson, means working out how the concerns and contributions involved can form a workable whole, rather than suppressing one side or simply adding separate wishes together. **Joint inquiry** is the cooperative study through which people try to find that arrangement. The essay repeatedly draws on Follett’s wider account of integration; it is not a complete theory of conflict resolution in isolation. [PDF pp. 4–6, 9–13.](#sources)

Follett contrasts handling a disagreement with merely silencing it. Even an individual can have competing concerns: wanting a purchase and fearing its disadvantages, for example. A salesperson who suppresses the objections may secure an immediate agreement while leaving a dissatisfied buyer. Her better salesperson addresses the competing reasons rather than forcing one out of view.

**Editorial example:** A supervisor wants a review completed quickly; the reviewer wants enough time to check the most consequential risks. Joint inquiry might reveal that routine checks can be automated, leaving the reviewer more time for the uncertain cases. That would reorganize the task rather than demand that speed or care simply surrender.

The word “might” matters. Perhaps no adequate shortcut exists. Then there is a real decision about delay or risk, and somebody will bear a cost. Calling the outcome integration does not establish that everyone’s concern has been met. The proposal must show how the concerns are actually accommodated.

Follett does not promise a frictionless workplace. She expects differences in perception and interpretation and wants to put that disagreement to work. Nor is her argument a proposal for independent workers’ control: she explicitly distinguishes her joint study from that movement. The scope of participation she endorses should not be upgraded into a political program she rejects or leaves undeveloped. [PDF pp. 8–11.](#sources)

### Circular response and an evolving situation

**Circular response** means that what one person does affects the other person and the circumstances, whose response then affects the first person’s next action. It contrasts with a **linear** picture in which a command travels one way to a passive recipient. Follett’s question is how organization can make the returning influence useful rather than pretend it does not exist. [PDF pp. 4, 13.](#sources)

**Editorial example:** A scheduler asks a workshop to increase output. Workers try the arrangement and discover a queue at inspection. That queue changes what the original target means: increasing unfinished work will not increase deliveries. Their report leads the scheduler to alter the sequence and provide inspection capacity.

In a linear account, the original instruction remains the standard, and the queue looks like an obstacle to obedience. In a circular account, the result becomes information capable of correcting the instruction. The instruction is part of the process producing the new circumstances.

An **evolving situation** is therefore more than an external environment that happens to change. The act of carrying out a decision helps change the conditions under which the decision must continue. Follett asks how orders can remain responsive to that development. [PDF p. 13.](#sources)

This need not mean changing instructions continuously or disregarding a stable procedure. An editorial implementation would specify what feedback matters, who can act on it, and which conditions require review. Otherwise a demand for flexibility can create confusion instead of learning. The essay identifies the problem more fully than it specifies those arrangements.

### Responsibility without servility

**Responsibility** here means contributing judgment and accepting a defined part in the work, rather than treating obedience as a way to avoid thinking. **Servility** means being subordinated to another person’s arbitrary will. Follett asks how coordinated work can require responsibility while dispensing with that kind of subordination. [PDF pp. 10–13.](#sources)

The craft worker who objects to an instruction may be defending the standards of the work. The supervisor who is afraid of causing offense may instead neglect the duty to investigate and coordinate it. Follett’s alternative gives neither person an automatic victory. It asks what each contributes to understanding the problem.

Her conception of a **functional unity** is a whole formed through related contributions: people have reasons to follow a direction because they participate in the activity it coordinates. She describes an order as sometimes functioning like a signal expressing an arrangement already accepted, as when a referee starts a game. [PDF pp. 12–13.](#sources)

But her closing insistence on obedience after participation creates a hard boundary. What happens when execution reveals danger, an assumption fails, or meaningful participation never occurred? The evolving-situation argument suggests a need for renewed judgment. The obedience passage gives no complete procedure for deciding when that judgment can interrupt the order.

**Our editorial takeaway:** assign responsibility together with access to information, a chance to shape the plan, and a usable route for reporting changed conditions. Those provisions help make the proposal concrete; they are not a claim that Follett supplies a finished governance system.

### Who defines the situation?

The strongest objection is that management may preserve its power while giving its preference a more respectable name. “The situation requires this” can be harder to contest than “I want this,” especially if only one party controls the accounts, deadlines, or definition of success.

**Editorial case:** Management says the situation requires reducing rest breaks to meet a production target. Workers say the target ignores fatigue and the quality of the work. Before testing how to meet the target, the inquiry must allow the target itself and the distribution of its costs to be questioned. Otherwise participation concerns only how to execute a decision already insulated from challenge.

Follett offers resources for answering the objection. Workers have knowledge; persons belong inside the whole situation; disagreement is expected; and joint inquiry should precede the decision. Those commitments resist a purely managerial definition.

They do not eliminate differences in bargaining power or establish protections against retaliation. A worker who cannot safely contradict a supervisor does not become an equal investigator merely by being invited to a meeting. Independent representation, rights of appeal, access to information, and protected refusal may be necessary in a particular institution. These are editorial possibilities, not reforms detailed in this essay.

The test of the phrase “law of the situation” is whether the people most affected can change what counts as the situation. Does their testimony alter the problem being solved, or is it treated only as resistance to the preferred solution?

### Dignity as a means and as a claim

Follett links respectful treatment, self-respect, responsibility, and effective work. She criticizes abusive orders and recognizes the worker’s pride in craft. Yet some of her language treats thinking about one’s rights as a damaging reaction because it may obstruct the enterprise. [PDF pp. 6–7, 11–12.](#sources)

Two claims need separation:

1. Humiliation may damage cooperation and performance.
2. A person has a claim to decent treatment even when humiliation would increase output.

The first is a causal proposal requiring evidence in a specified setting. The second is an ethical judgment about what productivity cannot justify. Our evaluation endorses the second independently of the first. Follett’s organizational argument makes the first prominent without fully settling the second.

Similarly, apparent stubbornness may express a badly organized habit, a mistaken belief, a reasonable safety objection, or resistance to an unfair burden. Listening seriously does not require treating all objections as correct. It requires not diagnosing the objection as defective personality before investigating its grounds.

### Does integration conceal a conflict that cannot be removed?

Follett’s search for a better arrangement is valuable precisely because a first demand may not express the underlying need. The deadline and review example shows how reorganizing work could address two concerns at once.

But some conflicts concern the distribution of an unavoidable loss. Two departments may need the same scarce resource at the same time. A firm and its workers may disagree about pay even after sharing every relevant fact. Technical ingenuity may enlarge the available choices without making the remaining choice costless.

A rigorous application therefore needs a failure condition: what would show that the proposed integration has not met a concern? If a worker’s need disappears only because the worker is no longer allowed to state it, the disagreement has been suppressed. Calling it a whole does not repair it.

The essay’s confidence in integration should be evaluated at that point. Its strongest contribution is to require a search for a better organization of the problem. A universal guarantee that all interests can be reconciled would require a much larger argument.

### Supervision, mistakes, and the unanswered questions

Follett devotes the later pages to workers’ resentment of being watched. She believes supervision is necessary but does not offer a finished answer to how it can avoid humiliating surveillance. She also rejects electing supervisors as the solution. Those qualifications prevent reading the essay as a straightforward endorsement of worker self-management. [PDF pp. 14–15.](#sources)

Her treatment of correction is more definite. Criticism should accomplish something educational rather than merely express blame. When an order has been mistaken, the person responsible should help rectify it. She distinguishes acknowledging an error from doing something about it. [PDF p. 15.](#sources)

**Editorial example:** A manager revises an unrealistic target and updates the dependent schedule, staffing decision, and message to other teams. Saying “my mistake” while leaving the consequences with the worker would perform acknowledgment without repair.

A further limit concerns scale. Follett asks how an organization with many widely scattered employees should convey its directions. Her preference for direct joint study cannot simply be copied into every large system. Her own question acknowledges missing implementation work. Contemporary communication technology does not answer it automatically: a shared channel can transmit commands efficiently while still making correction difficult.

### Experimental management is a proposal to test

The opening and closing passages ask practitioners to identify their principles, choose provisional methods, examine results, and pool what they learn. Follett also distinguishes a principle from a method for carrying it out. A dispute can become confused when one speaker argues about whether participation matters and another only about when a committee should meet. [PDF pp. 1, 16–17.](#sources)

An editorial evaluation of a joint-inquiry procedure might examine who spoke, which recommendations changed, whether errors were detected, what time the process required, and whether burdens moved onto less powerful participants. A comparison must also consider staffing, task difficulty, and whether people could speak freely.

Those proposed measures are not findings from the essay. Its anecdotes, psychological vocabulary, and assertions about strikes do not establish the size or universality of the effects she anticipates. The name “scientific management” gives a historical setting and an aspiration to disciplined inquiry; it does not certify every claim made under that name.

### Importance without invented influence

The University of Exeter’s Rowntree project situates Follett among interwar management thinkers and documents her participation in the British business lectures. VCU Libraries traces her movement from community work and democratic thought toward management audiences. These histories give a reason to study the essay as part of a consequential body of organizational thought. [Contextual sources.](#sources)

The intellectual contribution, in our assessment, is to make giving orders a problem of knowledge, relationships, and changing activity. That is more demanding than adding courtesy to an unchanged command. The account anticipates questions a present reader can recognize without establishing that every later participatory technique descends directly from this paper.

The lesson does not claim that Follett invented all cooperative management, that her principles have been universally adopted, or that later prominence proves them true. Connections to present practices are comparisons unless a documented transmission is supplied.

### Comparisons within the catalogue

These are editorial comparisons, not claims of direct influence.

- [Nightingale on the management of conditions](/seminal-ideas/notes-on-nursing/#care-as-the-management-of-conditions) asks whether the intended assistance can actually reach its recipient. Follett similarly refuses to make an individual instruction substitute for the arrangements that enable action. Their purposes and institutional settings differ.
- [Wollstonecraft on educated dependence](/seminal-ideas/a-vindication-of-the-rights-of-woman/#educated-dependence) questions demanding judgment from people trained for obedience. Follett brings the relation between obedience and responsibility into the workplace, while preserving a stronger role for post-decision compliance than a simple liberation narrative would suggest.
- [Peirce on public correction](/seminal-ideas/the-fixation-of-belief/#public-correction-and-independent-reality) asks what can correct preferred beliefs. Follett’s situational inquiry needs a similar openness to unwelcome evidence. Deciding organizational purposes, however, also involves values that a factual observation cannot settle alone.

[Addams’s A Modern Lear on benevolence and social ethics](/seminal-ideas/a-modern-lear/#benevolence-and-social-ethics) questions whether benefits from an employer can replace workers’ participation in determining their needs. Read it beside Follett’s joint inquiry: which decisions may the affected people actually change, and what happens when their interests cannot be integrated? This is an editorial comparison, not a documented claim of influence.

### Common distortions

- **“Nobody should give instructions.”** Follett opposes both arbitrary bossing and evasion of supervisory duties.
- **“Depersonalizing means ignoring feelings.”** She places people and their relationships within the whole situation and suggests repersonalizing as the deeper description.
- **“The situation has one obvious answer.”** She expects differences in interpretation; contested purposes add a further difficulty.
- **“Integration guarantees everyone gets everything.”** A proposed arrangement must show which concerns it meets and which losses remain.
- **“Participation means consultation after the decision.”** She explicitly locates it before the order is issued.
- **“The essay abolishes hierarchy or endorses elected supervisors.”** It retains authority and expressly rejects that proposed solution to supervision.
- **“Circular response means adding a feedback form.”** A returning response matters only if it can affect subsequent judgment and action.
- **“Psychological terminology proves the recommendations work.”** The text offers arguments and examples, not modern experimental validation.

### Test your understanding

1. Why can someone agree with an instruction and still be unable to carry it out?
2. How should a worker’s relevant knowledge affect the authority of a supervisor’s proposal?
3. What distinguishes joint inquiry from manipulating someone into wanting a predetermined result?
4. How can executing an order change the situation to which the order must answer?
5. Why does identifying a factual constraint fail to settle every conflict over organizational purposes?
6. Which passage complicates the claim that Follett endorses unrestricted continuing consent?
7. How could a proposed integration fail even when everyone publicly agrees?

### If you read only a little

Use the [17-page teaching PDF](https://180360720.no/_resources/mary_parker_follett_the_giving_of_orders.pdf), with its own numbered pages. They do not match the pagination of the books from which the essay has been reprinted.

1. **PDF pp. 1–3:** Read the proposed attitude toward experience and the discussion of habit formation. Separate understanding a demand from being equipped to meet it.
2. **PDF pp. 7–10:** Begin with the contrast between bossing and failing to give directions. Follow situational authority through the revision of attitude-shaping into cooperative inquiry.
3. **PDF pp. 11–13:** Read craft knowledge, responsibility, obedience, and the evolving situation together. Ask what would justify reopening a decision.
4. **PDF pp. 14–16:** Keep the unresolved problem of supervision beside her definite statement about participation before orders and obedience afterward.
5. **PDF pp. 16–17:** Separate principles from methods and identify what her experimental proposal would need to measure.

For a second sitting, read pp. 4–6 on circular response and conflicting concerns. Examine the social-work and sales examples as illustrations in a historical argument, not controlled psychological findings.

### Where to go next

Read [Nightingale on continuity](/seminal-ideas/notes-on-nursing/#petty-management-and-continuity) and ask how a jointly designed arrangement survives the absence of a particular person. Then compare [Peirce’s four methods of fixing belief](/seminal-ideas/the-fixation-of-belief/#four-methods-of-fixing-belief): which method is operating when an institution declares that its preferred target is simply what reality requires?

For a practical editorial exercise, choose one recurring instruction. Before changing it, ask the people who carry it out which assumptions it makes, what they must do to make it work, and which conditions should trigger review. Record one change their answers produce. If no answer could alter the plan, reconsider whether the process is an inquiry.
