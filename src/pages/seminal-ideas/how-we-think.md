## Orientation

A student has followed every instruction in a laboratory exercise and reached the expected answer. Ask what the result rules out, and the student cannot say. A colleague has an elegant explanation for a failure but cannot name an observation that would challenge it. Both have done something recognizable as intellectual work. What would turn it into inquiry?

John Dewey’s answer connects thinking to the way a difficulty is investigated. Reflection begins when something unsettles our understanding or obstructs a purpose. We formulate what needs explaining, entertain possibilities, work out what they imply, and return to observations that can support or revise them. Neither collecting facts nor producing a clever idea completes the process.

*How We Think* helped articulate reflective thinking as an educational aim. Its historical role in critical-thinking education is discussed in the [Stanford Encyclopedia’s historical account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/critical-thinking/history.html). That importance does not establish that a five-phase worksheet improves learning, or that every kind of valuable thought has the form described here.

**The lasting question:** What would let a promising idea answer to the situation it claims to explain?

**The essential tension:** Inquiry needs structure, yet applying a fixed procedure without judgment can become another way of avoiding thought.

## Core lesson

### Reflective thought and grounds for belief

**Reflective thought** is deliberate examination of a belief through its supporting reasons and its consequences. Dewey narrows a broad everyday word. An image passing through the mind, an imaginative story, and a conviction acquired from others may all be called thoughts. Reflection asks what warrants accepting something as true. His distinction concerns the purpose and organization of the activity; it does not establish that imagination is worthless. [I, §1, pp. 1–6.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_a.html)

A **ground** is something offered as a reason to believe. An **inference** is the move from what is observed or accepted to something beyond it. Seeing that a pavement is wet and concluding that rain fell involves inference. The conclusion might be correct, but the wetness could also have another cause. Merely having one thought follow another does not make the second well supported.

Dewey’s pedestrian feels a change in temperature, considers possible rain, and looks at the sky. The important relation is between a perceived condition, a suggested explanation, and a further observation. A cloud imagined as an animal can prompt an entertaining association without being evidence that the animal is present. Evidence involves an asserted connection that can be examined. [I, §2, pp. 6–9.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_a.html)

**Editorial example:** A teacher notices that several students missed the same question and thinks the class did not study. That explanation may occur immediately. Reflection begins when the teacher asks whether it is supported: was the wording ambiguous, was the topic taught, and did otherwise well-prepared students make the same mistake? A plausible association becomes a question about grounds.

This distinction changes what it means to ask someone to think harder. More time repeating the same explanation may add no evidence. The useful demand specifies what remains uncertain and what could bear on it.

### Defining the difficulty

A **problem**, here, is a particular obstruction or uncertainty calling for resolution. It need not be a formal exercise. Dewey’s traveler at a branching road needs a way forward; that purpose directs attention toward signs and remembered routes. A different purpose, such as finding the most attractive walk, would make different features relevant. [I, §§3–4, pp. 9–13.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_a.html)

Chapter VI distinguishes feeling that something is wrong from locating what is wrong. These can happen together, but an initial uneasiness often needs investigation before a workable question emerges. If a proposed answer takes over too early, it can determine what the investigator notices and conceal the actual difficulty. [VI, pp. 72–75.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html)

**Editorial example:** An organization asks how to make employees answer messages faster. That formulation already selects response speed as the problem. Initial observation might reveal that urgent messages are buried among routine notifications, that responsibility is unclear, or that interruptions prevent work from being finished. Those findings support different questions and different interventions. Reflection includes inspecting the question before optimizing its assumed answer.

The distinction is practical, not an instruction to achieve a perfectly neutral description before having ideas. Suggestions help identify what deserves attention, and observation can change the question. Dewey’s analysis allows phases to overlap. A useful provisional formulation might be: urgent requests reach the right person inconsistently. It identifies a difficulty while remaining open to correction.

### Suggestions, reasoning, and testable consequences

A **suggestion** is a possible interpretation or solution. Entertained provisionally, it functions as a **hypothesis**: an idea used to guide investigation without yet being accepted as the conclusion. **Reasoning** develops what follows from that idea. Suggestion supplies a candidate; reasoning makes its implications explicit. [VI, pp. 75–76.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html)

Dewey describes five distinguishable phases: encountering a difficulty, defining it, suggesting a solution, reasoning through its implications, and obtaining further observations or experiments that support acceptance or rejection. This is an analysis of an inquiry’s work, not five boxes whose completion proves an answer true. Chapter VI ends by rejecting inflexible rules for how far each phase must be carried. [VI, pp. 72, 77–78.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html)

His ferryboat example makes the relation visible. A projecting pole resembles a flagpole, but its position and missing equipment create difficulties for that interpretation. Alternative purposes are considered. The eventual steering explanation connects the pole’s position with what the pilot needs to see. Dewey presents the account as one of several descriptions drawn almost verbatim from students’ class papers. It illustrates a reasoning pattern; it is not a controlled psychological study. [VI, pp. 69–70 and note 1.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html)

**Editorial example:** A shared printer fails intermittently. One hypothesis concerns the network; another concerns a particular document. If the network is responsible, failures should not depend solely on that document. If the document is responsible, sending it through another available connection may preserve the failure while other documents print. Thinking through these differences helps choose a test. Listing possible causes without developing their consequences leaves the inquiry unfinished.

An implication remains conditional: *if* the explanation and relevant background assumptions hold, a certain result should follow. Finding that result need not uniquely establish the explanation. A rival may predict it too. Dewey’s discussion of corroboration explicitly includes considering whether competing conditions could yield the same result. [VI, p. 77.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html)

### The double movement of inquiry

Dewey calls the movement from fragmentary observations toward an organizing explanation **inductive**, and the movement from the explanation toward its particular implications **deductive**. His use of induction is broader than accumulating many examples and announcing a rule. Interpreting a single complicated situation can already involve the movement toward a general meaning. These terms describe connected work within inquiry. [VII, §1, pp. 79–84; §2, pp. 88–90.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_g.html)

Observation does not finish before reasoning starts. A hypothesis directs the next observation; the finding may then change the hypothesis. Nor can one conjure good suggestions by willpower alone. What occurs to an investigator depends partly on knowledge and experience. Dewey therefore emphasizes indirect control: inspect the material differently, obtain better records, compare contrasting cases, or arrange conditions that make a hidden difference visible. [VII, §2, pp. 84–90.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_g.html)

**Editorial example:** Twenty successful printer tests using the same short document may tell us less about the suspected document problem than one carefully chosen comparison with the failing file. The number of observations matters alongside their relation to the question. Repetition under a narrow set of conditions leaves other conditions unexamined.

Dewey stresses contrast as well as likeness. Different samples can matter because they come from different parts of what is being assessed. Deliberate variation can expose a relation that waiting for another ordinary occurrence would leave obscure. His account is a methodological argument; it supplies no universal sample size, statistical threshold, or guarantee of causal identification. [VII, §§2–3, pp. 88–93.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_g.html)

### Learning through inquiry and proportionate judgment

The educational conclusion follows from the account of inquiry. If learners merely handle materials, they may perform operations without understanding why. If teachers invite guesses but supply all the reasoning, students practice suggesting answers while someone else performs the intellectual development. If general principles are memorized but never used in new situations, their meaning remains incomplete. [VII, §5, pp. 96–100.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_g.html)

**Editorial teaching example:** Students compare two paper bridges. Asking only which held more weight can end in a result without an explanation. Asking what differed, what each proposed explanation predicts, and what a redesigned comparison would reveal gives the measurements an intellectual purpose. The teacher still supplies knowledge, constrains an unsafe or irrelevant test, and helps students notice what their first comparison confounded. This is guided inquiry, not abandonment of instruction.

There is also a limit to prolonging reflection. Chapter VI says the trained mind must judge the necessary extent of inquiry in the particular case. A small reversible choice and a consequential long-term commitment warrant different effort. Sometimes delay defeats the purpose of acting; sometimes the honest conclusion remains unsettled. Dewey gives a demand for judgment rather than a numerical stopping rule. [VI, p. 78.](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html)

**Editorial takeaway:** Before accepting an answer, specify the difficulty, a live alternative, and a further finding that would matter. Then ask whether the cost and feasibility of that check suit the decision. This short practice is our adaptation of the account. It is neither a validated intervention nor a substitute for the subject knowledge that makes good questions and relevant tests possible.

## Deep exploration

### Reconstructing the argument

The selected chapters support the following editorial reconstruction:

1. Belief reaches beyond what is immediately present, so an inference can mislead even when its conclusion feels natural.
2. Reflection makes the connecting grounds available for examination rather than treating the first suggestion as settled.
3. A specified difficulty directs the search. Without it, observations accumulate and ideas proliferate without a criterion of relevance.
4. Suggestions need development: reasoning reveals consequences, incompatibilities, and possible checks.
5. Observation and experiment can then bear on those consequences and on alternatives. Inquiry moves repeatedly between interpretation and particulars.
6. Education in thinking should cultivate participation in that movement, supported by knowledge, materials, and guidance.

The last move is a normative educational proposal: it concerns what teaching should enable. The description of illustrative inquiries makes that proposal intelligible, but does not by itself demonstrate the effectiveness of any curriculum designed in its name.

### Is the account too centered on solving problems?

Some valuable thought begins in enjoyment, contemplation, play, or imaginative exploration without an initially defined difficulty. A poem need not answer an explanatory question; a mathematician may explore a pattern before knowing what it could establish. If all such activity were dismissed as inferior thinking, the proposed educational aim would be too narrow.

Dewey announces his restricted subject at the beginning. He separates the production of imaginative stories from the establishment of factual belief and recognizes that imaginative activity can prepare the way for reflection. That supports reading his account as specifying one important intellectual activity. It does not oblige us to evaluate every activity by how efficiently it resolves uncertainty.

Yet his language about reflection’s educational value can invite the broader reading. Our evaluation retains the distinction while rejecting a monopoly: learning to appreciate a form, tolerate an unresolved experience, or invent a possible world can matter without becoming an empirical investigation. A later question about the invented world may start an inquiry of its own.

### Does testing an idea establish it?

A prediction that succeeds may be shared by several explanations. A test can also depend on unnoticed background conditions or unreliable instruments. The risk survives a conscientious five-phase account of what the investigator did.

Dewey recognizes the importance of alternatives and makes acceptance revisable when contrary facts appear. He also gives a particularly strong formulation in VII, §4, p. 96: the observed details must agree with the deduced results without exception. These passages should be read together. They express an ideal of careful verification; they do not deliver a complete modern account of uncertainty, measurement error, statistical models, or evidence that supports one hypothesis more than another.

**Editorial counterexample:** Suppose two accounts predict that a printer will work after a restart. The successful restart cannot discriminate between them. The next useful check concerns something on which their predictions diverge. If no feasible observation separates them, the correct report may be that the fault is temporarily resolved while its cause remains uncertain.

The practical value of Dewey’s account is strongest when it makes that limit visible. A completed action and a warranted causal explanation are different achievements.

### Observation contains interpretation

It can sound as though inquiry should begin with perfectly uninterpreted facts. Dewey complicates that view in VII, §2, pp. 86–88. An ordinary report already uses classifications and relationships. His advice is to identify the interpretations most exposed to error in the circumstances, rather than strip all meaning out of observation.

**Editorial example:** A record saying that a student was inattentive folds an interpretation into a report. A more inspectable record might describe which questions the student answered, what task was in progress, and when participation changed. Those descriptions still involve selection and language. Their advantage is that they expose more of the basis for the judgment and leave room for rival explanations.

Dewey emphasizes permanent instrumental records as checks on personal expectation. Our qualification is that records also need interpretation: their selection, calibration, and missing intervals matter. A printed measurement is more inspectable than a fleeting impression in some respects, but its appearance does not certify it. Compare the catalogue’s [Bose lesson on differential recording](/seminal-ideas/response-in-the-living-and-non-living/#differential-recording-and-a-misleading-zero), where a zero can conceal equal changes at two measured points.

### Knowledge and teaching cannot be replaced by a checklist

At the end of I, Dewey makes prior knowledge central to useful suggestion. In VI, he says his most experimental example presupposes some scientific training. In VII, he explains how connected principles help investigators identify significant deviations and work out what else to look for. The account therefore provides little support for teaching generic thinking skills while neglecting what people are thinking about.

A novice may not know which alternative is plausible or which observation is informative. The instruction to test alternatives helps only if learning supplies the concepts, methods, and access required to perform that task. A checklist can prompt a question; it cannot supply all of its content.

This also limits what historical influence proves. The [Stanford historical supplement](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/critical-thinking/history.html) places Dewey in the development of critical-thinking education and describes his extensively rewritten 1933 edition. It is context for the book’s educational importance. We do not infer from that history a measured improvement produced by the three chapters, this lesson, or any specific classroom activity.

### Who gets to define the problem?

Dewey’s selected cases make the investigator’s purpose fairly easy to identify: arrive on time, explain an object, understand an event. Collective situations may contain conflicting purposes. Making employees respond faster, reducing interruptions, and improving customers’ access are not automatically the same goal. The question of whose difficulty counts can precede technical investigation.

**Editorial objection:** If a manager defines the question so narrowly that only cheap answers are admissible, a diligent investigation might optimize an objectionable arrangement. The quality of inference does not settle the justice of the goal. Expanding participation can reveal a different problem, although participation alone does not make every proposed solution sound.

This is an extension and limitation of the selected account, not a claim that Dewey’s wider work ignores democracy. The [Stanford entry on Dewey](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dewey/) situates inquiry within a much broader philosophy. To develop his full account of collective ends would require reading beyond the chapters treated here.

### Historical examples need their own scrutiny

Chapter I, pp. 5–6, tells a Columbus story to contrast inherited acceptance with investigated belief. This guide does not use that story as evidence about what earlier populations knew or what Columbus established. Dewey’s rhetorical contrast cannot authenticate the historical premise of his illustration. Chapter VII also contains period medical and scientific generalizations; their presence in an influential book does not make them present-day scientific guidance.

The same principle applies to the student reports in VI. They reveal the kinds of relations Dewey wants the reader to examine. They do not show how often people reason this way, that every competing explanation was exhausted, or that the described result was independently replicated. The reader can learn from the argument while keeping those evidential limits in view.

### Comparisons within the catalogue

These connections compare arguments; they do not assert direct influence between the particular works.

- [Peirce on genuine doubt](/seminal-ideas/the-fixation-of-belief/#genuine-doubt-and-the-settlement-of-opinion) asks how a settled opinion differs from reliable inquiry. Dewey’s selected chapters develop a teachable account of what investigating an actual difficulty involves. The shared emphasis on inquiry should not erase differences between their projects.
- [Poincaré on verification after conviction](/seminal-ideas/mathematical-creation/#verification-after-conviction) distinguishes an idea’s arrival from establishing it. Dewey likewise gives suggestion a role while requiring its development and checking. Neither account guarantees the production of an original idea.
- [Follett’s law of the situation](/seminal-ideas/the-giving-of-orders/#the-law-of-the-situation) directs attention toward shared investigation in coordinated work. Read it alongside the question of who defines a problem and whose knowledge can change it.
- [Snow’s natural experiment](/seminal-ideas/on-the-mode-of-communication-of-cholera/#natural-experiment) offers a concrete comparison to test against Dewey’s broader methodological account. Investigating can require exploiting existing differences when deliberately arranging conditions is impossible or objectionable.

### Common distortions

- **All mental activity is reflection.** Dewey selects a narrower activity concerned with examining grounds and consequences of belief.
- **The five phases are a compulsory order.** He allows overlap and explicitly makes their extent depend on the case.
- **Suspending judgment means doing nothing.** Inquiry includes observation and deliberate activity; a practical decision can remain distinct from settled explanatory belief.
- **Induction is just counting similar instances.** Dewey includes organizing a single situation and stresses meaningful contrasts.
- **An observed prediction proves the hypothesis uniquely.** Alternative explanations and background conditions still matter.
- **Learning by inquiry eliminates teaching.** The account depends on knowledge and criticizes leaving the reasoning entirely to the teacher; it does not abolish guidance.
- **Useful reasoning certifies the goal.** A well-investigated means can still serve a disputed end.
- **Historical importance supplies empirical validation.** Neither the book’s reputation nor its later educational use proves a particular intervention works.

### Test your understanding

1. What distinguishes remembering an animal when seeing a cloud from treating a cloud as evidence of coming rain?
2. Why might asking how to make employees answer faster prematurely narrow an inquiry?
3. What work remains after someone lists three plausible explanations?
4. Why can twenty identical printer tests leave the relevant uncertainty untouched?
5. How can observation be interpreted without every observation becoming equally doubtful?
6. What does a successful restart establish, and what might it leave unsettled?
7. Why can a novice need instruction before the demand to test a hypothesis becomes useful?
8. What prevents the five phases from becoming a rigid recipe?

### If you read only a little

Use the chapter links below and the printed page markers in parentheses. The original page span appears in each transcription’s bibliographic header.

1. **[I, §§1–2, pp. 1–9](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_a.html):** Separate association, imagination, unexamined belief, and reflection. Read the definition on p. 6 and the pedestrian example together; do not adopt the Columbus illustration as a historical source.
2. **I, §§3–4, pp. 9–13:** Follow how a particular uncertainty directs a search and how previous knowledge makes a suggestion possible. Identify what generic advice to think leaves unspecified.
3. **[VI, pp. 68–78](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_f.html), complete:** Compare the journey, pole, and tumbler examples. Mark the five phases on p. 72, the alternatives on p. 75, the qualification about corroboration on p. 77, and the rejection of inflexible rules on p. 78.
4. **[VII, §§1–3, pp. 79–93](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1910a/Dewey_1910_g.html):** Track the movement between facts and explanation. Pause over interpretation in observation, contrasting samples, and deliberate variation; ask what each device changes about the evidence.
5. **VII, §§4–5, pp. 93–100:** Connect organized knowledge with the consequences it lets us derive. Then inspect the classroom criticisms: which part of inquiry is missing when a learner only handles objects, guesses, or repeats a principle?

For the next explanation you find convincing, identify one feasible observation that could make it less convincing. If nothing comes to mind, first ask whether the explanation is developed enough to tell you where to look.
