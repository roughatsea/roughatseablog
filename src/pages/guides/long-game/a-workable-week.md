A schedule becomes useful when you can see what happens on Tuesday, how long it takes, and what changes when Tuesday goes badly. “Lift, do cardio, and eat enough protein” leaves those decisions unwritten.

Here is a **constructed example**, not a program tested as a complete package. Our fictional reader is a generally healthy adult who can already tolerate a 30-minute moderate walk, has access to basic gym equipment, and can reserve two sessions for strength training. They want to develop muscle while preserving room for other health priorities. Nothing in the example establishes that its precise exercises, days, or progression rule are uniquely best. [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

Someone starting from substantially less activity should not treat the finished schedule as a first-week requirement. Someone managing an injury, a medical condition, pregnancy, or significant functional limitations needs an appropriately adapted starting point.

## Put all the activity on the calendar

| Day | Planned activity |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | 30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. |
| Tuesday | Whole-body strength session, approximately 45–60 minutes including preparation. |
| Wednesday | 30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. |
| Thursday | Whole-body strength session, approximately 45–60 minutes including preparation. |
| Friday | 30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. |
| Saturday | 30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. |
| Sunday | 30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. |

Five 30-minute aerobic sessions add up to **150 minutes**. Two 45–60-minute strength sessions add another **90–120 minutes**. The planned total is therefore **240–270 minutes per week**, before travel, changing clothes, or any unlisted activity. This arithmetic matters: describing the plan as “just two workouts a week” would conceal more than half its time commitment. The schedule is one way to instantiate adult activity guidance, not a superior arrangement established by research. [C01](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C01) [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

Walking is only the fictional reader’s chosen mode. A suitable alternative could be cycling, swimming, or an adapted activity. For ordinary aerobic activity, CDC describes moderate intensity as an effort at which you can talk but not sing. The same speed can represent different effort for different people. This talk test does not measure how challenging a set of weight training is. [C03](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C03)

Thirty continuous minutes are not mandatory; activity can be accumulated in smaller periods. For this example, two 15-minute periods would preserve a day’s planned total. A shorter beginning is also legitimate: WHO’s guidance does not treat activity below a weekly target as worthless. [C01](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C01) [C13](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C13)

## Understand the strength session before copying it

A **repetition** is one complete performance of an exercise. A **set** is a group of repetitions performed before a rest. In “two sets of eight repetitions,” you perform the movement eight times, rest, and then perform it another eight times. The **load** is the resistance used. CDC presents 8–12 repetitions and one or more sets as general strength-training guidance, not as the only range capable of producing results. [C04](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C04)

The table below proposes the same basic session on both strength days. Repetition gives the fictional reader fewer exercises to learn and fewer variables to change. That is a teaching and organizational choice; this exact menu has not been compared with every alternative. Exercise setup and technique should be learned with suitable instruction, particularly when the movements are unfamiliar. [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

| Movement in this example | What the reader actually does | Proposed working sets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leg press | Sit in the machine and move its platform by bending and straightening the legs through a suitable range. | 2 sets of 8–12 repetitions. |
| Seated leg curl | Bend the knees against the machine’s resistance. | 2 sets of 8–12 repetitions. |
| Machine chest press | Push the handles forward and bring them back under control. | 2 sets of 8–12 repetitions. |
| Seated row | Pull the handles toward the torso and return them under control. | 2 sets of 8–12 repetitions. |
| Machine shoulder press | Press the handles upward using a setup and range appropriate for the person. | 2 sets of 8–12 repetitions. |
| Seated calf raise | Lift and lower the heels against resistance. | 2 sets of 8–12 repetitions. |
| Cable or band anti-rotation press | From a stable position, press the hands away from the torso while resisting the band or cable’s sideways pull. | 1 set of 8 controlled repetitions per side as an illustrative starting dose. |

The pushing and pulling exercises involve the arms as well as larger muscle groups. The overall selection is intended to distribute work across the body rather than devote both days to one favored area. This is a conventional application of all-major-muscle-group guidance, not a claim that each listed exercise is necessary or that the menu is a rehabilitation prescription. [C01](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C01) [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

Start with loads that permit controlled repetitions without needing to force an unsuccessful repetition. For this example, the reader stops a set while they believe roughly two further controlled repetitions would still be possible. This estimate is a practical effort cue, not a precision instrument or a proven universal optimum. ACSM’s 2026 summary does not make momentary muscle failure a requirement for the average healthy adult. [C02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C02) [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

Allow roughly two minutes between working sets as a planning assumption, and longer when necessary to perform the next set appropriately. Preparation and lighter practice sets are included in the session’s time allowance but not in the working-set counts. If the session consistently takes longer than the allotted time, revise the schedule rather than pretending the extra time is free. The rest interval and estimated duration are illustrative logistics, not experimentally determined optima. [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

Machines are used here to make one example concrete. They are not a purchasing requirement. Bands, body weight, and free weights can also support resistance training, although substitutions require appropriate setup and skill. “Use whatever equipment exists” is not permission to improvise an unstable anchor or unsafe arrangement. [C02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C02)

## Know what changes at the next session

Here is an illustrative progression rule. Suppose the chest press is performed for eight controlled repetitions in each set. At a later session, with the same load and the same stopping rule, the reader might complete nine. The immediate task is to add a repetition when feasible, not to increase the weight automatically every Tuesday.

Once the reader reaches twelve repetitions in both sets with the intended control and effort, they can try the smallest available load increase and return toward the lower end of the range. If the increment is too large, there is no obligation to force it. Continuing to practice with the previous load is an available choice. This “repetitions first, then load” procedure is an explicit coaching synthesis; the cited sources support resistance training, not the superiority of this exact algorithm. [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

Record only enough to inform that decision: exercise, load, repetitions, and a short note when something unusual affected the session. A notebook line such as “chest press, same load, 10 and 9, controlled” is sufficient for the example. No claim is being made that detailed tracking is necessary for every reader.

This is a **starting plan**, not a promise of maximal hypertrophy. ACSM’s summary points toward higher weekly volume for a muscle-growth emphasis. Adding work should be a later, explicit decision about the reader’s goal and capacity, not an assumption that a beginner must immediately adopt a high-volume routine. [C02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C02)

## Do the aerobic sessions undermine the lifting?

A meta-analysis by Schumann and colleagues combined 43 studies comparing concurrent aerobic and resistance training with resistance training alone. It found no statistically significant average reduction in measured hypertrophy or maximal-strength gains; explosive-strength development was more vulnerable. Explosive strength concerns producing force rapidly, rather than simply the greatest force someone can produce. [C07](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C07)

That is a reason not to assume that aerobic activity must be sacrificed whenever muscle growth matters. It is not proof that any amount of endurance exercise fits any strength program without tradeoffs. The research concerned particular supervised interventions, not every athlete, schedule, or training volume. Our decision to retain both forms of activity also reflects the guide’s health-first objective. [C07](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C07) [R02](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R02)

## A protein target you can actually interpret

For food planning, give the fictional reader a body weight of **80 kilograms**. NIH’s Office of Dietary Supplements reports athlete protein recommendations of **1.2–2.0 grams per kilogram per day**. Applying that range to this example produces 96–160 grams per day. Translation from athlete guidance to a recreational trainee is a judgment, and the range is not a longevity prescription. [C06](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C06)

Choose **1.6 grams per kilogram** solely to demonstrate the calculation:

**80 kilograms × 1.6 grams per kilogram = 128 grams of protein per day.**

The kilograms cancel, leaving grams. Four portions of 32 grams would sum to 128 grams. That partition is arithmetic, not evidence that four eating occasions or exactly 32 grams at each one is best. The complete day’s food counts toward the total; protein does not have to arrive exclusively in a shake or a designated main dish. [R03](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R03)

A meal-planning illustration might use yogurt or a suitable alternative at breakfast, beans and another protein-containing food at lunch, tofu or fish at dinner, and an additional food as needed. Check actual portions and labels rather than borrowing invented protein counts from this example. Pair the protein choices with a varied pattern of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, pulses, and appropriate fats. These foods illustrate the pattern, not a verified complete menu. [C05](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C05) [R03](/guides/long-game/evidence/#R03)

Do not mechanically apply this body-weight calculation to every reader. Kidney disease, substantial obesity, pregnancy, and other clinical contexts can change the assessment; they are outside this example. Nor does the calculation decide calorie intake. A protein target is one input to a diet, not the diet itself.

## Have a smaller version without pretending it is equivalent

For a disrupted week, write down what is realistically available: perhaps one strength session rather than two, or shorter aerobic periods. Lower activity does not reproduce the full plan’s dose, and the guide should not claim identical results. It can still be a legitimate starting or temporary level. [C13](/guides/long-game/evidence/#C13)

The practical purpose of a fallback is to identify an available action instead of presenting only an unavailable ideal. Whether a preplanned fallback reliably improves long-term adherence is a separate, testable question, not something this example proves. That open hypothesis is recorded in [the methods chapter](/guides/long-game/methods/#an-open-hypothesis).

Do not “repay” missed sessions with a compulsory pile of work. Reconsider the coming week and use an appropriate starting workload. Persistent difficulties, unfamiliar symptoms, or exercise-related pain deserve assessment rather than a motivational speech. This is a general educational example, not individualized medical or injury advice.

At the end of the week, the useful review is concrete: did the scheduled activities fit, were the exercise instructions understandable, did the actual time match the estimate, and which decision now needs changing? The plan exists to answer those questions—not to turn an interrupted Thursday into a verdict on the person following it.
