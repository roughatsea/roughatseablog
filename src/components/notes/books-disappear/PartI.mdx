## The success problem

A censorship failure announces itself. There are trials, headlines, seized shipments, illicit editions, famous photographs of bonfires. A censorship success may leave one letter in a publisher's archive and nothing on a bookstore shelf.

That creates a brutal selection effect. The easiest cases to find are the cases in which the work escaped. The closer a suppressor came to complete success, the less searchable evidence remains. A title may survive only in a contract. A vanished manuscript may be mentioned in correspondence without any description of its contents. When the sole copy was destroyed, every later claim about the work must pass through the memories and motives of the people who saw it.

So this investigation treats the **particular version** as the unit of analysis. Ian Hamilton eventually published a biography of J. D. Salinger, but not the biography Random House had set in galleys in 1986. That original book counts as successfully stopped. A redacted memoir may be on sale while the unredacted edition remains destroyed. A novel may exist in Italian while being absent from its author's country for thirty years. “The book was published” is sometimes true only after the most important details have been removed.

The outcome labels used here describe what happened, not whether the intervention was morally justified:

- **Extinguished:** the known manuscript or copies were destroyed, with no complete text known to survive.
- **Entombed:** a substantially complete work survives, but only in an archive, private collection, or legally unusable form.
- **Mutilated:** the intended edition was stopped and only a materially altered or redacted descendant appeared.
- **Delayed:** suppression held for years or generations before publication.
- **Exiled:** publication was blocked in the work's home jurisdiction but succeeded elsewhere.
- **Recalled:** copies were printed, then withdrawn or pulped.
- **Escaped:** the suppression attempt failed to contain the work.

The evidence labels matter just as much. **Confirmed** means a court opinion, government record, archival finding aid, publisher file, surviving proof, or similarly direct source establishes both intervention and outcome. **Strongly supported** means contemporary accounts and later research converge, though a decisive document remains missing. **Disputed** marks a real conflict in the evidence. **Alleged** is reserved for a claim resting mainly on an interested party's account.

This is not a presumption that every suppressed book was accurate, admirable, or safe to publish. Some contained classified information. Some prompted credible defamation or copyright claims. Some were propaganda. Some may have been bad books. The attempt to stop a work is evidence of **consequence**, not evidence of **truth**.

That caveat does not weaken the project. It makes the cases more revealing. The question is not merely who behaved like a censor. It is who possessed enough power—legal, political, financial, familial, or physical—to decide whether a text could enter the world.
