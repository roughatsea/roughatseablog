### A father keeps his daughter's novel

Mary Shelley completed *Mathilda* soon after *Frankenstein*. She sent the manuscript to her father, the philosopher and novelist William Godwin. He objected to its central subject: a father's incestuous desire for his daughter. He did not send it back.

That small act of custody was enough. Shelley repeatedly asked for the manuscript, but Godwin retained it. She did not oversee its publication or make the editorial decisions an author ordinarily makes while bringing a difficult text into print. The novel appeared only in 1959, 140 years after its composition.

The [Shelley-Godwin Archive](https://shelleygodwinarchive.org/contents/ms_abinger_d33/introduction/) makes the chain unusually visible. It identifies Godwin's condemnation of the subject, his refusal to return the manuscript, the Bodleian volume in which the fair copy survives, and the consequences for later editors working without Shelley's final instructions.

No state intervened. No court issued an order. The suppressor was the person who happened to possess the pages and whose relationship to the author made defiance costly. The case belongs here because it exposes the smallest effective monopoly: one manuscript, one custodian, one daughter unable to retrieve her own book.

### The one-copy book

The Kuwait Oil Company commissioned Ian Fleming to write about Kuwait and the oil industry. The arrangement included an approval condition. Fleming completed the work, photographs were assembled, and a bound copy was made. The required approval did not arrive.

That copy now sits in Indiana University's Lilly Library. Its [archival description](https://archives.iu.edu/html/InU-Li-VAD6379.html) is one of the starkest records in this investigation: 151 leaves, sixteen photographs, and a signed statement by Fleming identifying it as the only bound copy. Fleming called the project “stillborn.” He also recorded that the Kuwait Oil Company owned the copyright and that the text could not be printed or quoted without written approval.

Fleming's signed statement says the oil company approved the manuscript but that it did not receive the sheikhs' approval. Whatever the precise objections, the archive proves the decisive facts. The work was completed. Its commissioning structure contained a veto. The veto held.

This is not the same as a government seizing an independent manuscript. Fleming accepted an approval regime as part of a paid commission, and the rights holder's control was explicit. Yet from the reader's side the result is uncanny: **a finished book exists, and ordinary publication remains forbidden**. It may be consulted under archival rules, but it has never entered the public life for which a book is normally made.

### The book the court stopped in its present form

Ian Hamilton spent three years writing an unauthorized biography of J. D. Salinger. By May 1986, Random House had produced galleys. Salinger saw that the book quoted or closely paraphrased unpublished letters held in university collections. He registered copyrights in seventy-nine letters and sued.

The litigation did not merely delay a release date. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit directed the lower court to issue a preliminary injunction barring publication of the biography in its then-current form. The [opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/811/90/206150/) treated the unpublished status of the letters as central: copyright included the author's right of first publication, even though the physical letters belonged to their recipients or archives.

Hamilton later removed virtually all of Salinger's language and published *In Search of J. D. Salinger*. That descendant does not erase the original outcome. Princeton's [finding aid for Hamilton's papers](https://findingaids.library.upenn.edu/records/PRIN_MUDD_C0714) describes *J. D. Salinger: A Writing Life* as never published because of the judicial decisions. The collection includes drafts, legal papers, and uncorrected proof copies of the New York and London editions.

This is a case where “suppression” should not do the moral work by itself. Salinger had a recognized copyright interest in private, unpublished expression. Hamilton had signed access agreements at some of the libraries. The court did not order biographical facts erased from history; it protected expressive material and required a different book.

But the version-level result is exact. Random House had a book. A court stopped it. A materially altered book took its place. The first one survives as an archival object rather than a publication.
