### A philosopher's system, reduced to the evidence against it

Gemistos Plethon spent the last years of the Byzantine world arguing toward a religious and political order that was not Christian. His *Nómoi*, or *Book of Laws*, drew on Platonic philosophy and a revived pagan theology. It was exactly the sort of work that could become more dangerous after its author's death, when disciples might copy it without his ability to qualify or defend it.

The surviving account has a cruel symmetry. Plethon's manuscript came into the hands of Gennadios Scholarios, the Orthodox patriarch and an intellectual opponent. Scholarios destroyed most of the only copy, but kept selections that showed why, in his judgment, the rest deserved destruction. The censor preserved the prosecution's exhibits and burned the defendant.

The result is not total silence. Fragments were eventually published in 1858, and scholars have reconstructed parts of Plethon's thought from those passages and from other writings. But the full system cannot be checked against the excerpts chosen by its enemy. The [Bryn Mawr Classical Review's account of modern Plethon scholarship](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2013/2013.07.12/) states the essential bibliographic fact plainly: the only copy was partly burned, while selected passages were retained to justify the act.

This case also stretches the modern meaning of publication. Plethon wrote before print made identical copies cheap. “Stopping publication” meant preventing a manuscript from entering the copying networks through which a work acquired readers. One fire could still alter the intellectual record.

### The meeting in the drawing room

On May 17, 1824, less than a month after Byron died, the people closest to his literary estate assembled at publisher John Murray's house in London. They included Murray, poet Thomas Moore, Byron's friend John Cam Hobhouse, and representatives of Lady Byron and Augusta Leigh. They discussed the memoir manuscript. Then they burned it in the drawing-room fireplace.

The event survives unusually well in the archive because it was controversial immediately. The [John Murray Archive at the National Library of Scotland](https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/67498) preserves Hobhouse's account, related letters, and press cuttings under the blunt folder title “Destruction of the Byron Memoirs.” There is no need to infer a vanished conspiracy from a gap in the record. The record describes the destruction.

What vanished is harder to state. The people who had read the memoirs later disagreed about how sexually explicit, indiscreet, or publishable they were. Their testimony was entangled with self-protection: some had participated in the burning; others had reputations or relationships implicated by whatever Byron wrote. No later reader can arbitrate the dispute. The decision to preserve Byron's reputation also preserved the destroyers' control over the story of what they destroyed.

A commercial publisher participated, which keeps this from being a simple tale of piety defeating profit. Murray owned a potentially lucrative manuscript. Moore had expected eventual publication. Family pressure, friendship, propriety, copyright, and fear of scandal converged around one physical object. Once it was ash, those competing claims no longer had to be resolved.

### The dangerous confusion between a book and its predecessor

Richard Francis Burton's 1886 *Perfumed Garden* remains obtainable. That fact makes the loss of *The Scented Garden* easy to misunderstand. They were not interchangeable editions.

Burton spent his final period working on a new translation and extensive scholarly apparatus, intended for posthumous publication. After his death, his wife and literary executor, Isabel Burton, destroyed the manuscript. A contemporary biographical account says the translation and annotations had occupied the last year of his life; the episode later became the subject of a dedicated [study in the *Journal of Victorian Culture*](https://academic.oup.com/jvc/article-abstract/2/2/229/4085715).

Isabel's stated rationale mixed religion, reputation, and an estimate of how readers would use the text. She believed a small minority might approach it as scholarship while the overwhelming majority would consume it as pornography. She rejected a large offer and burned it.

The wider legend—that she then annihilated nearly every important Burton paper—is less secure and has been contested by biographers comparing inventories. The destruction of *The Scented Garden* itself is not seriously in doubt. That distinction is worth preserving. A dramatic act attracts dramatic additions; soon the confirmed burned manuscript becomes a bonfire containing everything anyone wishes had survived.

The earlier *Perfumed Garden* can therefore create false reassurance. A reader can buy a Burton translation bearing almost the same title and conclude that the lost book was recovered. It was not. What survives is the work Burton was revising, not the work he had nearly finished.
